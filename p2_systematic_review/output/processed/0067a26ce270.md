## Abstract summary
The paper proposes a quantum version of the least squares Monte Carlo (LSM) algorithm for stochastic optimal stopping problems, leveraging quantum access to the underlying process, quantum circuits to compute stopping times in superposition, and quantum Monte Carlo techniques. Under mild smoothness assumptions the quantum LSM achieves a near-quadratic runtime speedup over the classical LSM, and the authors analyze applications to American option pricing, including detailed cases for Brownian and geometric Brownian motion models.
## Methodology
This is a theoretical / algorithmic paper that designs and analyzes a quantum version of the Least-Squares Monte Carlo (LSM) method for discrete-time stochastic optimal stopping problems (motivated by American option pricing). The authors: (1) fix a computational model including a fixed-point arithmetic encoding for real numbers and a quantum arithmetic model; (2) specify quantum input oracles required by the algorithm: quantum sampling access to the underlying Markov chain (preparation of amplitude-encoded path superpositions) and quantum query access to payoff functions z_t and basis/expansion functions e_{t,k}; (3) define and use controlled-rotation primitives for amplitude-encoding expectations; (4) adopt Montanaro's quantum Monte Carlo subroutine (QMonteCarlo) as the quantum estimator for expectation values (amplitude-estimation style Monte Carlo with explicit complexity/variance dependence); (5) construct explicit quantum circuits (unitaries W_t, V^{(k)}_t and compositions C^{(k)}_t) that propagate the dynamic-programming recursion for stopping times backwards in time in superposition, enabling estimation of continuation-value related vectors b_t and Gram matrices A_t via quantum Monte Carlo subroutines; (6) combine quantum estimation of matrix and vector entries with classical inversion of the (small) linear systems to obtain regression coefficients α_t, then iterate backwards until t=0 and evaluate the final payoff estimator; (7) perform a full, rigorous error analysis decomposing the overall additive error into (i) Monte Carlo / stochastic estimation error and (ii) approximation (projection) error due to finite-dimensional basis; (8) derive sample / query / time complexity bounds for both the proposed quantum LSM and the classical LSM, highlighting nearly quadratic speedups in the Monte Carlo-dominated regime; (9) analyze special-case models (Brownian motion and geometric Brownian motion) where particular basis choices (Hermite polynomials or monomials) simplify or allow explicit bounds on Gram matrices and their minimum singular values; and (10) relate approximation errors to function smoothness via Jackson-like inequalities (polynomial approximation under C^n or Lipschitz assumptions) to obtain end-to-end ǫ-accuracy runtime scalings. The methodology is mathematical and algorithmic, relying on circuit constructions, quantum Monte Carlo subroutines, linear-algebra sensitivity bounds, and approximation theory, with no empirical experiments or numerical hardware runs.

**Algorithms used:** Least Squares Monte Carlo (classical LSM), Quantum Least Squares Monte Carlo (quantum LSM) (proposed), QMonteCarlo (Montanaro's quantum Monte Carlo / amplitude-estimation-based estimator), Quantum amplitude estimation (as underlying principle for QMonteCarlo), Grover-Rudolph state preparation (discussed as an approach to prepare distributions), HHL (mentioned as a possible alternative for linear systems; not used in the main algorithm)

## Findings
- [speculative] The authors propose a quantum least-squares Monte Carlo (LSM) algorithm for stochastic optimal stopping problems that replaces classical Monte Carlo subroutines with quantum Monte Carlo and computes stopping times in superposition.
- [speculative] Under mild smoothness assumptions on the continuation value functions (continuation values in C^n with n sufficiently large), the quantum LSM achieves an asymptotic nearly-quadratic improvement in runtime dependence on the Monte Carlo error parameter ϵ versus the classical LSM (roughly improving from O(ϵ^{-2}) to O(ϵ^{-1}) up to polylog and dimension-dependent factors).
- [speculative] The paper provides precise complexity bounds (theorems and corollaries) for both the quantum and classical LSM algorithms in terms of T (number of time steps), m (number of basis functions), d (state dimension), n (smoothness order), problem-dependent norms (R, L), and the minimum singular values of certain matrices (σ_min).
- [speculative] The quantum algorithm requires quantum sampling access to the Markov chain (state-preparation oracle) and quantum query access to payoff and basis functions; these oracles are assumed as inputs and are nontrivial to implement in practice.
- [speculative] For the special case of Brownian motion and using Hermite polynomials, the approximation matrices A_t become (approximately) identity matrices, simplifying analysis and improving constants in the complexity bounds.
- [speculative] For geometric Brownian motion, chosen monomials yield Vandermonde-structured matrices; the minimum singular value of these matrices is exponentially sensitive to polynomial degree q and complicates the complexity dependence on ϵ.
- [speculative] The algorithm computes dynamic programming / stopping times in superposition and requires redoing backward-recursion steps, leading to an O(T^2) factor in some parts of the runtime (instead of O(T) in naive counting).
- [speculative] Error bounds are provided that combine approximation error from projection onto the chosen finite basis and stochastic (Monte Carlo) estimation error; the final output error is the sum of these contributions (with multiplicative factors depending on T and other constants).
- [speculative] Comparison with prior quantum work (Miyamoto): authors claim their projection-based approach is more general (applies to polynomial approximation spaces) and that their requirements on smoothness are weaker than assuming analyticity, though their bounds involve problem-specific quantities (e.g., σ_min).
- [speculative] The authors explicitly note several practical caveats: exponential dependence on dimension d and on parameters (and on T in some bounds), need for high-precision arithmetic and controlled-rotation primitives, the nontriviality of preparing the required quantum sampling state, and no numerical experiments are provided to validate practical speedups.

**Results summary:** This theoretical preprint introduces a quantum version of the classical least-squares Monte Carlo (LSM) algorithm for stochastic optimal stopping (e.g., American option pricing). The quantum LSM replaces classical Monte Carlo with quantum Monte Carlo (amplitude-estimation-style subroutines) and constructs stopping times in superposition via quantum circuits. The authors derive rigorous error bounds and time-complexity formulas for both the quantum and classical procedures, showing that under smoothness assumptions on continuation values (Cn regularity with n growing slowly with 1/ϵ) the quantum algorithm attains a nearly quadratic improvement in dependence on the additive Monte Carlo error parameter ϵ (roughly from O(ϵ^{-2}) to O(ϵ^{-1}), up to polylog and dimension-dependent factors). They analyze special cases (Brownian motion and geometric Brownian motion), discuss the role of basis choice (Hermite polynomials, monomials), highlight required quantum input models (state preparation and function oracles), and emphasize multiple practical caveats and dependencies (σ_min, dimension, T, precision). No experimental or empirical performance results are presented.

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** theoretical

The paper proves (theoretically) that, under explicit assumptions—most importantly: access to quantum sampling/function oracles, bounded problem-dependent quantities (R, L), a positive lower bound on σ_min, and smoothness of continuation values (E[Z_{τ+1}|X] ∈ C^n with n growing slowly with 1/ϵ)—the quantum LSM can reduce the Monte Carlo sampling dependence from O(ϵ^{-2}) to O(ϵ^{-1}) (nearly quadratic speedup) in asymptotic runtime. The advantage is conditional and theoretical (no experimental validation), and it is weakened or negated when problem-specific constants (dimension d, σ_min, required polynomial degree q, or T) introduce large or exponential overheads.

## Contradictions
- The paper qualifies its quantum speedup: although it shows near-quadratic improvement in the Monte-Carlo precision parameter under its input model, it also reports an increased time-step dependence (O(T^2) vs classical O(T)) and sensitivity to quantities like the Gram matrix minimum singular value (σ_min). This contradicts simplistic claims that a quantum LSM uniformly outperforms classical LSM for real-world instances without accounting for input-oracle costs and conditioning.
- The authors emphasize that the quantum speedup addresses only the Monte-Carlo error component and not deterministic approximation error (projection bias). Thus, any claim of overall quantum superiority must be tempered: if approximation error dominates, the asymptotic Monte-Carlo speedup may not yield practical gains.
