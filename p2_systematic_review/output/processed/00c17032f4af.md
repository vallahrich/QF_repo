## Abstract summary
The paper proposes an enhanced binary quadratic programming model for currency arbitrage that adds simple-cycle preservation constraints to ensure feasible, non-redundant trading cycles. It evaluates multiple solvers — quantum annealing, a gate-based variational approach using Adaptive Cost Encoding (ACE) with classical local-search post-processing, Tabu Search, and Gurobi — on real exchange-rate data, comparing arbitrage profit and execution time and showing quantum/hybrid methods offer competitive solution quality with lower execution latency.
## Methodology
The authors formulate currency arbitrage as a node-based binary quadratic programming (BQP) model that enforces simple-cycle preservation (sub-tour elimination) via node-position binary variables x_{i,k} and constraints (one currency per cycle position, each currency at most once, adjacency feasibility, and start/end equality). The BQP is converted to a QUBO by adding quadratic penalty terms for constraints; penalty weights (λ1..λ4) are chosen using the Verma and Lewis Method (VLM). They solve the QUBO/BQP with multiple solvers: Quantum Annealing (QA) via D-Wave Ocean, a qubit-efficient gate-based variational approach (Adaptive Cost Encoding, ACE) implemented and simulated in PennyLane using the DefaultQubit backend, classical Tabu Search (TS), and Gurobi (solving the BQP directly). For ACE, they use the qubit-efficient encoding (number of qubits = ceil(log2(n)) + 1 for n binary variables), prepare hardware-efficient ansatz circuits (two different ansatz variants called Circuit 1 and Circuit 2), and train circuit parameters using various classical optimizers (COBYLA, SLSQP, Differential Evolution (DE), Genetic Algorithm (GA), Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO)); Differential Evolution was selected as best in experiments. The ACE output is decoded from measured state-index probabilities to a binary candidate solution and then improved using a classical local-search post-processing (sequential bit-swap flips that check feasibility and objective improvement). Experiments use real OANDA exchange-rate data for 14 currencies, vary cycle length K from 2 to 10 (thus varying problem size and number of binary variables N(K+1)), and compare solution quality (arbitrage profit reported in pips) and execution time across solvers. Each ACE circuit configuration was run multiple times (five runs reported) and best solutions retained. Gurobi and Tabu Search serve as classical baselines.

**Algorithms used:** Quantum Annealing, Adaptive Cost Encoding (ACE) - qubit-efficient VQA, Variational Quantum Algorithm (VQA), QUBO formulation / penalty method, Differential Evolution (classical optimizer), COBYLA, SLSQP, Genetic Algorithm (GA), Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO), Classical Local Search (multi-bit swap / bit-flip postprocessing), Tabu Search, Gurobi (BQP/MIP solver)
**Frameworks:** PennyLane (DefaultQubit simulator), D-Wave Ocean, Gurobi

**Experimental setup:** Experiments run on a classical workstation with an 11th Gen Intel Core i5-1145G7 @ 2.60GHz (8 logical cores) and 16 GB RAM. ACE circuits were simulated using PennyLane's DefaultQubit simulator. Quantum Annealing experiments used D-Wave Ocean. Gurobi and Tabu Search executed on the same classical machine.

**Dataset:** Real-time currency exchange rates from OANDA (2025). A 14×14 exchange-rate matrix for 14 currencies at a single timestamp was used; zeros indicate absent direct pairs. Rates were used to build directed graph edges and then converted (negative logarithm) for objective construction and QUBO formulation.
## Experiment details
### Input
Source: OANDA currency converter (2025). Size: 14 currencies (N=14); experiments vary cycle length K from 2 to 10, producing N(K+1) binary variables per instance. Preprocessing: construct directed-graph representation (edges only where direct exchange exists), convert exchange rates to negative log weights for objective, encode adjacency impossibilities as constraints, build QUBO by adding quadratic penalty terms for constraints and tune penalty weights using Verma and Lewis Method (VLM).

### Process
1) Formulate node-based BQP with simple-cycle preservation constraints. 2) Convert BQP to QUBO by adding squared-penalty terms with penalties λ1..λ4 chosen using VLM. 3) For Quantum Annealing: submit QUBO to D-Wave Ocean and collect samples. 4) For ACE (gate-based VQA): reduce qubit count using ACE encoding (qubits = ceil(log2(n)) + 1), prepare ansatz (two variants: Circuit 1 and Circuit 2), initialize equal superposition, train circuit parameters with classical optimizers (COBYLA, SLSQP, DE, GA, PSO) on DefaultQubit simulator; run each circuit configuration five times and keep best run. 5) Decode ACE measurement distributions into candidate binary vectors (Pr(x_i=1)=|β_i|^2), then apply classical local-search (sequential bit-swaps) that flip bits only if feasible and improving; complexity O(n^2) per full local-search round. 6) Run classical baselines: Tabu Search and Gurobi (direct BQP solve). 7) Evaluate outputs by arbitrage profit (computed as product of exchange rates along cycle minus one, reported in pips) and execution time. Parameters varied: K (2..10), number of variables N(K+1), ACE circuit layers (tested depths, Circuit 2 with two layers selected), number of iterations/runs (5 repeats reported for ACE).

### Output
Outputs: best-found arbitrage cycle(s) per instance, arbitrage profit reported in pips, and total execution time per solver. Baselines: Gurobi (optimal BQP solver) and Tabu Search. Comparative metrics: profit (pips) and execution time scaling with problem size. Results presented as tables of profit per solver and plots of execution time vs problem size. ACE and QA reported competitive profit on small instances and lower execution latency compared to Gurobi and Tabu Search.

**Results summary:** The paper introduces an enhanced BQP/QUBO model for currency arbitrage with simple-cycle preservation constraints and evaluates multiple solvers (quantum annealing via D-Wave Ocean SDK, an ACE-based variational hybrid method run on a PennyLane simulator with classical local search, Tabu Search, and Gurobi) on real-world 14-currency exchange data. Empirically, for the tested problem sizes (K=2..10), QA and ACE-LS produced comparable arbitrage profits to Gurobi and Tabu Search on small/medium instances and showed lower execution times and more favorable scaling in the authors' experiments. ACE encoding reduced qubit counts per the ⌈log2(n)⌉+1 formula, DE worked best as the classical optimizer for ACE training, and classical local-search post-processing improved ACE outputs. The authors frame these findings as encouraging for future quantum-enabled, time-sensitive financial applications, while noting the need for experiments on real quantum hardware and further hybrid algorithm development.

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** speculative

The paper reports that QA and ACE-LS showed lower execution times and competitive solution quality versus classical solvers on the tested small-to-medium instances; however, ACE-LS was run on a simulator (PennyLane DefaultQubit) and the QA experiments appear to use D-Wave's Ocean tooling (not clearly reported as hardware runs), and no broad or provable quantum speedup or advantage is demonstrated. Thus claims about quantum advantage remain speculative and limited to the specific experimental settings.
## Limitations
- ACE-LS experiments were performed on a simulator (PennyLane DefaultQubit) rather than on real gate-based quantum hardware
- QA and ACE-LS performance and timings reported do not reflect end-to-end production latencies (e.g., device queueing, embedding, readout, and communication overheads)
- Quantum approaches (QA, ACE-LS) produce comparable or optimal results for small problems but solution quality degrades or is not best for larger problem sizes (observed for K ≥ 8)
- Classical local-search post-processing (multi-bit swap) is required to improve ACE outputs, introducing classical overhead
- The experimental dataset is small/sparse (14 currencies, a single timestamp, many missing direct exchange pairs), limiting generality and realism [inferred]
- Model and experiments do not demonstrate meeting the microsecond-to-millisecond latencies required for real-time arbitrage in production trading systems
- Scalability concerns: the node-based formulation uses N(K+1) binary variables and may become intractable as N and K grow; qubit and circuit resource limits remain a bottleneck even with ACE encoding [inferred]
- Penalty weight selection (λ1..λ4) is done using VLM but sensitivity and robustness to these choices are not extensively analysed; suboptimal penalties can produce infeasible or poor solutions [inferred]
- Market realism: treatment of transaction costs, slippage, latency, order-book dynamics, and market impact is limited or not fully modelled in the experiments [inferred]
- Benchmarking lacks comparison to highly optimized classical low-latency solutions (e.g., FPGA/GPU/HPC implementations) that are used in production arbitrage trading [inferred]

## Open questions
- How will ACE-LS perform on real, noisy gate-based quantum hardware compared to simulator results?
- Can hybrid classical-quantum algorithms be designed to further reduce execution time while maintaining or improving solution quality?
- Is it possible to implement the local-search (LS) / multi-bit swap step natively in the quantum domain to remove or reduce classical post-processing overhead?
- Can neutral-atom quantum computers or other emerging hardware platforms provide practical advantages (scalability, coherence) for this problem?
- What are the practical limits on problem size (number of currencies K and N) that can be solved with ACE, QA, and classical methods under realistic hardware and time constraints?
- How to systematically and robustly choose penalty weights in the QUBO to guarantee feasibility and good solution quality across instances?
- How sensitive are the quantum methods to hardware noise, readout errors, and finite sampling, and can error mitigation or robust encodings address this?
- What is the full end-to-end latency (including data acquisition, model encoding, quantum execution, decoding, and order submission) for quantum-enabled pipelines, and how does it compare with classical ultra-low-latency systems?
- Can alternative encodings, ansatz designs, or variational strategies improve solution quality or reduce required circuit depth/qubits beyond ACE?
- How to incorporate richer market dynamics (transaction costs, slippage, order-book effects) into the model without exploding the QUBO complexity?
- What are the trade-offs between qubit efficiency (ACE) and the classical overhead of decoding and local search in streaming real-time applications?
- How do embedding choices, chain strengths, and qubit connectivity on annealers affect solution validity and repeatability for currency arbitrage QUBOs?
- Can a demonstrable quantum advantage (in either solution quality or latency) be achieved in a realistic currency-arbitrage deployment?
- How often would gate-based variational circuits need retraining in a non-stationary market, and what is the associated operational cost?
