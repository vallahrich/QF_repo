## Abstract summary
This paper presents an end-to-end application of a Quantum Support Vector Machine (QSVM) for payment fraud detection using IBM Safer Payments and Qiskit, comparing QSVM to rule-based methods and classical ML (Random Forest, XGBoost). The authors introduce a quantum feature selection algorithm based on quantum feature maps/kernels and evaluate a hybrid quantum-classical ensemble, finding that QSVM offers complementary feature-space exploration and can improve accuracy on heavily reduced datasets (simulated quantum hardware); results are primarily from simulation with future hardware tests planned.
## Methodology
The study compares three approaches for payment fraud detection on the same underlying transaction data: a domain-expert rule-generator (deterministic rule-based model), classical machine-learning (tree-based models: XGBoost and Random Forest) and a quantum-machine-learning approach based on a Quantum Support Vector Machine (QSVM). Data were ingested and feature-engineered in IBM Safer Payments, exported and pre-processed for the different methods. Classical ML models used conventional preprocessing (categorical handling, removal of highly correlated features, under-sampling to balance classes, hyperparameter tuning with XGBoost CV and RandomizedSearchCV for Random Forest). For QSVM, the authors developed a forward/permutation quantum feature-selection algorithm that iteratively evaluates combinations of features by computing quantum kernels via Qiskit's ZZ- and Z-feature-maps (depth 1 and 2, varying entanglement) and selecting the best features according to accuracy. Quantum kernel values were sampled on simulators (state_vector and qasm with/without noise models derived from IBM devices) with measurement-error mitigation and classical optimization (solve SVM dual) applied using the kernel matrix. A hybrid ensemble was built by training both classical and quantum classifiers, identifying instances where the classifiers disagreed, and training a small meta-classifier (logistic regression or classical SVM) on that disagreement subset to choose which classifier to trust. Evaluation used Accuracy, AUC and business-oriented KPIs (Hit Rate vs False Alarm Ratio / modified ROC*). Multiple randomized trials and chronologically consistent train/test splits were used to reduce sampling bias.

**Algorithms used:** Rule-based (Rule Generator), XGBoost, Random Forest, Quantum Support Vector Machine (QSVM), Quantum kernel methods (Z and ZZ feature maps), Quantum Feature Importance Selection (permutation/forward selection using QSVM), Classical SVM (meta-classifier), Logistic Regression (meta-classifier)
**Frameworks:** IBM Safer Payments, Qiskit, XGBoost, scikit-learn

**Experimental setup:** Workflows run via IBM Quantum cloud. QSVM kernels and feature maps implemented with Qiskit. Initial QSVM development and feature-search used state_vector simulator; further runs used qasm simulator with and without noise models (noise model derived from the ibm_cairo device). Transpilation used optimization_level=3 and Sabre layout/routing; measurement-error mitigation was enabled for noisy runs. Example execution parameters shown in the paper: training size 1500, test size 1000, feature size up to 7, order_of_expansion 'ZZ', depth 2, entanglement 'full', alpha 2.0, n_shots 8192. Circuit depths reported on the order of ~70 for seven features. Multiple trials (5–10) were executed to estimate variation.

**Dataset:** Proprietary real card payment transaction dataset from a European cross-border processing portfolio (approx. 2.4 million transactions; ~80% debit, ~20% credit), with ~3,216 transactions labeled as fraudulent. Raw inputs: ~12 transaction attributes + ~2 demographic attributes; feature engineering produced behavioral/profile features bringing total attributes to 48 (and increased to 69 after categorical expansion).

## Findings
- [supported] A QSVM-based quantum feature selection algorithm (iterative permutation of feature subsets with QSVM evaluation) can be used to identify a small set of features for a reduced-dimension fraud-detection model.
- [supported] The QSVM-selected features differ from the feature rankings produced by classical methods (XGBoost, Random Forest), indicating complementary exploration of the feature space.
- [supported] Quantum feature maps with greater entanglement (ZZ feature map, depth=2) produced better accuracy in the authors' QSVM experiments on the reduced dataset than less-entangled maps.
- [supported] On simulated (noise-free) quantum backends, QSVM achieved comparable accuracy and AUC to classical models on a heavily under-sampled/reduced dataset (accuracy ≈ 0.78, AUC ≈ 0.81).
- [supported] In noisy simulations emulating real devices, QSVM performance degraded substantially (reported accuracy dropped to ≈ 0.55 with large variance), indicating sensitivity to realistic noise.
- [supported] A hybrid/mixed quantum-classical ensemble (using a metaclassifier to resolve disagreements between classical XGBoost and QSVM) produced modest improvements in accuracy versus the individual models on the reduced balanced dataset (e.g., QSVM+XGBoost-optimized accuracy 81.0% vs XGBoost-optimized 80.0%).
- [supported] The quantum and classical classifiers disagreed on labels for a small but non-negligible fraction of datapoints (≈5.2% training, ≈5.5% test), providing the basis for the metaclassifier ensemble approach.
- [supported] Classical tree-based methods (XGBoost, Random Forest) performed well on the original imbalanced dataset; XGBoost showed slightly better ROC*/Hit-rate tradeoffs in the authors' experiments.
- [supported] The rule-generator (human/expert-assisted rule model) showed overfitting (train curve substantially above test curve) in the studied dataset.
- [speculative] Quantum machine learning approaches (e.g., QSVM) could be disruptive for payment fraud detection by identifying feature relationships that classical feature selection might remove or miss.
- [speculative] As quantum hardware scales (more qubits, lower error rates), the number of features and/or data that can be encoded and evaluated by QSVM will increase, potentially improving performance further.
- [supported] The end-to-end workflow requires careful data pre-processing (handling categorical variables, under-sampling, normalization) before applying quantum feature mapping and QSVM.
- [supported] The authors implemented the experiments primarily on Qiskit simulators (statevector and qasm with/without noise) and did not present production hardware results; they note hardware implementation as future work.
- [speculative] The QSVM-derived kernel/feature-map selection could provide a different and useful inductive bias compared to classical kernels, but this is not proven as a general advantage in the paper.

**Results summary:** The paper reports an end-to-end study applying a QSVM-based quantum feature selection and classification pipeline to a real-world payment fraud dataset (heavily under-sampled/reduced for quantum feasibility). The authors developed an iterative QSVM feature-selection procedure, found that entangled ZZ feature maps (depth=2) performed best in their tests, and showed that QSVM-selected features differ from classical feature-importance rankings. On noise-free simulators QSVM achieved accuracy and AUC comparable to classical models on the reduced dataset (accuracy ≈ 0.78, AUC ≈ 0.81). Realistic noise simulations caused substantial performance degradation. Combining QSVM and classical XGBoost via a metaclassifier ensemble yielded modest accuracy improvements (e.g., up to 81.0% for the optimized hybrid vs 80.0% for optimized XGBoost alone) on the reduced balanced dataset. The study emphasizes complementarity between quantum and classical methods but does not demonstrate a proven, general quantum advantage on real hardware or large-scale, production data.

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** speculative

The paper demonstrates that QSVM provides complementary feature-space exploration and that hybrid quantum-classical ensembles can yield modest accuracy gains on a heavily reduced, simulated dataset. However, experiments were primarily on simulators (with noisy simulations showing severe degradation), the dataset was drastically under-sampled to fit near-term quantum constraints, and no hardware-level demonstration or theoretical proof of a general quantum advantage for fraud detection is provided. Thus any claim of quantum advantage remains speculative based on these results.
## Limitations
- Results are obtained primarily on quantum simulators (statevector and qasm); extension to real quantum hardware is not included in this paper.
- Current quantum hardware constraints: limited number of qubits restricts the number of features that can be encoded (each qubit associated with a feature in their encoding).
- Quantum experiments required drastic under-sampling and drastic reduction of dataset size to fit current quantum hardware/simulation budgets.
- Noisy quantum simulations show markedly degraded accuracy compared to ideal simulation (qasm with noise: much lower accuracy and higher variance).
- Circuit depth is high (e.g., depth ~70 for 7 features) and needs reduction/optimization before feasible hardware runs.
- There is no general recipe for building quantum kernels for arbitrary real-world data; kernel design remains ad hoc and problem-dependent.
- QSVM lacks an in-built feature-importance method at the time of the study; classical feature selection may bias quantum model performance.
- Comparability of KPIs across methods is complicated: Rule Generator reports monetary hit rate while ML models report record-count based hit rates.
- Categorical and mixed-type data limit the applicability of standard classical dimensionality reduction (e.g., PCA); FAMD showed no discrimination for this dataset.
- The Rule Generator method showed pronounced overfitting (train curve well above test curve).
- Metaclassifier approach relied on a small disagreement subset between classical and quantum models, limiting complexity of the metaclassifier that could be trained.
- [inferred] The heavy preprocessing (feature engineering, categorical handling, normalization) required to make data usable for QSVM may remove patterns or introduce biases.
- [inferred] Results reported are specific to one real-world dataset (European cross-border card payments); generalizability to other portfolios or product types is not demonstrated.
- [inferred] The paper does not address end-to-end operational constraints for production deployment (latency, throughput, integration complexity) beyond noting feasibility with IBM Safer Payments.
- [inferred] Error mitigation was only briefly applied; the robustness of mitigation strategies across different devices and workloads is not fully explored.
## Open questions
- Does QSVM (or other QML approaches) provide a material advantage over state-of-the-art classical models on larger, less-reduced real-world fraud datasets when run on real quantum hardware?
- How to design quantum feature maps / kernels systematically for financial transaction data to maximize classification performance (is there a general recipe)?
- What is the trade-off between feature-map entanglement (which improved accuracy here) and practical hardware realizability (circuit depth, noise)?
- How effective and scalable are error mitigation techniques for QSVM on current and near-term quantum devices in the fraud-detection context?
- How to translate quantum-model KPIs computed on heavily under-sampled/normalized datasets into operational monetary KPIs comparable with classical/legacy systems?
- How robust are the QSVM-selected features across different data splits, portfolios, and levels of under-sampling? Do they generalize?
- What is the impact of different data encoding strategies (beyond one-feature-per-qubit angle encoding) on performance and required qubit counts?
- Can mixed quantum-classical ensembles consistently improve operational KPIs in production settings, and what are the best metaclassifier strategies when larger disagreement datasets are available?
- How to reduce circuit depth and optimize transpilation for specific hardware topologies without losing the quantum model's discriminatory power?
- What are the privacy, security, and regulatory implications of using quantum models or quantum cloud services for sensitive financial data?

## Key ideas
- #idea:quantum-advantage — QSVM (quantum kernel methods) provided complementary feature-space exploration and yielded improvements in accuracy/AUC on heavily reduced datasets compared to classical baselines (XGBoost, Random Forest) in simulation experiments.
- #idea:hybrid-approach — A hybrid ensemble that trains both classical models and QSVM and uses a small meta-classifier on the disagreement subset can leverage complementary strengths and improve business-oriented KPIs (Hit Rate vs False Alarm Ratio).
- #idea:near-term-feasibility — Experiments were executed on statevector and qasm simulators (with and without IBM-device-derived noise models) using realistic transpilation settings and measurement-error mitigation, showing a path for near-term NISQ exploration though limited to small feature sizes.
- #limitation:simulation-only — Reported results are primarily from classical simulators (statevector and qasm with noise models); no experiments on real quantum hardware are reported (hardware tests planned for future work).
- #limitation:qubit-count — The approach required drastic dimensionality reduction (feature sets reduced to ~7 features) to fit into the quantum feature-map circuits, indicating current qubit limits constrain practical deployment.
- #limitation:noise — Noisy-run experiments relied on device-derived noise models and measurement-error mitigation; circuit depths (~70 for seven features) and high shot counts (n_shots=8192) imply sensitivity to real-device noise and overheads.
- #limitation:data-encoding — Feature encoding required MinMax scaling to [-1,1] and specific quantum feature maps (Z/ZZ maps); the cost and fidelity of encoding high-dimensional engineered features into quantum kernels is a practical limitation.
- #limitation:no-empirical-validation — Although realistic device noise models were used, there is no empirical validation on actual QPUs in this study, limiting claims about real-world performance.
## Contradictions
- contradiction:scalability — The paper's positive outcomes are limited to small, heavily reduced datasets and simulator/noise-model experiments; this contradicts any broad claims that QSVM or quantum ML approaches will straightforwardly scale to production-scale fraud detection without substantial increases in qubit counts, improved noise performance, or alternative data-encoding strategies.
