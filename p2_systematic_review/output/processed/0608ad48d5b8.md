## Abstract summary
The paper presents a digital quantum algorithm that maps the Black–Scholes PDE to a Schrödinger-type (non-Hermitian) Hamiltonian and simulates its dynamics by embedding the non-unitary propagator into an enlarged Hilbert space using a single ancillary qubit (unitary dilation) and periodic boundary conditions. The method leverages efficient Hamiltonian simulation techniques (e.g., Quantum Signal Processing), duplicates the initial condition to mitigate edge effects, and reports accuracy comparable to classical numerical methods with an estimated implementation using 9 qubits and a post-selection success probability above 60%; it is also extensible to time-dependent volatility PDEs.
## Methodology
The paper develops a digital quantum algorithm to solve the Black–Scholes partial differential equation (PDE) by mapping it to a Schrödinger-type evolution and then simulating the resulting (non-Hermitian) Hamiltonian on a quantum computer. Key methodological steps are: 1) analytic transform: change of variables S = e^x and time reversal (t → τ = T − t) to write the PDE as a first-order-in-time evolution ∂τV = iĤ_BS V, with an explicit decomposition of Ĥ_BS into Hermitian and anti-Hermitian parts that commute; 2) discretization: spatial domain is digitized on an equispaced grid using Nyquist–Shannon sampling bounds, producing position and (via discrete Fourier transform) momentum bases on N_x = 2^n points; two discretization approaches are described for the derivative (finite-difference circulant matrix with periodic boundary conditions, and derivative defined through the DFT); 3) boundary handling and state preparation: to match the periodic momentum discretization, the initial payoff function (e.g., European put) is shifted and duplicated (via reflection) so the boundary becomes periodic and edge effects are mitigated. The normalized duplicated payoff state is prepared using amplitude-encoding techniques (noted that general arbitrary state loading is exponential but smooth payoff functions can be approximately loaded efficiently citing known methods); 4) embedding non-unitary dynamics: the non-unitary propagator component O(τ)=exp(iτĤ_BS^A) (anti-Hermitian part exponent) is embedded into a larger unitary via unitary dilation by adding a single ancillary qubit; the embedded unitary is implemented so that post-selecting the ancilla in |0> reproduces the non-unitary action on the system; 5) efficient Hamiltonian simulation: because the momentum operator diagonalizes under the quantum Fourier transform, the dynamics reduce to simulation of diagonal (sparse) operators in momentum space. The authors advocate the use of Quantum Signal Processing (QSP)/qubitization techniques as the optimal Hamiltonian simulation primitive, and apply complexity bounds from QSP; they also note other subroutines (QFT, diagonal phase application, inverse QFT); 6) measurement and post-selection: after applying the embedded unitary and the Hermitian component e^{iτĤ_BS^H}, the ancillary embedding qubit is measured and only outcomes with ancilla=|0> are kept (post-selection). Stock-price point values are retrieved by multi-controlled projection onto the position basis and amplitude (or amplitude estimation) is used to estimate probabilities/amplitudes corresponding to option prices. The paper provides analytical expressions and lower bounds for the post-selection success probability (showing >~0.6 for typical financial parameters), complexity estimates (referencing QSP optimal scaling), and discusses extensions (e.g., stock-dependent volatility) and practical considerations (e.g., number of qubits: 9 qubits claimed for a proof-of-principle fault-tolerant run, use of one ancillary embedding qubit and one duplication qubit).

**Algorithms used:** Quantum Signal Processing (QSP) / qubitization, Quantum Fourier Transform (QFT) / Discrete Fourier Transform, Unitary dilation / embedding of non-unitary propagator, Amplitude encoding (state preparation of payoff), Amplitude estimation (for amplitude readout), Finite-difference discretization (momentum via circulant matrix), Nyquist–Shannon sampling (for discretization)

## Findings
- [speculative] The Black–Scholes PDE (under constant volatility and interest rate) can be mapped to a Schrödinger-like equation yielding a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian formulation.
- [speculative] The non-unitary propagator of the resulting non-Hermitian Black–Scholes Hamiltonian can be embedded into a larger unitary using a single ancillary qubit via unitary dilation, enabling simulation on a (digital) quantum computer followed by post-selection.
- [speculative] Imposing periodic boundary conditions on the discretized momentum operator and duplicating the initial payoff (via one discretization qubit) mitigates edge effects and improves stability and performance.
- [speculative] The dynamics (which reduce to functions of the momentum operator after QFT) can be simulated efficiently using state-of-the-art Hamiltonian simulation techniques, specifically Quantum Signal Processing (QSP); the simulation is optimal in the sense of QSP complexity bounds for sparse Hamiltonians.
- [supported] Classical numerical emulations reported in the paper show convergence of the discretized simulation and indicate achievable precision comparable to classical numerical algorithms for the chosen parameter ranges.
- [supported] Numerical evaluation (simulations) indicates an expected post-selection success probability above 60% for typical financial parameters (they provide a lower bound and parameterized plots).
- [speculative] The approach is presented as extensible to time-dependent (stock-price-dependent) volatility PDEs and more complex PDEs (e.g., coupled options), although such extensions are not worked out in detail here.
- [speculative] The method is an alternative to Monte Carlo–based quantum sampling approaches (it targets solving the PDE via Hamiltonian simulation rather than sampling an SDE), and could be used as a subroutine in larger pricing pipelines (e.g., combined with forward stock dynamics and expectation evaluation).
- [speculative] Efficient loading of the smooth payoff initial state is assumed feasible via referenced amplitude-encoding/state-preparation methods (polynomial gate cost for smooth functions) rather than demonstrated experimentally here.

**Results summary:** The paper proposes a digital quantum algorithm to solve the Black–Scholes PDE by mapping it to a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian and embedding its non-unitary propagator into a unitary on an enlarged Hilbert space using one ancillary qubit. Periodic boundary conditions and duplication of the initial payoff are used to mitigate edge effects. The authors argue the momentum-based form enables efficient diagonalization via the quantum Fourier transform and that Quantum Signal Processing yields optimal Hamiltonian simulation complexity. Classical numerical emulations (simulations) are presented showing convergence, discretization error behavior, and that, for chosen parameters, a 9-qubit circuit could achieve accuracy comparable to classical algorithms with an expected post-selection success probability above 60%. The work is positioned as a theoretical/algorithmic contribution with numerical support, and extensions to more complex volatility models are proposed as future work.

**Performance claims:**
- 9 qubits (plus required ancilla) suffice in the authors' numerical simulations to simulate the Black–Scholes PDE dynamics with precision comparable to classical numerical algorithms (reported via discretization convergence plots).
- Expected success probability of the post-selection procedure (to recover the non-unitary dynamics after unitary embedding) above 60% for typical maturity and interest-rate parameters (numerically evaluated; a lower bound e^{-2Tr} γ(Nx,K) is given and plotted).
- Embedding overhead: only one additional ancillary qubit is required for the unitary dilation embedding of the non-unitary propagator.
- Hamiltonian simulation complexity (per cited QSP theorem): O(t d ||H||_max + log(1/ε)/log log(1/ε)) oracle queries (where d is sparsity); the authors assert the Black–Scholes Hamiltonian is 1-sparse after diagonalization and thus fits within QSP's optimal regime.
- Amplitude estimation for expected-value extraction offers quantum sampling complexity O(1/ε) versus classical Monte Carlo O(1/ε^2), as referenced in the manuscript.
- Quantum Fourier Transform is used to diagonalize the discrete momentum operator efficiently with gate complexity O(n log n) for n qubits (standard QFT cost cited).
- Duplication of initial condition uses one of the discretization qubits (i.e., a deliberate symmetric encoding) to reduce boundary artifacts when applying periodic boundary conditions.
## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** theoretical

The paper presents a theoretically efficient Hamiltonian-simulation-based algorithm (using QSP) and cites asymptotically optimal simulation complexity for sparse Hamiltonians; however it does not demonstrate an end-to-end, empirical quantum speedup for option pricing on real quantum hardware. Numerical classical emulations support feasibility (accuracy and post-selection probability) but no experimental quantum advantage is demonstrated.
## Limitations
- Algorithm focuses on the Hamiltonian simulation subroutine and does not present an end-to-end option-pricing pipeline (e.g., it does not fully integrate the underlying stock evolution or the final expected-value aggregation).
- The Black–Scholes Hamiltonian is non-Hermitian and must be embedded into a larger unitary via an ancillary qubit with a post-selection step; the simulation is therefore probabilistic and requires discarding runs where the ancilla outcome is unfavorable (success probability depends on parameters).
- Efficient initial state preparation is assumed for payoff states (smooth functions), but in general loading arbitrary states requires exponential resources; the paper relies on cited approximate/specialized loading methods rather than solving the general loading problem.
- The method enforces periodic boundary conditions for efficient diagonalization (QFT) and compensates by duplicating the initial condition; this requires using one of the discretization qubits for duplication and alters the effective simulated domain.
- Results and resource claims assume a fault-tolerant quantum computer (the paper reports feasibility with 9 qubits on a fault-tolerant device); effects of NISQ noise and realistic hardware errors are not analyzed.
- The stock-price-dependent (spatially varying) volatility case is not solved here: introducing σ(x) increases complexity (operator ordering, embedding of full dynamics) and is left unaddressed.
- [inferred] The embedding + post-selection approach introduces overhead from repeated runs: even with reported success probabilities >60%, total runtime/resources increase due to discarded trials.
- [inferred] Measurement/estimation overhead may be substantial: amplitudes for individual grid points scale like O(2^{-n/2}), so amplitude estimation or sampling to obtain prices with given precision can require many repetitions or costly amplitude-estimation subroutines.
- [inferred] Dependence on periodic boundary conditions and analytic diagonalization via QFT limits applicability: problems without suitable periodicity or without known analytical momentum eigenvalues may not admit the same efficient diagonalization/oracle construction.
- [inferred] The construction assumes accessibility of oracles (or analytic formulas) for Hamiltonian eigenvalues used by QSP; for more complex PDEs or discretizations, building these oracles may be nontrivial and costly.
## Open questions
- Can choices of stock-price-dependent volatility σ(x) preserve sparsity of the discretized Hamiltonian, or can such Hamiltonians be efficiently approximated by controllable sparse operators?
- How to extend and scale the embedding/unitary-dilation technique to simulate more general non-Hermitian Hamiltonians and imaginary-time evolution in practice?
- What is the algorithm's performance and resource/accuracy trade-off when implemented on realistic NISQ hardware with noise and limited coherence?
- How to efficiently prepare initial payoff states for more general payoffs and for higher-dimensional/multivariate problems without incurring exponential overhead?
- How to integrate the Hamiltonian-simulation subroutine into a full end-to-end option-pricing workflow (stock dynamics evolution, risk-neutral expectation estimation) efficiently?
- How to adapt the method to handle early-exercise features and path dependence (e.g., American and Asian options)?
- How does the post-selection success probability scale with grid size (Nx), strike K, time to maturity T, and interest rate r in practical market parameter regimes?
- What are the concrete resource costs (gate counts, QSP queries, ancilla/qubits) for the amplitude-estimation or expected-value extraction required to obtain prices to a target precision in realistic instances?
- How to generalize the periodic-boundary duplication trick for problems or PDEs with non-periodic or more complex boundary conditions in a way that preserves efficiency?

**Future work:**
- Introduce and analyze errors associated to NISQ devices to assess realistic implementation and robustness to hardware noise.
- Study stock-price-dependent volatility (σ(x)) cases in detail, including appropriate operator ordering, sparsity considerations, and embedding of the full dynamics.
- Extend the embedding techniques to simulate general non-Hermitian Hamiltonians and imaginary-time evolution.
- Introduce additional degrees of freedom in the model: spatial-time dependent volatility (stochastic local volatility) and coupled options.
- Combine the approach with quantum principal component analysis and coupled Black–Scholes models to address problems with correlated/coupled options.
- Extend the method to other option types such as American and Asian options (handling early exercise and path dependence).
- Investigate efficient initial-state-loading techniques and improved amplitude/expected-value estimation methods to reduce measurement overhead.

## Contradictions
- The paper claims near-term feasibility via a small (9-qubit) circuit yet simultaneously assumes a fault-tolerant regime and efficient amplitude state-loading; this contradicts practical near-term/NISQ applicability and understates scaling/overhead required for error correction and state preparation.
- The authors highlight potential asymptotic advantage via QAE vs classical Monte Carlo but also acknowledge small amplitudes, post-selection costs and QAE resource overhead — creating an internal tension between the claimed advantage and realistic end-to-end resource requirements.
