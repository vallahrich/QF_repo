## Abstract summary
The paper proposes QLSTM, a hybrid quantum-classical model that replaces LSTM gates with variational quantum circuits to improve stock price prediction. The authors validate the approach on IBM quantum simulators and an IBM quantum device using Apple stock data, reporting lower RMSE and higher prediction accuracy than classical LSTM and several other time-series models.
## Methodology
The study develops and evaluates a hybrid quantum-classical sequence model (QLSTM) for single-company stock closing-price prediction. The QLSTM replaces the internal gates of a classical LSTM cell with variational quantum circuits (VQCs): four VQCs map to forget, input, update and output gates, and two VQCs transform cell/hidden states to outputs. Classical input vectors (concatenation of previous hidden state and current feature vector) are encoded into quantum states using angle/rotation encoding (Hadamard followed by Ry and Rz rotations with arctan-based angles). The variational layers use single-qubit rotations (three angles per qubit) and CNOT entanglement (cyclic adjacency) with tunable parameters. Outputs are Pauli-Z expectation values per qubit which undergo classical nonlinearities (sigmoid/tanh) and post-processing. Training uses the parameter-shift rule to compute gradients of VQC parameters, with classical optimization (Adam) minimizing MSE over 50 epochs. Experiments compare QLSTM variants trained in a noiseless IBM simulator and a noisy IBM simulator (noise model derived from real hardware) against a classical LSTM baseline and several other time-series models; inference is also performed on an actual IBM quantum backend (Nazca) to assess hardware effects.

**Algorithms used:** Quantum-Enhanced LSTM (QLSTM) with Variational Quantum Circuits (VQCs), Classical LSTM, Variational Quantum Circuits (VQC), Angle (rotation) data encoding (Ry, Rz), Parameter-shift rule for gradient estimation, Adam optimizer, Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss
**Frameworks:** PyTorch, PennyLane

**Experimental setup:** Training and simulation were performed with PennyLane/PyTorch on classical machines using IBM backends: a noiseless IBM simulator, and a noisy IBM simulator (with injected noise profiles from actual devices). Predictions/inference were also run on an actual IBM quantum processor (IBM Nazca) accessed via the IBM Quantum cloud platform.

**Dataset:** Apple Inc. historical stock data (daily) covering 2022-01-01 to 2023-01-01. Dataset contains 251 observations with columns Date, Open, High, Low, Close. Data were normalized to the range [-1, 1] and split 70% train / 30% test.

## Findings
- [supported] The proposed hybrid QLSTM (quantum-enhanced LSTM) model outperformed a classical LSTM and several classical time-series models on the Apple Inc. stock dataset used in this study (training on IBM simulators).
- [supported] Noiseless QLSTM achieved training accuracy 1.00 and training RMSE 0.0371, and achieved prediction accuracy 0.9736 with prediction RMSE 0.0602 on the test split in the reported experiments.
- [supported] Noisy QLSTM (trained on a simulator with device noise model) achieved training accuracy 0.9714 and training RMSE 0.0511, and prediction accuracy 0.9210 with prediction RMSE 0.0648.
- [supported] QLSTM run on actual IBM hardware (Nazca) produced substantially worse prediction results (prediction accuracy 0.7619, prediction RMSE 0.1401) than simulator-based runs.
- [supported] The classical LSTM baseline in this study achieved training accuracy 0.92, training RMSE 0.0567, and test prediction accuracy 0.8815 with prediction RMSE 0.0693 on the same dataset and training setup.
- [supported] Noisy simulators using simplified noise models can produce better results than actual quantum hardware; the paper observed this effect (simulator > actual device) for their QLSTM.
- [supported] The QLSTM implementation used approximately 254 trainable parameters while the classical LSTM had 288 parameters; QLSTM with fewer parameters gave better results on the reported dataset.
- [speculative] The authors attribute QLSTM's superior performance to quantum data encoding into a higher-dimensional Hilbert space and to expressive power of VQCs (i.e., improved data representation due to quantum encoding).
- [speculative] Increasing the number of qubits does not necessarily improve performance and may degrade performance; the authors link observed degradation to barren plateau effects in VQC training.
- [speculative] The study claims novelty: it is 'among the first' to predict stock prices using a hybrid quantum–classical framework (QLSTM).
- [supported] The study demonstrates practical limitations today: limited dataset size, long queue times for real-device access, device noise, and the need for error mitigation and further work for scaling.
- [disputed] The paper asserts in text that the QLSTM achieved a '50% reduction in RMSE and a 10% improvement in accuracy compared to LSTM' — this numeric claim conflicts with the reported table values (e.g., prediction RMSE 0.0693 vs 0.0602 is ~13% reduction, not 50%).
- [speculative] The authors suggest future directions including deeper variational circuits, quantum error mitigation, expanded datasets, and quantum-inspired algorithms to bridge performance gaps — proposed but not empirically validated here.

**Results summary:** The paper presents a hybrid quantum-classical QLSTM that replaces LSTM gates with variational quantum circuits. On a 251-point Apple stock dataset (70/30 train/test), QLSTM trained and evaluated on an IBM noiseless simulator achieved the best reported metrics (training acc 1.00, training RMSE 0.0371; test acc 0.9736, test RMSE 0.0602). A noisy simulator run produced slightly worse but still superior results versus classical baselines (noisy QLSTM test acc 0.9210, RMSE 0.0648). However, running inference on real IBM hardware produced notably poorer performance (test acc 0.7619, RMSE 0.1401), highlighting current hardware noise and practical limitations. The authors report that QLSTM used fewer parameters than the classical LSTM while achieving better simulator-based results, and they discuss potential causes (quantum encoding, barren plateaus) and limitations (small dataset, simulator vs real-device discrepancy, need for error mitigation).

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** speculative

The paper reports improved predictive performance of their QLSTM on noiseless and noisy simulators compared to classical baselines for a small-stock dataset, but does not demonstrate robust, device-level quantum advantage: results on actual hardware were worse, dataset size and problem scale are small, and causal attribution to uniquely quantum effects (beyond representational/architectural differences) remains theoretical. Therefore any claim of quantum advantage is tentative/speculative rather than demonstrated.
## Limitations
- Limited dataset: only 251 Apple stock observations (1 Jan 2022–1 Jan 2023) were used, restricting statistical power and generalizability.
- Training performed primarily on simulators (noiseless and noisy); actual IBM quantum hardware was used only for prediction due to long queue times, limiting on-device training validation.
- Poorer performance on actual quantum hardware: predictions on the real device showed substantially worse RMSE and accuracy, attributed to device noise and decoherence.
- Model design constrained to specific choices (particular data-encoding scheme, rotation gates, variational layer architecture, and fixed hyperparameters); alternative designs were not exhaustively explored.
- Limited qubit use and circuit sizes in experiments; larger qubit counts and deeper circuits were not thoroughly tested, restricting assessment of scalability.
- No error-mitigation techniques were implemented or evaluated on the real hardware results, leaving hardware-noise effects insufficiently addressed.
- Barren plateau risk: increasing qubit/circuit size can cause vanishing gradients during VQC optimization, which may have impacted performance for larger circuits.
- Possibility of classical simulability: some VQC types (noisy or noiseless) can be simulated classically in polynomial time for small-to-moderate problem sizes, complicating claims of quantum advantage.
- Application scope narrow: focused on single-company stock closing price prediction using structured time-series data; unstructured data (e.g., news, social media) and multi-asset/multivariate settings were not considered.
- [inferred] Risk of overfitting: the Noiseless QLSTM achieved training accuracy of 1.0, suggesting potential overfitting that was not analyzed with cross-validation or held-out validation strategies.
- [inferred] Limited hyperparameter search: hyperparameter choices (e.g., number of qubits, depth, rotation parameterization) appear restricted and not exhaustively optimized.
- [inferred] Resource/access constraints: practical limitations (queue times, limited on-hardware runs) reduced the ability to run repeated/large-scale experiments on real devices.
- [inferred] Lack of interpretability analysis: the quantum-enhanced model outputs and representations were not analyzed for interpretability or explainability in the financial context.
- [inferred] Economic/practical evaluation absent: no downstream evaluation of economic utility (e.g., trading strategy backtest, transaction costs, risk-adjusted returns) was provided.
## Open questions
- Can QLSTM models scale effectively to much larger datasets and higher qubit counts while maintaining or improving predictive performance?
- Which quantum error-mitigation techniques are most effective to close the performance gap between noisy simulators and actual quantum hardware for QLSTM?
- How can VQC and data-encoding designs (gate types, entangling topology, encoding functions) be optimized to maximize expressiveness while avoiding barren plateaus?
- Where is the practical boundary (problem size and noise regime) at which quantum models provide a clear advantage over classical models or classical simulation?
- What is the optimal number of qubits and circuit depth for time-series prediction tasks like stock-price forecasting before training becomes intractable or untrainable?
- How robust are QLSTM models to realistic hardware noise, and how well do noisy simulators capture the true noise behavior of quantum devices?
- To what extent can quantum-inspired algorithms reproduce quantum-like benefits on classical hardware for small-to-medium scale financial problems?
- Can QLSTM generalize to multivariate/multi-asset financial forecasting, incorporate unstructured textual data, or be applied reliably in other time-series domains (energy, healthcare, IoT)?
- How should one evaluate and validate the economic significance (e.g., profitability, risk) of quantum-enhanced forecasts in real trading systems?
- How sensitive are results to normalization/feature-scaling choices, encoding nonlinearities (e.g., arctan vs arcsin), and post-measurement classical processing?

**Future work:**
- Expand QLSTM scalability: evaluate with larger datasets and greater numbers of qubits to assess real-world performance limits.
- Explore advanced quantum error-mitigation techniques to improve performance on actual quantum hardware and reduce the effect of noise.
- Experiment with deeper variational quantum circuits to study effects on model expressiveness and generalization while managing the risk of barren plateaus.
- Investigate diverse quantum circuit designs: different data-encoding schemes, alternative rotation/entangling gates, and varied variational-layer architectures.
- Integrate quantum-inspired algorithms to bridge the gap between quantum and classical models and potentially enable quantum-like performance on classical hardware for smaller-scale tasks.
- Extend application domains: apply QLSTM to other time-series domains (e.g., energy forecasting, healthcare, IoT) and to multivariate/multi-asset financial forecasting.
- Assess and apply methods to identify the boundary where quantum computation significantly outperforms classical simulation, to validate genuine quantum advantage.

- #idea:hybrid-approach — Hybrid quantum-classical architecture: classical preprocessing/postprocessing with six variational quantum circuits replacing LSTM gates; training uses parameter-shift gradients and Adam optimizer within a PennyLane+PyTorch workflow.
- #idea:near-term-feasibility — Experiments include noiseless simulator, noisy simulator (device-derived noise model), and inference runs on an IBM Nazca superconducting QPU, demonstrating that parts of the model can be executed on current NISQ hardware (inference only).

- #limitation:noise — Noisy-simulator and actual-device inference show that hardware noise affects results; full training on real QPUs was not demonstrated, and hardware-induced degradation is a practical limitation.
- #limitation:data-encoding — Uses angle/rotation (Hadamard + Ry(arctan(x)) + Rz(arctan(x^2))) encoding which may limit fidelity and requires cost in circuit depth as input dimensionality grows.
- #limitation:no-empirical-validation — While inference was run on real hardware, the main training was performed on simulators (no full training on QPU), so end-to-end empirical validation on hardware is incomplete.
- #limitation:simulation-only — Key training experiments rely on classical statevector/noisy simulators; reported quantum advantage is on small-scale data and may not generalize to larger real-world datasets without further hardware advances.
- #idea:hybrid-approach — Code and model details are publicly available (GitHub), aiding reproducibility; dataset is small and can be reconstructed from public market data.
## Contradictions
- The paper reports simulator-based improvements of QLSTM over classical LSTM but actual-device experiments perform substantially worse than both the noisy simulator and classical baseline, undermining claims of practical quantum superiority on current hardware.
- Authors assert an aggregate claim of 'approximately 10% improvement in accuracy and ~50% reduction in average RMSE' compared to classical models, which is inconsistent with the detailed table values provided (e.g., simulator RMSE improvements are smaller and hardware results contradict the aggregate claim).
- Increasing the number of qubits did not consistently improve performance and sometimes degraded it; this contradicts informal expectations that larger quantum circuits/capacity will straightforwardly yield better predictive performance and raises scalability concerns.
- The manuscript notes that certain VQC instances may be classically simulable in polynomial time, which contradicts any implicit claim that the observed simulator benefits imply a fundamental, hard-to-classically-reproduce quantum advantage.
