## Abstract summary
This preprint develops a hybrid quantum-classical pipeline that uses Quantum Amplitude Estimation (QAE) to price multi-asset basket options using real historical market data. It systematically studies the trade-offs between the number of uncertainty qubits per asset and basket dimensionality, benchmarking QAE against classical Monte Carlo and Black–Scholes methods, and identifies a near-term sweet spot of about 3–4 uncertainty qubits per asset while highlighting limitations in state preparation, correlation modeling, and hardware resource demands.
## Methodology
The authors implement a hybrid quantum-classical pipeline to price multi-asset basket options using real historical market data. They retrieve daily adjusted close prices via yfinance for a specified interval (Jan 1–Jun 30, 2024), compute log-returns, estimate per-asset mean and standard deviation, scale these parameters to the option maturity T (µT = µ·T, σT = σ·√T), and add a small positive shift to the mean (≈+5%). Each asset's log-normal distribution is truncated to ±3σ around the scaled mean and discretized into 2^n grid points where n is the number of uncertainty qubits allocated to that asset. Individual asset PDFs are evaluated and normalized; assuming independence, the joint distribution is formed by the product of per-asset probabilities. Basket prices (weighted sums) are computed at all grid points and binned into a 1D price grid for encoding. On the quantum side, the discretized distribution is loaded into uncertainty qubits using a LogNormalDistribution state-preparation primitive (amplitudes = sqrt(probabilities)), the weighted sum is computed with a reversible integer adder (WeightedAdder2), the strike K is mapped to the integer domain, and a piecewise-linear payoff approximation is embedded using LinearAmplitudeFunction via controlled rotations into an objective qubit. The state-preparation + payoff operator is packaged into an EstimationProblem and quantum amplitude estimation (QAE, with references to iterative/IAE variants) is applied to estimate the expected payoff (they focus on final basket price at maturity). Results are post-processed back to continuous price units and compared against classical baselines: classical binned summation (using the same discretized distribution), Monte Carlo simulation (M ≈ 10,000), and Black–Scholes approximations. A parametric study varies (a) uncertainty qubits per asset (1–8) for fixed basket sizes and (b) number of assets (3–9) for fixed qubit counts, reporting final-basket-price estimates and percentage deviations versus baselines.

**Algorithms used:** Quantum Amplitude Estimation (QAE), Iterative Amplitude Estimation (IAE) (discussed/alternatives), Amplitude amplification / Grover-like operator, LogNormalDistribution state preparation, WeightedAdder (reversible integer addition), LinearAmplitudeFunction (piecewise-linear payoff encoding), Classical Monte Carlo simulation, Black–Scholes analytic formula, Classical binned summation (on discretized grid)
**Frameworks:** Qiskit (Qiskit Finance primitives such as LogNormalDistribution, WeightedAdder, LinearAmplitudeFunction, EstimationProblem), yfinance (for historical data retrieval)

**Dataset:** Daily Adjusted Close prices for selected equities (examples: AAPL, GOOG, MSFT, NVDA, AMZN, TSLA, V, JNJ, XOM) from January 1 to June 30, 2024, obtained via yfinance. The authors compute log returns, per-asset mean and standard deviation, and scale parameters for the option maturity T. Datasets are subset by chosen basket combinations (3–9 assets).

## Findings
- [supported] The authors implemented a hybrid quantum-classical pipeline that integrates real historical market data (Jan–Jun 2024) to prepare discretized log-normal asset-price distributions and apply Quantum Amplitude Estimation (QAE) for multi-asset basket pricing, benchmarked against classical Monte Carlo (MC) and Black–Scholes (BS).
- [supported] Empirical results show that with very few uncertainty qubits per asset (1–2 qubits) QAE discretization is too coarse and produces large under- or over-estimates of final basket prices across tested baskets (3–9 assets).
- [supported] Empirical results indicate a pragmatic near-term 'sweet spot' of about 3–4 uncertainty qubits per asset: for small baskets (3–4 assets) this yields QAE estimates closely matching MC (deviations on the order of ~0.7% or less in reported cases).
- [supported] For larger baskets (6–9 assets) the QAE estimates remain sensitive to discretization: even with increased qubits QAE sometimes converges to values that overshoot MC estimates, indicating diminishing accuracy gains and heightened sensitivity to state-preparation and correlation modeling limitations.
- [supported] Increasing uncertainty-qubit count improves accuracy up to a point; beyond roughly 4 qubits per asset the accuracy gains in the reported experiments taper off while quantum resource (qubit count and circuit depth) requirements grow rapidly.
- [supported] The paper reports concrete numerical examples (per-basket final-price estimates and deviation percentages) showing the trends above across 3–9 asset baskets.
- [speculative] The paper reiterates the theoretical expectation from prior work that QAE can provide a quadratic sampling-speedup over classical Monte Carlo (O(ε^{-1}) vs O(ε^{-2})), but does not empirically demonstrate a runtime or wall-clock advantage on quantum hardware in this work.
- [speculative] The authors claim that improved state-preparation techniques and better correlation modeling would reduce discrepancies for larger baskets and make QAE more reliable in high-dimensional settings.
- [speculative] The authors anticipate that future advances in quantum hardware, circuit design, and error mitigation will further narrow the gap between quantum-based and classical pricing for high-dimensional financial problems.
- [supported] The paper acknowledges and operates under independence assumptions for the asset distributions (covariance modeled as σ^2 I) and states that correlation modeling is left for more complex state-preparation strategies.

**Results summary:** The preprint implements a hybrid quantum-classical workflow using real historical stock data to discretize asset price distributions and apply Quantum Amplitude Estimation (QAE) for basket pricing. Empirical simulations show that QAE with very few uncertainty qubits (1–2) yields large errors, while allocating about 3–4 qubits per asset typically produces estimates closely matching classical Monte Carlo for small baskets (3–4 assets). For larger baskets (6–9 assets), QAE estimates become more sensitive to discretization and can overshoot MC values; accuracy gains diminish beyond ~4 qubits per asset as circuit complexity grows. The work reiterates the theoretical quadratic sampling advantage of QAE but does not demonstrate a practical runtime advantage on hardware, and it highlights that better state preparation and correlation modeling are needed for high-dimensional baskets.

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** theoretical

The paper cites the standard theoretical QAE result (quadratic sampling speedup over classical Monte Carlo) but does not demonstrate an empirical runtime or resource advantage on real quantum hardware; the experiments focus on estimation accuracy, discretization effects, and qubit-resource trade-offs in simulation/benchmarks rather than on demonstrating a realized quantum computational speedup.
## Limitations
- Assumption of asset independence in the implemented state-preparation (explicitly stated); correlations are not modeled in the current implementation.
- Use of truncated and discretized log-normal distributions (grid-based discretization) introduces approximation error and loses continuous-tail fidelity.
- Piecewise-linear approximation of payoff via LinearAmplitudeFunction introduces approximation/scaling errors in payoff encoding.
- Dependence on classical pre-processing parameters and heuristics (e.g., adding a +5% shift to mean returns) that may bias results.
- Mapping continuous prices and strike values into integer domains (affine mapping and Kmapped) introduces quantization and rounding errors.
- Results evaluated on final basket price rather than full option payoff dynamics (authors treat final price as sufficient approximation), which may omit details relevant for option valuation dynamics.
- Empirical evaluation limited to historical data from a single interval (Jan 1 – Jun 30, 2024), reducing assessment of robustness across market regimes. [inferred]
- Experiments are limited to baskets up to nine assets; scalability to larger baskets is not demonstrated and may be infeasible with current resources.
- QAE sensitivity to number of uncertainty qubits — coarse discretization (1–2 qubits) causes severe under/overshooting; increasing qubits increases circuit depth rapidly and may be impractical on NISQ hardware.
- Diminishing returns in accuracy beyond ~3–4 uncertainty qubits per asset while circuit complexity grows exponentially.
- State-preparation and distribution-loading overheads are non-trivial and may negate theoretical QAE runtime advantages in practice.
- No demonstration on real quantum hardware; results appear to be simulator-based and do not account for noise, decoherence, or gate errors.
- Simplifying modelling assumptions (log-normal dynamics, constant volatility scaling) may fail to capture realistic market features such as stochastic volatility, jumps, or volatility clustering. [inferred]
- Use of classical benchmarks (Monte Carlo and Black–Scholes) as ground truth has limitations: Black–Scholes is known to be inappropriate for multi-asset correlated baskets and MC itself depends on model assumptions and sample sizes.
- The paper focuses primarily on final basket price rather than a fully end-to-end option pricing pipeline that includes discounting, Greeks, or risk measures.
- Weighted adder and integer-only arithmetic in the circuit can cause loss of precision in representing weighted sums of discretized asset prices.
- Potential overfitting of discretization choices (bounds, number of bins) to the tested baskets; generalization to different asset universes or maturities is unclear. [inferred]
- No systematic exploration of different QAE variants (e.g., iterative AE, maximum-likelihood AE) and their relative robustness in the hybrid workflow. [inferred]
- No discussion or integration of practical error mitigation techniques, which are critical for NISQ execution. (Authors mention error mitigation as anticipated future need but do not implement it.)

## Contradictions
