## Abstract summary
This community white paper reviews the state of quantum optimization, surveying complexity-theoretic perspectives, core quantum algorithmic paradigms, and problem classes (discrete, continuous, mixed-integer, dynamic, control, and robust/multi-objective). It analyzes scaling and noise challenges for executing algorithms on near-term hardware, proposes benchmarking principles and concrete benchmark problems and metrics, and highlights illustrative industrial use cases in finance and sustainable energy to guide future research and validation of practical quantum advantage.
## Methodology
This work is a multi-author, survey-style synthesis and roadmap produced by a cross-disciplinary working group. The authors systematically review theoretical foundations (complexity theory and worst-case vs. instance-based analyses), algorithmic paradigms (exact, approximate, and heuristic quantum approaches), and problem classes (discrete, continuous, mixed-integer, dynamic programming, robust and multi-objective optimization). They compile and summarize prior results and constructions from the literature, identify core algorithmic building blocks, examine practical execution issues on noisy hardware (software stack, transpilation, pulse-level control, and error mitigation), and propose benchmarking goals, design principles, metrics and candidate problems (including application case studies in finance and sustainable energy). The methodology therefore consists of literature review, synthesis across subfields, theoretical exposition, taxonomy construction, identification of open questions, and the proposal of concrete benchmarking designs and metrics rather than new empirical experiments or original numerical simulations.

**Algorithms used:** Grover Search, Quantum Adiabatic Algorithm (QAA)/Quantum Annealing, Quantum Phase Estimation (QPE), Quantum Imaginary Time Evolution (QITE), Quantum Gibbs Sampling, Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA), Quantum Alternating Operator Ansatz (generalized QAOA), Variational Quantum Algorithms (VQAs) / VQE, Quantum Random Access Optimization (QRAO), Quantum SDP solvers (MWU-based and others), Quantum Interior-Point Methods (QIPM), Quantum Hamiltonian Descent / Quantum Central Path Method, Quantum-enhanced Markov Chain Monte Carlo / Quantum walks, Quantum Branch-and-Bound variants, Quantum dynamic programming (Grover-assisted hybrid DP), Quantum linear-system algorithms (HHL and variants), Quantum Singular Value Transformation / Quantum Signal Processing, Counter-diabatic / Shortcuts to adiabaticity techniques, Quantum Gibbs-state-based matrix multiplicative weights
**Frameworks:** OpenQASM, Quil, Blackbird, Qiskit, QRAM (input model discussed), MIPLIB / QPLIB (benchmark libraries referenced)

## Findings
- [speculative] Complexity-theoretic analysis indicates exponential quantum speedups for NP-hard optimization problems are unlikely in the worst case; the most general unstructured search speedup is quadratic (Grover).
- [speculative] Quantum algorithms can provide substantial (even exponential) speedups for structured or non-worst-case instances (e.g., factoring with Shor); thus complexity separations are useful but not sufficient/necessary to predict practical advantage.
- [speculative] Quantum Adiabatic Algorithms (QAA) require run times that scale inversely with the square of the minimum spectral gap; for glassy problems the gap can close exponentially, implying exponential runtime for exact adiabatic evolution.
- [speculative] Quantum Phase Estimation and related linear-algebraic quantum subroutines (block-encodings, QSP, QSVE, QSVT) provide potentially powerful building blocks for optimization (e.g., SDPs, Gibbs sampling) but rely on strong input models (QRAM, good oracles).
- [supported] Variational approaches (VQE, QAOA and variants) are the main near-term heuristic strategies; they are useful empirical heuristics but face serious scaling obstacles (e.g., barren plateaus, optimization noise) on current noisy hardware.
- [supported] Barren plateaus and other trainability issues have been empirically observed and theoretically linked to ansatz expressibility, entanglement, and the dynamical Lie algebra; these are core bottlenecks for VQAs.
- [speculative] QAOA has provable approximation guarantees in specific settings: e.g., MAXCUT p=1 lower bound ~0.692; for low p there are analytical bounds (p=2 ~0.7559, p=3 ~0.7924) for certain graph families, but these do not imply general superiority over classical algorithms.
- [speculative] Quantum SDP solvers and multiplicative-weights-based approaches can give asymptotic speedups in some parameter regimes (dependence improvements in dimension, sparsity), but their end-to-end advantage is limited by large dependence on precision and by strong assumptions (QRAM, block-encodings).
- [speculative] Quantum Gibbs sampling and preparing thermal states is generally hard (related to QMA/#P/PP complexity), though variational, dissipative, and specially tailored quantum algorithms may make practical progress for restricted instances.
- [supported] Error suppression and mitigation techniques (Dynamical Decoupling, ZNE, PEC, probabilistic ZNE/PEA) extend what noisy devices can do, but mitigation costs typically grow rapidly (e.g., variance amplification γ^2), limiting scalability without fault tolerance.
- [supported] Hardware- and problem-aware compilation (qubit selection, SWAP networks, hardware-tailored ansätze, mid-circuit measurement) materially improve empirical performance on superconducting (and other) platforms; experiments demonstrate benefit.
- [supported] Experimental, near-term demonstrations have executed QAOA/VQE-style algorithms on devices up to hundreds of qubits (the paper surveys many works); however the achieved approximation ratios and solution qualities remain modest and carefully problem-dependent.
- [speculative] Benchmarks for quantum optimization must be problem-class aware and should report resource cost, run time, solution quality, and problem complexity; the paper proposes design principles and specific problem families (QUBO/MIS/knapsack/LABS/QAP/timetabling/etc.) for benchmarking.
- [speculative] Mixed-integer programming (MIP) may benefit from hybrid quantum-classical approaches (quantum subroutines inside branch-and-bound, ADMM, Lagrangian relaxations), with theoretical potential for speedups, but many open questions remain about end-to-end gains and what quantum subroutines must deliver.
- [speculative] Dynamic programming and certain exponential-time DP algorithms admit quantum speedups (often quadratic) by combining DP with Grover-like subroutines; provable improvements exist for specific problems but general limits persist.
- [speculative] Two application domains with rich optimization structure — finance and sustainable energy (e-mobility, grid optimization) — offer promising testbeds for quantum optimization research, but practical impact is still uncertain and requires progression through model realism, uncertainty, and scale.
- [supported] Practical near-term quantum optimization progress will depend crucially on co-design across modeling, algorithm choice, encoding, transpilation, pulse-level control, and error mitigation; the paper documents concrete best-practice recommendations and references empirical studies.

**Results summary:** This comprehensive review synthesizes theoretical and empirical perspectives on quantum optimization. From complexity theory it clarifies that worst-case exponential speedups for generic NP-hard optimization are unlikely, but structured instances and non-worst-case regimes remain open to quantum advantage. The paper catalogs core quantum algorithmic paradigms (Grover, QAA, QPE, Gibbs sampling, QAOA, VQE, quantum SDP/IPM approaches), highlights practical bottlenecks (spectral gap for adiabatic methods, barren plateaus and trainability for variational methods, input/output and precision costs for quantum SDP solvers), and surveys contemporary hardware and experimental work. It emphasizes the central role of error mitigation, hardware-aware compilation, and benchmarking, and proposes concrete benchmark problems and metrics. Finance and sustainable-energy problems are discussed as illustrative domains with layered formulations of increasing realism. Overall, the review concludes that a practical, demonstrable quantum advantage in optimization remains speculative at present but motivates focused empirical benchmarking and algorithmic co-design.

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** speculative

The review concludes that while rigorous complexity-theoretic proofs make worst-case exponential quantum speedups for generic NP-hard optimization unlikely, there remain theoretical possibilities and empirical hope for meaningful (polynomial to superpolynomial) advantages on structured, realistic instances or via new heuristics. However, to date no general, practical quantum advantage for broad optimization tasks has been demonstrated; progress is speculative and contingent on algorithmic, hardware, and benchmark advances.
## Limitations
- Quantum advantage for NP-hard problems is generally unlikely; at best only quadratic speedups (e.g., Grover) are achievable in unstructured worst-case settings.
- Complexity-theoretic separations are neither necessary nor sufficient to predict practical quantum advantage for optimization.
- Many promising quantum algorithms (Grover-based methods, QPE, QAA/QA, QITE, certain SDP solvers) require fault-tolerant quantum computing (FTQC) and are therefore not near-term practical.
- Noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) devices have limited coherence times, limited circuit depth, limited qubit counts, and sparse connectivity, which constrain algorithm scale and fidelity.
- Variational algorithms (VQAs/QAOA) suffer from training issues including barren plateaus and parameter optimization difficulties; landscape analysis is incomplete.
- QAOA has provable depth/structure limitations (needs growing depth for certain instances); parameter transfer/concentration is instance- and problem-dependent.
- Encoding constraints into QUBO as large penalties often produces dense matrices and numerical issues; penalty encodings may only approximate constraints and can break solution quality.
- Quantum Gibbs sampling and preparation of thermal states is challenging and in many cases QMA-hard; efficient, scalable quantum Gibbs methods are not established.
- Quantum SDP solvers and other quantum linear-algebraic routines often rely on QRAM or compact functional input models; QRAM is not yet practical and input/output bottlenecks can eliminate asymptotic advantages.
- Quantum linear system and IPM proposals incur dependence on condition numbers and require tomography/state readout overheads, which limit practical speedups.
- Quantum acceleration of dynamic programming and related exponential-time classical algorithms is limited (often quadratic speedups at best) and typically applies to specific DP-table structures (e.g., hypercube); general applicability is unclear.
- Mixed-integer programming (MIP) hybrid approaches lack general provable end-to-end speedups; it is unclear how much quantum subroutines must help to produce net practical gains for MIP.
- Most near-term experimental demonstrations are capability showcases on contrived or small instances, not evidence of real-world advantage; noiseless simulation differs markedly from hardware results.
- Error suppression and mitigation methods (PEC/ZNE/PEA) can have prohibitive sampling/variance costs (e.g., PEC variance scaling ~γ^2, where γ grows with depth and system size), making mitigation expensive for large circuits.
- Transpilation, routing (SWAP insertion), and hardware mapping add substantial overhead and depth on sparse-connectivity devices; hardware/compiler stack is a major bottleneck.
- Benchmarking practices are fragmented: there is no standardized cross-platform benchmark suite for quantum optimization, making fair comparisons difficult.
- [inferred] Practical adoption is further limited by industrial constraints such as proprietary/limited access to real-world datasets, regulatory/privacy issues, and the difficulty of publishing hard industrial instances.
- [inferred] For many real-world applications (finance, energy), model complexity, uncertainty, and requirement for certified bounds (a posteriori guarantees) create extra barriers that are not yet met by quantum methods.
- [inferred] Cost-to-solution, energy-use, and monetary cost comparisons between classical and quantum approaches are not yet established or are hard to compute fairly, limiting claims about practical value.
## Open questions
- Can quantum algorithms saturate inapproximability bounds for optimization problems where classical algorithms do not yet reach those bounds?
- Are there practically relevant subclasses or structured instances of NP/NPO-hard problems for which quantum algorithms yield super-polynomial or otherwise meaningful speedups?
- What are the best strategies to design ansätze that avoid barren plateaus while remaining expressive enough for useful variational performance?
- Which problem constraints can (and should) be handled natively in quantum algorithms (QUBO-Plus) to avoid dense penalty encodings, and how to do so efficiently?
- How can quantum Gibbs sampling be made practically efficient and scalable for optimization subroutines that require thermal sampling or partition-function estimates?
- Can quantum SDP solvers be made end-to-end advantageous given realistic input/output models and without impractical reliance on QRAM?
- How to eliminate or mitigate the dependence on condition numbers and tomography overhead in quantum interior-point or linear-system based optimization methods?
- What structures of dynamic programming state spaces beyond Boolean hypercubes admit nontrivial quantum speedups? Can quantum DP be extended to more general DP formulations and MDPs?
- What are the fundamental lower bounds for quantum algorithms in polynomial and non-convex optimization settings, and what classes allow useful quantum speedups?
- Can quantum heuristic approaches be systematically designed to exploit structure in practical instances (finance, energy) rather than contrived oracle-based separations?
- How to design quantum-native pre-processing and kernelization methods that complement quantum solvers and preserve meaningful a posteriori bounds?
- What is the role and practical viability of QRAM-like input models; are there alternative input encodings that enable quantum speedups without QRAM?
- How can error mitigation be adapted to produce noise-mitigated samples (not only expectation values), which is essential for optimization heuristics that require sampling?
- Which benchmarks (problems, instance distributions, metrics) provide fair, reproducible assessments of quantum vs classical optimization for real-world applications?
- What hybrid algorithmic architectures (e.g., branch-and-bound with quantum subroutines, ADMM with QUBO solvers) yield provable end-to-end gains, and under which resource assumptions?
- For mixed-integer programming: is there a meta-theorem bounding the possible speedups from combining quantum convex-relaxation solvers with quantum-accelerated tree search?
- How to best exploit denser encodings (more classical variables per qubit) such as QRACs in practice given the tradeoffs in readout fidelity and approximation quality?
- What are problem classes in sustainability and finance (under realistic data assumptions) that remain classically hard at sizes appropriate for near-term quantum hardware and could reveal practical advantages?

**Future work:**
- Develop and analyze quantum approximation algorithms and heuristics (including VQAs/QAOA variants) targeted at structured, practical instances rather than worst-case or oracle separations.
- Design ansatz and training strategies that mitigate barren plateaus and scale to larger problem sizes (e.g., transfer of parameters, initialization heuristics, adaptive/mixer designs, restarting strategies).
- Advance quantum Gibbs-state preparation and sampling techniques and investigate their application in optimization (e.g., simulated annealing, SDP relaxations, trace estimations).
- Improve quantum SDP and interior-point methods to reduce dependence on precision, condition numbers and QRAM, and to better model realistic input/output constraints.
- Study hybrid classical–quantum algorithms for mixed-integer programming: branch-and-bound with quantum-accelerated relaxations, ADMM/decomposition approaches, Lagrangian relaxations, moment/SOS reformulations, and copositive formulations.
- Investigate quantum dynamic programming and quantum algorithms for MDPs and reinforcement learning, including practical algorithms for classical-access environments.
- Develop quantum-aware modeling and problem-encoding techniques that trade off model fidelity against implementability on noisy hardware (hardware-tailored problem formulations, QUBO-Plus).
- Advance error mitigation methods that are specifically tailored for optimization tasks and sampling (reducing variance growth, probabilistic ZNE, sample-based mitigation), and study their scalability.
- Develop standardized, reproducible benchmark suites and metrics for quantum optimization (including resource/cost accounting, run-time, solution quality, feasibility, diversity) and implement cross-platform evaluation frameworks.
- Explore denser encodings (e.g., QRACs, variable-to-qubit mappings, mid-circuit measurement reuse) to increase the variable-to-qubit ratio while managing readout and approximation tradeoffs.
- Build and test quantum-accelerated pipelines for concrete real-world domains, with staged complexity levels (e.g., finance: mean-variance → robust → multi-period/stochastic; energy: scheduling → control → grid integration).
- Improve transpilation, routing and pulse-level compilation techniques to produce shallower, hardware-tailored circuits for QAOA/VQE and other optimization ansätze.
- Study cross-platform comparisons (superconducting, trapped ions, analog annealers, digital-analog simulators) to identify architectures best suited for particular optimization problem classes.
- Research how to include pre- and post-processing, calibration, and mitigation costs in holistic time-to-solution and cost-to-solution benchmarks for fair comparison with classical methods.
