## Abstract summary
This review surveys how quantum computing can be applied to financial problems, covering quantum optimization (notably quantum annealing for portfolio optimization, arbitrage detection, and feature selection in credit scoring), quantum machine learning approaches for prediction and risk modeling, and quantum amplitude estimation to accelerate Monte Carlo methods. It highlights potential algorithmic speedups and near-term prospects while noting practical hardware challenges and the distinction between NISQ-era applications and longer-term fault-tolerant quantum computing.
## Methodology
This is a conceptual, literature-review style study that surveys existing research at the intersection of quantum computing and quantitative finance. The authors synthesize prior theoretical results, algorithmic proposals and proof-of-principle experiments from the literature (including mapping classical finance problems to quantum formulations, descriptions of quantum algorithms and summaries of small-scale implementations). Their approach is to: (1) classify core financial problem classes (optimization, machine learning, Monte Carlo sampling), (2) map each class to relevant quantum algorithmic paradigms (e.g. adiabatic quantum computation / quantum annealing for combinatorial optimization; HHL, QFT, quantum PCA and quantum SVM for linear-algebra based ML; quantum amplitude amplification/estimation and Montanaro’s methods for Monte Carlo), (3) discuss representative case studies drawn from the literature (optimal trading trajectory, arbitrage detection, feature selection for credit scoring), (4) summarize algorithmic speedups and limitations, and (5) discuss hardware platforms, current experimental demonstrations and outstanding challenges. The paper does not present novel primary experimental data; rather it synthesizes and interprets published algorithms, proofs-of-principle and small-scale experiments to provide an overview and perspective.

**Algorithms used:** Quantum Annealing (Adiabatic Quantum Computation), Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA), Grover's algorithm / Quantum Amplitude Amplification, Quantum Amplitude Estimation (QAE), Harrow-Hassidim-Lloyd (HHL) linear systems algorithm, Quantum Fourier Transform (QFT), Quantum Principal Component Analysis (qPCA), Quantum Support Vector Machine (Quantum SVM), Montanaro's quantum Monte Carlo speedup, Swap test (state overlap estimation), Quantum Boltzmann Machine, Quantum Perceptron models, Quantum Hidden Markov Models, Quantum Reinforcement Learning
**Frameworks:** QuTiP (quantum dynamics simulation library), 1QBit SDK, IBM Q Experience

## Findings

- [supported] Quantum annealers (D-Wave family) have been used in proof-of-principle experiments for small instances of finance problems: dynamic portfolio (optimal trading trajectory) and arbitrage detection.
- [supported] Small-scale implementations/prototypes exist for: optimal trading trajectory on D-Wave chips (512 and 1152 qubits), optimal arbitrage on D-Wave 2X (small five-asset example), and a feature-selection formulation for credit scoring using a 1QBit SDK / quantum-annealer-compatible QUBO.
- [theoretical] Quantum optimization paradigms (adiabatic quantum computation / quantum annealing, QAOA) map many financial optimization problems to ground-state or QUBO formulations and could in principle improve solution quality or runtime.
- [theoretical] Quantum machine learning subroutines (HHL linear-system solver, quantum PCA, quantum SVM, quantum Boltzmann training, amplitude estimation) can provide polynomial or exponential asymptotic speedups under specific technical assumptions (e.g., sparse matrices, eﬃcient state preparation / qRAM, favorable condition numbers).
- [supported] Some small experimental demonstrations of QML primitives exist (e.g., a quantum SVM demonstration cited; Boltzmann-machine-related training experiments on D-Wave are reported).
- [theoretical] Quantum Amplitude Estimation (QAE) and related techniques give a quadratic reduction in Monte Carlo sample complexity for expectation estimation (used in pricing derivatives and risk metrics like VaR/CVaR).
- [supported] Small proof-of-concept implementations of quantum-accelerated risk estimation (VaR/CVaR) and derivative-pricing algorithms have been prototyped (including small tests on IBM Q Experience noted in the literature).
- [supported] Important, practical hardware and systems challenges remain: decoherence, need for error correction (potentially thousands of physical qubits per logical qubit), absence of scalable qRAM, and limits on circuit depth (NISQ era constraints).
- [speculative] No general, large-scale, practical quantum advantage for mainstream financial workloads has yet been demonstrated; near-term benefit is expected most likely in specialized/limited-size applications (NISQ/annealer use-cases).
- [speculative] QAOA and related NISQ algorithms are promising candidates for producing useful approximate solutions to combinatorial financial optimizations but their practical, general advantage over classical heuristics is not established.
- [theoretical] Many claimed algorithmic speedups carry caveats: readout/state-tomography overhead, state preparation costs, sparsity/condition-number assumptions, and classical pre/post-processing can nullify asymptotic advantages in practice.

**Results summary:** The paper surveys ways quantum computing could impact finance. It reviews mapping financial optimization problems to QUBO and adiabatic/annealing frameworks and documents small proof-of-principle uses of D-Wave processors for portfolio trajectory optimization, arbitrage detection, and feature selection for credit scoring. ## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** theoretical

The paper presents well-known, provable algorithmic speedups (Grover-type quadratic, HHL/QFT exponential under assumptions, QAE quadratic for Monte Carlo) and reports small proof-of-principle experiments on annealers and limited gate devices. However, large-scale, practically demonstrated advantage for real-world financial workloads is not shown; advantages depend on strong assumptions (sparse matrices, efficient state preparation/qRAM, manageable readout costs) and significant hardware progress (error correction, deeper circuits). Hence the advantage is characterized as theoretical at this stage.
## Limitations
- Decoherence: uncontrolled interactions with the environment limit circuit depth and number of operations.
- High overhead for quantum error correction: fault-tolerant qubits may require many thousands of physical qubits.
- Practical speedups can vanish when classical processing required for error correction is included.
- Absence of a practical qRAM: we do not currently have a qRAM capable of efficiently encoding and storing large classical datasets as quantum states.
- Many QML algorithms require a universal, fault-tolerant quantum computer which is not yet available.
- NISQ devices are noisy and limited; there is a lack of mature algorithm libraries tailored for NISQ processors.
- Adiabatic/quantum annealing implementations are approximate in practice and may not satisfy ideal adiabatic conditions.
- Quantum state preparation and readout (including tomography) can be costly and may negate theoretical algorithmic speedups.
- Some quantum algorithms assume sparse matrices or special data structures (e.g., for HHL), limiting applicability to general datasets.
- Existing experimental demonstrations are small-scale (proofs-of-principle) and do not yet show end-to-end superiority over classical methods at industry scale.
- Mapping real-world financial problems into suitable quantum-friendly forms (e.g., QUBO) may be nontrivial and scale-limited.
- Model risk: even if quantum algorithms achieve computational speedups, underlying financial models may still make erroneous predictions in qualitatively new situations (e.g., 2008 crisis).
- Unclear end-to-end advantage: it is not yet established how quantum speedups persist when including data loading, preprocessing, post-processing, and classical-quantum integration.
- [inferred] State fragility and storage: quantum-encoded models or parameters may be difficult to store and maintain over time for production use.
- [inferred] Economic feasibility: near-term quantum hardware and its operational costs may limit practical deployment in finance.
- [inferred] Regulatory and interpretability concerns: quantum-based models (especially QML) may be difficult to interpret and thus harder to satisfy financial regulation and auditability requirements.
- [inferred] Robustness to non-stationarity: financial data are non-stationary and regime-shifting; it is unclear how quantum algorithms will cope with rapidly changing distributions.
- [inferred] Security and privacy considerations for moving sensitive financial data onto quantum devices remain unresolved.
## Open questions
- Can quantum computing predict qualitatively new and rare market events (e.g., financial crises) that classical models fail to anticipate?
- When will universal, fault-tolerant quantum computers capable of supporting the proposed QML and linear-algebra algorithms become available for practical financial use?
- How can error-correction schemes be developed with significantly more reasonable (lower) resource requirements?
- How can practical, scalable qRAM be designed and implemented to allow efficient loading of large classical datasets into quantum states?
- Which financial problems will see the earliest and clearest practical quantum advantage (optimization, Monte Carlo, ML, other)?
- How can we design NISQ algorithms and an algorithm library specifically tailored to near-term quantum devices for finance?
- To what extent do quantum annealers (approximate adiabatic implementations) reliably find near-optimal solutions for large, realistic financial optimization problems?
- How does the full-system (end-to-end) runtime and accuracy compare to state-of-the-art classical methods once state preparation, readout, and classical integration are accounted for?
- How to mitigate the cost of state tomography and readout for algorithms that encode results in quantum amplitudes?
- How can quantum models be made interpretable, auditable, and compliant with financial regulations?
- How to incorporate model risk and regime-change robustness into quantum algorithm design and evaluation?
- What are the best strategies to hybridize quantum and classical resources for practical financial workflows?
- How will advances in quantum cryptography and quantum computing affect financial security, digital assets, and blockchain systems?

**Future work:**
