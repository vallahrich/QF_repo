## Abstract summary
This dissertation demonstrates a discrete-time quantum walk implemented in momentum space using an ultra-cold 87Rb Bose–Einstein condensate. Each step combines a microwave 'coin' creating internal-state superpositions and a resonant quantum ratchet (derived from the atom-optics kicked rotor) as the unitary shift, enabling observation of quantum-walk features, biasing and reversal, and the quantum-to-classical transition; the approach is robust, extendable to higher dimensions, and amenable to many-body extensions.
## Methodology
The study implements a discrete-time quantum walk in momentum space using an ultra-cold 87Rb Bose–Einstein condensate (BEC). The experimental methodology comprises: (1) preparation of a 87Rb BEC via a standard laser cooling pipeline (MOT, optical molasses, loading into an optical dipole trap and evaporative cooling) on a custom optical/vacuum apparatus; (2) preparation of a chosen initial internal and momentum-state superposition (using Bragg pulses to set initial momentum classes and microwave (MW) pulses to prepare internal hyperfine superpositions); (3) realization of the walk step as a sequence ˆU_step = ˆT ˆM where ˆM is a MW pulse (typically π/2 rotations, phase-controlled) acting as the coin and ˆT is a state-dependent momentum shift implemented by a resonant atom-optics kicked-rotor (AOKR) ratchet (a pulsed near-resonant standing-wave optical lattice). The standing-wave pulses are timed at quantum-resonance conditions so that the ratchet induces momentum currents of opposite sign for the two hyperfine states, thereby entangling internal and external degrees of freedom. The coin + ratchet sequence is repeated for j steps (up to O(20) in this apparatus) with controlled parameters: kick strength |k| (experimentally varied 1.2–1.8), MW phase and rotation angle (allowing biased coins and phase noise), initial quasimomentum width and thermal fraction. After the sequence the trap is switched off, atoms expand in time-of-flight, are repumped/imaged on resonance and the resulting momentum distributions (CCD images) are recorded. The experiment explores standard symmetric walks, steered (biased) walks via biased coin or ratchet strengths, walk reversal by applying inverse operators, and the quantum-to-classical transition by adding controlled phase noise to the MW coin. Experimental results are compared to numerical simulations of the unitary dynamics including realistic effects (quasimomentum spread, thermal fraction, spontaneous emission). Observables include momentum-resolved populations, mean momentum, and mean energy growth versus step number.

**Experimental setup:** Cold-atom laboratory apparatus: 87Rb MOT and optical molasses for pre-cooling; polarized repump and cooling laser systems (near 780 nm D2 line) delivered by fiber links; optical dipole trap using a CO2 laser for evaporative cooling to BEC; vacuum chamber with anti-Helmholtz coils; Bragg beams for initial momentum-state preparation; pulsed standing-wave (near-resonant) optical lattice generated with AOM-controlled beams to implement the AOKR ratchet (kick strengths |k| ≈ 1.2–1.8), timing set to quantum resonance; microwave source and delivery (≈6.8 GHz) to drive hyperfine ground-state transitions (F=1 ↔ F=2) with phase- and pulse-area control (π/2, biased rotations); time-of-flight expansion (~10 ms) and resonant imaging (repump then probe) onto CCD camera for momentum distribution readout. Typical walk repetition: up to ~20 steps (practically limited by detection window); coin/ratchet timing sequence synchronized on pulse generator.
## Experiment details
### Input
Laboratory-prepared 87Rb BEC: ~10^5 atoms in optical dipole trap, initial momentum-state prepared via Bragg pulses (superpositions of momentum classes, e.g., |n=0> and |n=1> or multi-component initial states of up to 7 momentum classes), internal-state superposition prepared by microwave (π/2) pulses (1/√2(|1>+|2>) or biased compositions). Key sample characteristics varied: quasimomentum width δβ_FWHM ≈ 0.025–0.04 ℏG, thermal fraction contributions up to ≈15%, spontaneous-emission probability per kick ~0.5% (in some simulations). Source: in-house experimental preparation; no external dataset.

### Process
Pipeline: (1) Create and characterize 87Rb BEC (MOT → optical molasses → CO2 dipole trap → evaporative cooling). (2) Prepare desired initial momentum superposition with Bragg pulses and internal superposition with MW pulse. (3) For each walk step j: apply MW coin pulse ˆM (typically π/2 rotation, phase-controlled; can be biased ρ≠0.5 or have randomized phase for noise), then apply pulsed standing-wave ratchet ˆT (AOKR pulse) at quantum resonance to impart state-dependent momentum shift; repeat for j steps. (4) Optionally apply steering: change MW coin composition (biased coin) or use different ratchet strengths for each internal state (biased ratchet). (5) For reversal experiments apply a sequence of inverse operations to recover initial state. (6) For quantum-to-classical transition add controlled random phase noise to coin pulses (percent-level up to uniform 2π distribution). (7) Release atoms, allow time-of-flight expansion (~10 ms), apply repump pulse then probe pulse and image atomic momentum distribution. (8) Analyze images to extract momentum-resolved populations, compute mean momentum and mean energy vs step number; compare to numerical simulations that include experimental imperfections (quasimomentum spread, thermal component, spontaneous emission). Parameters varied across runs: number of steps (up to ~20), |k| (1.2–1.8), MW phase, coin bias (e.g., ρ=0.7), thermal/quasimomentum fractions, level of phase noise.

### Output
Outputs: time-of-flight CCD images giving momentum-resolved atomic populations as a function of walk step number; derived scalar metrics: mean momentum, mean energy (second moment) vs step number; internal-state populations after recombiner pulses (for reversal visibility); reconstructed probability distributions across momentum lattice sites. Comparisons: experimental distributions and moments compared to numerical simulations (ideal quantum-walk behavior and models including decoherence and thermal/quasimomentum effects) and contrasted qualitatively with classical random-walk Gaussian signatures. Metrics reported include distribution shapes, mean momentum drift (steering), energy growth scaling (∼j for quantum walk), and visibility/contrast of reversal oscillations.

**Results summary:** This dissertation reports an experimental realization of a discrete-time quantum walk in momentum space using an 87Rb Bose-Einstein condensate. Each step combined a microwave-driven coin toss on two internal hyperfine states with a unitary shift implemented by an AOKR-derived quantum ratchet that entangles internal and momentum degrees of freedom. The work demonstrates hallmark quantum-walk behavior (ballistic spreading), controllability (biased coins/ratchets), partial reversal/recovery of the initial state, and a tunable quantum-to-classical transition via added noise. Experiments reached on the order of 20 steps (detection-limited) and highlight potential advantages and extensions (higher dimensions, many-body effects), though these extensions remain proposals rather than demonstrated results.

**Performance claims:**
- Implemented quantum walks up to ~20 steps in the described apparatus (limited by momentum detection window).
- Typical BEC occupancy cited as about 100,000 atoms sharing the lowest energy level (macroscopic quantum state).
- BEC phase-space density at transition noted as 2.612 (theoretical value for ideal Bose gas).
- Quantum-walk probability distribution displays characteristic standard deviation scaling approximately ∝ number of steps (σ ∼ j).
## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** theoretical

The dissertation demonstrates quantum-walk physics (entanglement-enabled ballistic spreading and coherence effects) and argues that quantum walks are promising for quantum algorithms and faster propagation relative to classical walks, but it does not present an empirical demonstration of computational advantage or algorithmic speedup in a financial-services or benchmarking context; claims of algorithmic/technological advantage are presented as potential applications rather than experimentally proven outcomes.
## Limitations
- Maximum demonstrated walk length is limited (~20 steps) by the current detection / imaging momentum window
- Dephasing and imperfect recovery in walk reversal due to finite quasimomentum distribution and thermal fraction of the BEC (limits fidelity of reversal and state recovery)
- Sensitivity of the walk to global phase offsets from the DC component of the optical standing wave; requires compensation to avoid smearing of momentum currents
- Decoherence sources such as spontaneous emission (even at low per-kick probabilities) degrade walk behavior
- Requirement for a narrow momentum (quasimomentum) distribution and low thermal fraction to achieve clear quantum-walk signatures and high visibility
- [inferred] Scaling to many more steps will require improvements in imaging/detection range and/or reduction of dephasing mechanisms
- [inferred] Extending the scheme to higher dimensions or multi-frequency lattices may involve nontrivial experimental complexity and control challenges
- [inferred] Implementation of many-body quantum walks (including atom-atom interactions) will complicate dynamics and demands theoretical and experimental developments
## Open questions

- How can many-body interactions in a BEC be incorporated into the walk dynamics, and what new phenomena would arise from many-body quantum walks?
- How can the momentum-space walk be extended to higher dimensions experimentally (e.g., multi-dimensional lattices or multiple spatial frequency components)?
- Can the walk reversal capability be harnessed for metrological applications such as enhanced atom interferometry or state-search algorithms, and what are the practical limits?
- What are the requirements and concrete protocols to realize topological phases using these walks?
- How robust is the steering (biasing) of walks via coin or ratchet manipulation under realistic experimental noise?
- [inferred] To what extent can controlled noise be used as a tool to study and quantify the quantum-to-classical transition in this platform?

## Key ideas
- #idea:near-term-feasibility — Demonstrated a discrete-time quantum walk in momentum space on a real cold-atom platform (87Rb BEC) with up to ~20 steps using a microwave coin and atom-optics kicked-rotor (AOKR) ratchet.
- #idea:near-term-feasibility — Experimental protocols enable steering (biased coin and biased ratchet) and coherent reversal (inverse operations), showing controllability of internal-external entanglement and walk dynamics.
- #limitation:noise — Decoherence sources (phase noise on microwave coin, quasimomentum spread, thermal fraction, spontaneous emission) degrade quantum-walk signatures and induce quantum-to-classical transition; these are quantified and modelled.
- #limitation:scalability — Practical limits observed (detection window, decoherence) restrict walk depth (~20 steps) and challenge direct scaling; authors note extendability to higher dimensions/many-body but do not demonstrate large-scale implementations.
## Contradictions
- This experimental implementation shows practical scalability limits (imaging window and decoherence) that contradict any implicit expectation that discrete-time quantum walks can be straightforwardly scaled to very large step counts in this platform without addressing detection range and noise.
- The observed rapid classicalization under added coin-phase noise and non-negligible spontaneous-emission probabilities highlights that claims of robust long-duration quantum-walk coherence must be tempered by hardware noise considerations.
