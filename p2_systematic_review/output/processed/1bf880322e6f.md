## Abstract summary
This preprint extends quantum Monte Carlo integration methods for derivative pricing beyond the Black–Scholes/GBM setting by identifying a structural property of some SDEs called "fast-forwardability" and showing end-to-end quadratic quantum speedups for Cox–Ingersoll–Ross (CIR) and a variant of multi-asset Heston models. It also introduces primitives and arithmetic-free state-loading routines, a quantum Milstein sampler (including a quantum L´evy-area sampler) to enable quantum MLMC for certain correlated processes, improves numerical-quadrature analyses to reduce qubit costs, and analyzes barriers to using quantum PDE solvers for path-dependent pricing.
## Methodology
This is a theoretical and algorithmic study developing a comprehensive framework for analyzing quantum algorithms applied to Monte-Carlo derivative pricing. The authors: (1) formalize the pricing problem as estimation of expectations of payoff functionals over discrete-time path processes induced by Itˆo SDEs and categorize SDEs by a "fast-forwardable" property that admits efficient direct sampling; (2) present an end-to-end error and resource analysis that decomposes total error into truncation, discretization (numerical quadrature), arithmetic, distribution-loading and amplitude-estimation errors, and derive how these must be scaled relative to each other; (3) develop primitive distribution-loading subroutines (both arithmetic-free via quantum eigenvalue transformation / polynomial approximation and Grover-style black-box state preparation) for 1D primitives encountered in finance (standard Gaussian, central and non-central chi-squared, conditional integrals of CIR, 2D L´evy-area marginals), including bounds on polynomial degrees, L2 filling fractions and total-variation guarantees; (4) design and analyze fast-forwarding schemes (classical and coherent) for specific models (CIR, a multi-asset Heston variant with only asset–asset correlations) and provide detailed discretization and truncation analyses showing when the quantum amplitude-encoding and amplitude-estimation pipeline preserves an end-to-end quadratic sampling speedup; (5) for non-fast-forwardable models, develop a quantum Milstein sampler based on a new quantum algorithm for sampling certain L´evy-area marginals and combine this with quantum multilevel Monte Carlo (QMCI + MLMC) analysis to prove quadratic speedups under bipartite correlation structures; (6) analyze polynomial approximations, coherent arithmetic costs (fixed-point arithmetic, Newton for sqrt, polynomial evaluation), and derive one- and two-qubit gate counts and qubit requirements as poly(T,d,log(1/epsilon)) in the asymptotic regime; (7) investigate and prove limitations of using quantum PDE solvers (Fokker–Planck) as subroutines for path-dependent pricing, showing barriers (history-state insufficiency, ℓ1→ℓ2 subnormalization cost, conditioning on non-normal operators and ill-conditioned linear systems) that obstruct end-to-end speedups. The methodology is primarily analytic (proofs, bounds, asymptotic resource counts), algorithm design and complexity analysis, not empirical benchmarking.

**Algorithms used:** Quantum Amplitude Estimation (QAE), Quantum Monte Carlo Integration (QMCI), Quantum Multilevel Monte Carlo (Quantum MLMC), Quantum Eigenvalue Transformation (QET) / QSVT-based state preparation, Grover-style black-box state preparation, Exact amplitude amplification, Quantum Milstein sampler (novel), Quantum L´evy-area sampler (novel), Euler–Maruyama (classical SDE discretization), Milstein scheme (classical SDE discretization), Polynomial approximation (Chebyshev / Taylor style) for function loading, Quantum linear-system / PDE solvers (discussed, e.g. HHL-type and adiabatic approaches)

- [speculative] They prove end-to-end quadratic quantum speedups (in 1/ϵ) for pricing path-dependent derivatives over the Cox–Ingersoll–Ross (CIR) model under stated technical assumptions (Theorems 6.1–6.3).
- [speculative] They prove an end-to-end quadratic quantum speedup for a practically relevant variant of multi-asset Heston (asset-asset correlations only) under stated parameter/truncation conditions (Theorems 6.4–6.6).
- [speculative] They introduce and analyze a suite of primitive distribution-loading subroutines (standard Gaussian, central and non-central χ2, conditional integrals of CIR, and two-dimensional L´evy-area loaders) suitable for coherent qsample preparation on a quantum computer.
- [speculative] The paper gives a quantum algorithm (a 'quantum Milstein sampler') based on a quantum L´evy-area sampler that enables quantum multilevel Monte Carlo (QMLMC) to retain a quadratic speedup for SDEs with bipartite (pairwise) correlations (Theorem 7.2).
- [speculative] They provide a refined numerical-quadrature error analysis that reduces the required number of arithmetic bits in several fast-forwardable cases (e.g., GBM and CIR), often from linear-in-dimension to logarithmic-in-1/ϵ (Theorem 4.1 and related results).
- [speculative] They identify a class of SDEs they call 'fast-forwardable' (and a stricter 'independently fast-forwardable') and argue these properties are central to obtaining end-to-end quantum speedups with vanilla QMCI.
- [speculative] They give explicit sufficient truncation bounds and parameter-regimes for Heston in which quantum loading / discretization is feasible; these bounds require model-parameter constraints to avoid moment explosions.
- [speculative] They argue and analyze that quantum PDE solvers (e.g., solving Fokker–Planck) are not a practical route to prepare qsamples compatible with QMCI for path-dependent derivative pricing because of amplitude vs. probability normalization mismatch, integration/curse-of-dimensionality overheads, and conditioning/conditioning-number issues.
- [speculative] They show that for general correlated multi-dimensional SDEs (beyond bipartite correlations) existing L´evy-area sampling techniques (e.g., KL expansions) likely remove asymptotic quantum advantage because they introduce costs comparable to classical MC.
- [speculative] The paper supplies asymptotic resource/gate and qubit scaling formulas (e.g., eO(poly(T, B)/ϵ) for CIR/Heston under assumptions), and identifies which algorithmic steps dominate costs in different regimes.
- [speculative] The authors present several open problems and barriers: (i) whether generic SDEs allow end-to-end quadratic speedups, (ii) whether multi-dimensional L´evy-area sampling can be significantly accelerated beyond current methods, and (iii) whether sublinear-in-T quantum state preparation compatible with QMCI is possible.

**Results summary:** This theoretical work extends the analysis of quantum Monte Carlo–based derivative pricing beyond Black–Scholes/GBM by (1) providing a comprehensive framework for end-to-end error and resource accounting, (2) defining 'fast-forwardable' SDEs and showing how that property enables efficient quantum qsample preparation, (3) proving asymptotic (quadratic-in-1/ϵ) quantum speedups for pricing over the CIR model and a multi-asset Heston variant (asset-asset correlations only) under explicit assumptions and truncation regimes, (4) introducing primitive quantum loaders (Gaussian, χ2, conditional integrals of CIR, 2D L´evy areas) and a quantum Milstein sampler enabling QMLMC speedups for bipartite-correlated processes, (5) refining numerical quadrature analysis to reduce arithmetic/qubit requirements in several cases, and (6) identifying theoretical obstacles to using quantum PDE solvers or extending speedups to fully general correlated SDEs.

**Performance claims:**
- QMCI reduces sample query complexity for mean estimation from O(σ^2/ϵ^2) (classical MC) to O(σ/ϵ) (quantum) in terms of queries to a qsample oracle (referencing Montanaro [Mon15]).
- For CIR and for a specified class of Heston models, the paper claims end-to-end quantum algorithms with asymptotic gate complexity eO(poly(T,B)/ϵ) and qubit cost O(poly(T)), i.e. linear-in-1/ϵ (Theorems 6.3 and 6.6).
- Quantum MLMC combined with the quantum Milstein sampler achieves asymptotic complexity eO(poly(T,d)·σ0/ϵ) (Theorem 7.2) for globally-Lipschitz payoffs and bipartite correlations.
- Improved discretization/qubit estimates: e.g., for multi-asset GBM the authors claim bits per Gaussian of O(log((1+σ) d T / ϵdisc)) suffice (Theorem 4.1); for CIR an O(log(η B T /(ϵdisc ϵtrunc))) bit scaling per distribution is claimed (Theorem 6.2); for Heston they give a (larger) bound O(T^4 d^2 log^{3/2}(T d / ϵtrunc) log(T d / ϵdisc)) qubits in Theorem 6.5 (noted as likely pessimistic).
- Primitive loader complexities: QET-based polynomial-loading has gate costs polynomial-logarithmic in 1/ϵ for many primitives (Theorems 5.1, 5.2, Corollaries 5.1–5.4); specific loader asymptotics claimed for χ^2, Gaussian and conditional CIR integral loaders.
- Quantum L´evy-area loading for 2D areas can be done with polynomial-logarithmic gate complexity and O(log N) qubits for a discretization with N points (Corollary 5.3).
## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** theoretical

The paper provides theoretical (provable, asymptotic) end-to-end quadratic speedup claims for derivative pricing over specific nontrivial SDEs (CIR and a version of multi-asset Heston) and for MLMC in the bipartite-correlation case using a quantum Milstein sampler. These are mathematical proofs under explicit assumptions and constructions rather than empirical demonstrations; the authors also identify parameter regimes, technical conditions, and open problems that limit generalization to all financial models.
## Limitations
- Quantum end-to-end quadratic speedups proven only for a restricted set of models (previously GBM; this paper extends to CIR and a restricted Heston) and do not automatically generalize to arbitrary SDEs.
- Fast-forwardability is a strong structural property of SDEs that appears rare in financially-relevant models; most SDEs lack it, forcing time-discretization and extra overhead.
- When fast-forwardability is absent, achieving a provable quantum speedup requires multi-level methods and higher-order SDE schemes (Milstein) that in general require sampling L´evy areas — a computationally challenging subproblem.
- Multi-dimensional L´evy-area sampling is hard: known methods (e.g., Karhunen–Lo`eve) have costs comparable to Monte Carlo, which can eliminate the quantum advantage.
- Quantum MLMC speedup proven here is limited to processes with 'bipartite' correlations (each process correlated with at most one other); general multi-process correlations remain untreated.
- The Heston truncation and discretization analyses impose nontrivial constraints on model parameters (e.g., relationships among κ, σ, ρ, ∆) for the bounds to be finite — limiting applicability to some parameter regimes.
- Some theoretical bounds (particularly for Heston discretization) are pessimistic/large; proofs produce polynomial or worse dependence on T and d that may be overly conservative for practical instances.
- State-preparation techniques based on QET are not universally applicable (e.g., conditional distributions need coherent angle-finding and arithmetic); black-box loading requires different tradeoffs.
- Quantum PDE (Fokker–Planck) approaches face fundamental obstacles for Monte-Carlo based pricing: history states do not capture path-dependent payoffs, conversion between ℓ1 and ℓ2 encodings introduces exponential overheads, and linear-system solvers have condition-number/runtime issues that can destroy speedups.
- Amplitude-estimation based QMCI still requires explicit truncation/discretization and can be biased; practical extraction of prices from PDE solutions or from amplitude-encoded states remains hard.
- Resource analyses are asymptotic and can hide large constants; fault-tolerant quantum error-correction overheads may invalidate practical advantages in near- to mid-term hardware.
- [inferred] The presented algorithms rely on many components (high-precision polynomial approximations, coherent arithmetic, amplitude amplification) that may be expensive in gate counts and ancilla qubits in practice.
- [inferred] The distribution-loading subroutines sometimes require polynomial approximation degrees that grow with model parameters (e.g., V(t) magnitudes), which can make arithmetic-free gains less significant.
- [inferred] The work mainly addresses asymptotic complexity; absence of concrete small-scale resource estimates (with all constants) leaves unclear when practical advantage might occur.
## Open questions
- Can fast-forwardability (or other structure that yields similarly efficient qsamples) be identified in a broader class of SDEs of financial interest?
- Is the Milstein (strong order-1) scheme truly necessary for quantum MLMC quadratic speedups in general, or are there alternative schemes/metrics that suffice?
- Can one design efficient (classical or quantum) algorithms for multi-dimensional L´evy-area sampling that avoid Monte Carlo–level costs, thereby enabling Milstein-scale simulation for general correlated SDEs?
- Can quantum MLMC be generalized beyond the bipartite-correlation setting to arbitrary correlation structures while preserving an end-to-end quantum speedup?
- Is it possible to prepare qsamples of T monitoring points with sublinear (in T) quantum resources in a manner compatible with QMCI (i.e., without destroying the σ/ϵ quantum advantage)?
- Can quantum PDE solvers (e.g., Fokker–Planck solvers) be used to prepare amplitudes or sampling primitives for QMCI without incurring exponential or otherwise prohibitive overheads (e.g., the ℓ1→ℓ2 normalization, condition-number dependence)?
- Are there improved numerical-quadrature analyses (or loading techniques) that reduce qubit/gate counts from the pessimistic bounds for Heston and other non-FF models?
- What parameter regimes for practical financial models (CIR/Heston) make the truncation/discretization bounds tight vs. vacuous — i.e., which realistic market parameters allow the proven speedups?
- Can one develop arithmetic-free, coherent conditional-loading techniques (e.g., for conditional χ2, integrals of CIR) that avoid the need for coherent angle finding in QET?
- [inferred] How do constant factors (gate counts, ancilla qubits, QEC overhead) affect the cross-over point where quantum pricing outperforms classical methods in real workloads?
- [inferred] Can alternative quantum primitives (e.g., improved quantum walks, quantum rejection sampling variants, or generative-model-based methods) enable sublinear space/time simulation for path-dependent derivatives?

**Future work:**
- Investigate the applicability and identification of fast-forwardable SDEs beyond GBM, CIR, and the restricted Heston variant.
- Develop faster and/or more practical algorithms for multi-dimensional L´evy-area sampling (classical or quantum) to enable Milstein-based quantum MLMC for more general correlated models.
- Extend quantum MLMC analysis and algorithm design to handle more general correlation patterns (beyond bipartite) while preserving quantum speedups.
- Improve numerical quadrature and distribution-loading analyses to reduce qubit counts and gate costs (seek tighter bounds than those currently derived for Heston/CIR).
- Explore arithmetic-free or low-arithmetic state-preparation techniques for conditional distributions and primitives (e.g., integral-of-CIR, noncentral χ2) to reduce coherent arithmetic overheads.
- Study whether quantum PDE solvers (and related spectral/pseudospectral methods) can be adapted or preconditioned to avoid the ℓ1→ℓ2 and condition-number bottlenecks for distribution loading compatible with QMCI.
- Benchmark and numerically evaluate practical resource requirements (including QEC overhead) to determine regimes where quantum algorithms might achieve a practical advantage over classical approaches.
- Seek new quantum algorithmic primitives or hybrid classical-quantum strategies that allow sublinear (in T) simulation of path processes while maintaining QMCI compatibility.
- Analyze and mitigate the impact of model-parameter regimes (e.g., moment explosions) on the feasibility of truncation/discretization bounds, and identify realistic parameter ranges where theory supports quantum advantage.
- Apply the distribution-loading and error-analysis framework to other continuous integration problems beyond derivative pricing to validate generality and transferability.
