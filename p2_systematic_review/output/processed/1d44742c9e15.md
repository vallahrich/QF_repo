## Abstract summary
The authors propose a variational quantum algorithm to price multi-asset derivatives by solving the Black–Scholes PDE using variational quantum simulation (VQS) and computing the discounted expected payoff as an inner product with the asset-price probability state. This approach uses state preparation (via generative models or VQS for the Fokker–Planck equation), VQS for the PDE, and the SWAP test to avoid the amplitude-extraction bottleneck of naive quantum methods, aiming to make derivative pricing feasible on small/noisy quantum devices; numerical experiments for a single-asset double barrier option validate the method.
## Methodology
The authors develop a variational quantum algorithm to price multi-asset derivatives under the Black–Scholes model. They (1) discretize the Black–Scholes PDE (BSPDE) in asset-price space via a finite-difference method (FDM) to obtain a linear ODE d/dτ V = F V + C(τ); (2) represent the matrix F and inhomogeneity C(τ) as linear combinations of unitary gates (LCU) so that they fit the requirements of variational quantum simulation (VQS); (3) prepare an unnormalized quantum state |p(tter)⟩ embedding the probability distribution of asset prices at a chosen future time tter (either via VQS applied to the Fokker–Planck equation or quantum generative models) and prepare an unnormalized initial payoff state |ψV⟩ (again via quantum generative models or parameter fitting); (4) simulate the ODE forward from τ=0 to τ=τter using VQS with a parameterized ansatz (repeated RY single-qubit rotations and CZ entangling layers), evaluating the variational equations via Hadamard/controlled circuits to obtain M and V matrices and integrating the parameter ODEs (they use Euler integration for parameter updates); (5) compute the inner product between the probability state and the VQS solution at τter via the SWAP test and obtain the present derivative price as V0 ≃ e^{−r tter} ⟨p(tter)|˜V(τter)⟩ (they actually obtain |⟨p|V⟩|^2 from SWAP and take the square root with normalization factors). They analytically and numerically analyze measurement/sample complexities (in particular the number of SWAP-test shots required) and provide explicit decompositions of finite-difference operators into implementable circuits. Numerical validation is performed for a single-asset double barrier option, using a noiseless state-vector simulation (NumPy) and classical optimizers to fit initial states where required.

**Algorithms used:** Variational Quantum Simulation (VQS), SWAP test (state overlap estimation), Hadamard test (expectation estimation), Finite Difference Method (FDM) for PDE discretization, Euler method (classical integration of variational parameters), Quantum generative models (proposed for state preparation), Classical optimizer L-BFGS-B (for parameter fitting), Parameter-shift rule (for gradient evaluation)
**Frameworks:** NumPy, SciPy

**Experimental setup:** No physical quantum hardware was used. All quantum circuits/state evolution were simulated on a noiseless state-vector simulator implemented using NumPy. The authors assume availability of circuits for LCU decompositions and evaluate circuit-level gate counts and measurement counts analytically. They do not simulate measurement noise or finite shots; SWAP and Hadamard test sample complexities are discussed theoretically but not executed with finite shot noise.

**Dataset:** Synthetic data generated from the Black–Scholes model for a single-asset double knock-out (double barrier) option. Probability distributions are analytical log-normal distributions for geometric Brownian motion under the risk-neutral measure. The payoff is a standard call-basket style payoff (for the single-asset tests: max(S(T) − K, 0)).

## Findings
- [speculative] The paper proposes a variational quantum algorithm (VQS) pipeline to price multi-asset derivatives by (i) preparing a quantum state embedding the underlying-assets probability distribution, (ii) solving the discretized Black–Scholes PDE (BSPDE) with VQS, and (iii) computing the inner product with a SWAP test to obtain the present derivative price.
- [speculative] Using the expected discounted payoff at a future time t_ter (rather than extracting a single amplitude at t=0) avoids the measurement bottleneck of naive amplitude-based retrieval and can remove terms like (1/ϵ)^{O(d)} from the complexity.
- [speculative] The number of SWAP-test measurements NSWAP required to estimate the overlap does not scale as (1/ϵ)^{O(d)}; an upper-bound expression (their Eq. (61)) is given and depends polynomially/logarithmically on 1/ϵ and exponentially on d only through milder factors.
- [speculative] The linear operators arising from the finite-difference discretization (matrix F and boundary vector C) can be decomposed into linear combinations of unitaries; authors give cost estimates: F decomposes into O(d^2 n^4) unitaries (each O(n^2) gates) and C into O(d^3 n^2) unitaries (each O(n^2) gates), where n = log N_gr.
- [speculative] Under stated assumptions (efficient state-preparation, bounded parameter norms, etc.), overall gate counts for the algorithm are O(poly(d log(1/ϵ))) and the asymptotic dependence on precision can be as favorable as O(1/ϵ^2 (log(1/ϵ))^d) (paper states this complexity in the conclusion).
- [supported] The authors implemented numerical simulations (no quantum hardware/no noise) for a single-asset double knock-out barrier option and demonstrated that VQS + SWAP test can reproduce prices comparable to classical finite-difference solutions for small grid sizes (4–6 qubits / 16–64 grid points) and that increasing ansatz depth and qubits improves accuracy.
- [supported] The authors report they can fit the initial (unnormalized) payoff state for a single-asset call (fpay(x) = max(x-K,0)) with an ansatz (6 qubits, 6 layers) achieving fidelity error on the order of 1.2e-5 in the optimization reported.
- [speculative] The method is presented as being implementable on small-scale (near-term) quantum computers because it uses variational algorithms (VQS) and SWAP test rather than fully fault-tolerant subroutines (QAE, quantum ODE solver), although this depends on assumptions about state preparation and measurement costs.
- [speculative] The approach can provide substantial speedup relative to classical finite-difference methods when the other parts (state preparation, VQS convergence) are efficient; however, no rigorous end-to-end proof of speedup is provided because VQS is heuristic.
- [speculative] For t_ter → 0 the advantage disappears (overlap becomes concentrated on a single grid point), recovering the measurement bottleneck; therefore t_ter must be chosen appropriately (trade-off between approximation error and overlap).
- [speculative] The SWAP-test based overlap estimation (and associated number of measurements) can be planned in advance because normalization factors α and β can be computed/classically estimated.

**Results summary:** The authors introduce a variational quantum algorithm pipeline for pricing multi-asset derivatives under the Black–Scholes model. They discretize the BSPDE via finite differences, represent the resulting linear operators as linear combinations of unitaries, prepare a quantum state for the underlying-assets distribution, evolve the payoff via VQS to a future time τ_ter, and estimate the expected discounted payoff by evaluating the overlap with a SWAP test. Theoretical resource estimates suggest that the method avoids the (1/ϵ)^{O(d)} measurement bottleneck associated with amplitude retrieval and can have gate/measurement costs polynomial in d and log(1/ϵ) under several assumptions. Numerical (noise-free) simulations for a single-asset double barrier option (4–6 qubits) show the method can reproduce classical finite-difference results for small grids and that the initial payoff state can be approximated with high fidelity by a variational ansatz. The authors emphasize the heuristic nature of VQS and the need for efficient state preparation as caveats to rigorous complexity claims.

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** speculative

The paper presents a theoretical/heuristic argument that using a future-time expected payoff plus overlap estimation via SWAP test avoids the amplitude-retrieval measurement bottleneck and can reduce dependence on precision from (1/ϵ)^{O(d)} to milder poly/log factors. However, the end-to-end speedup depends on several nontrivial and heuristic assumptions (efficient variational state preparation for both probability and payoff states, convergence of VQS, bounded parameter norms). There is no hardware demonstration or rigorous, provable complexity guarantee covering all steps, so the quantum advantage remains conjectural/speculative.
## Limitations
- No formal guarantee of overall computational complexity due to the heuristic nature of variational algorithms (author-stated).
- Assumes access to oracles / efficient procedures that prepare the unnormalized initial payoff state and the probability-distribution state; the paper assumes these can be generated efficiently but does not fully demonstrate scalable methods for doing so on realistic devices (author-stated).
- Complexity analysis depends on several assumptions (e.g., generative models prepare states in O(poly(d log(1/ϵ))) gates and measurements); these assumptions are not proven and may not hold in practice (author-stated).
- Number of SWAP-test measurements NSWAP still has exponential dependence on d (number of assets) (author-stated); while it avoids factors like (1/ϵ)^{O(d)}, NSWAP grows with d and other problem constants.
- Numerical validation is limited: simulations are noise-free and focus on a single-asset double barrier option; there is no multi-asset empirical demonstration (author-stated).
- The VQS implementation and resource estimates assume decomposition of the discretized operator F and boundary vector C into linear combinations of unitaries; this decomposition leads to many terms (O(d^2 n^4) etc.) and the practical gate/ancilla overhead may be large (author-stated / implied).
- They assume normalization factor |θ0(t)| is bounded by some constant when estimating measurement counts for VQS; this assumption may not hold for arbitrary payoffs or parameter regimes (author-stated).
- The paper does not model hardware noise or sampling/statistical errors in the quantum-circuit simulations; real device performance and error mitigation are not evaluated (author-stated).
- [inferred] Scalability risk from variational ansatz expressibility and trainability: as problem size grows (more assets, higher ngr) the chosen ansatz may become insufficient or hard to train (barren-plateau / optimization difficulties).
- [inferred] The practical cost (classical optimization, number of VQS time steps N_τ, and measurement overhead N_VQS_measure) may be large and could negate the theoretical advantages, but is not quantified concretely.
- [inferred] Sensitivity and dependence on the choice of t_ter: trade-offs between approximation error (outside boundaries, p_NB) and overlap (measurement cost) are discussed qualitatively but no systematic selection procedure is provided.
- [inferred] The constant bounds used in several estimates (e.g., ζ bounding ratio of squared norms, B bounding payoff) may be conservative or hard to determine, affecting measurement estimates and practicality.
- [inferred] The SWAP-test approach requires preparing two states coherently and assumes controlled operations; the circuit depth and coherence time requirements may be prohibitive on near-term devices.

- How tight/realistic are the assumptions such as bounded |θ0(t)| and bounded ratio ζ of squared norms over time? How to estimate these constants for practical problems?
- What are the best practices to choose t_ter to balance approximation error (from neglecting outside-boundary contributions and p_NB) against the SWAP-test measurement cost (overlap)?
- How expressive and trainable must the variational ansatz be to represent the initial payoff and the time-evolved BSPDE solution for higher-dimensional problems? Are there ansatz designs that mitigate barren plateaus here?
- What is the classical optimization cost (time, convergence reliability) for finding variational parameters for the initial state and for VQS in larger problem instances?
- Can the decomposition of F and C into sum-of-unitaries be optimized to reduce the number of terms / gate depth, or are alternative encodings more practical?
- How robust is the SWAP-test inner-product estimation when the two states have small but nonzero overlap, especially when state preparation and VQS are noisy?
- Can alternative inner-product estimation methods (other than the SWAP test) provide better measurement scaling or noise robustness in this context?
- What are the empirical trade-offs between preparing the initial payoff via an ansatz that starts from |0> vs. preparing it via a separate generative model (i.e., UV) as discussed in the paper?
- How does the method compare (in end-to-end wall-clock and resource use) to classical numerical methods or other quantum approaches for moderate to large d?
