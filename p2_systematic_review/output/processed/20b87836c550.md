## Abstract summary
This 2024 book surveys the application of quantum computing to real-world optimization problems across domains including logistics, finance, and life sciences, focusing on algorithms such as QAOA, VQE, and quantum Boltzmann machines. It evaluates the practical feasibility of NISQ-era and hybrid quantum-classical approaches, reports empirical simulation and case-study results, and discusses implications for portfolio optimization, risk analysis, and the transition to post-quantum secure systems.
## Methodology
This work is primarily a conceptual and literature-review driven study augmented by framework analysis and reported empirical validations. The methodology combines a structured review of recent literature with a cross-sectoral synthesis to build a five-layer conceptual model (Core, Application, Intelligence, Security, Connectivity). Where empirical validation is referenced, the approach is described as hybrid quantum–classical benchmarking: algorithms (e.g., QAOA, VQE, quantum annealing) are evaluated via simulations and pilot case studies to assess convergence speed, solution quality, and error tolerance under NISQ constraints. The text indicates comparative experiments using cloud-accessible simulation stacks and quantum annealers (notably IBM Qiskit simulations and D-Wave systems) and advocates statistical validation of probabilistic quantum outputs. It emphasizes hybrid pipelines that use classical optimizers around parameterized quantum circuits, noise-aware training methods, and metrics for fidelity and benchmarking, while also discussing limitations tied to qubit fidelity, decoherence, and scalability. However, the work does not provide a step-by-step experimental protocol, dataset specifications, or detailed parameter settings for the referenced empirical evaluations.

## Limitations
- Hardware limitations and the NISQ era: current quantum devices are not yet fault-tolerant.
- Decoherence: quantum systems are extremely sensitive to environmental disturbances causing loss of coherence.
- Noise and gate errors: imperfect quantum gates and accumulating errors degrade results.
- Qubit scalability: present hardware supports only a limited number of qubits; scaling to thousands/millions is an open engineering challenge.
- Probabilistic outputs: quantum algorithms produce probabilistic results that require statistical validation and repeated sampling.
- Immature quantum error correction (QEC): QEC approaches and fault-tolerant architectures are still under development.
- Benchmarking and fidelity: insufficient standardized benchmarks and variable gate fidelities make readiness assessment for commercial use difficult.
- Training difficulties for QML: issues such as barren plateaus, vanishing gradients, and noise complicate training of QNNs and other variational models.
- Practicality of quantum supremacy: laboratory demonstrations exist, but practical, near-term applications remain limited.
- Security risk to existing cryptography: quantum algorithms (e.g., Shor) threaten RSA/ECC, while the transition to post-quantum cryptography is gradual and incomplete.
- Ethical and socio-economic concerns: potential for unequal access, economic disparity, and governance/ethical risks in deployment.
- Integration gap: translating theoretical algorithms into robust, production-grade solutions for real-world systems remains challenging.
