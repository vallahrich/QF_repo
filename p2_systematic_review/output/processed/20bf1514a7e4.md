## Abstract summary
This paper investigates uncertainty quantification (UQ) in quantum-classical hybrid machine learning models applied to supply chain digital twins and financial risk assessment. It studies how quantum feature transformations and qubit scaling (4 to 16 qubits) alter uncertainty propagation, outlier detection, and a risk-adjusted cost-efficiency metric, finding that quantum-transformed features produce distinct uncertainty behaviors and can affect decision-relevant risk measures.
## Methodology
The study uses a quantum-classical hybrid pipeline to quantify predictive uncertainty for a synthetic supply-chain digital twin. Synthetic data (features such as product_demand, inventory_levels, lead_time, order_quantity, transportation_costs, storage_costs, production_costs, sales_price, supplier_reliability, customer_satisfaction, warehouse_capacity_utilisation, weather_conditions, market_trends, supply_chain_disruption, economic_shocks, natural_disasters, cybersecurity_threats) is generated and pre-processed (label encoding for categorical variables, scaling). Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) with a Gradient Boosting estimator selects feature subsets of sizes 4, 6, 8, ..., 16 to match qubit configurations. Quantum feature engineering encodes the selected features into parameterised variational quantum circuits using RX/RY/RZ single-qubit rotations, Hadamard layers, and multi-qubit entangling gates (Controlled-Y (CY), CRZ, CNOT, CZ) and extracts expectation values of Pauli-X, Pauli-Y, and Pauli-Z per qubit as transformed features. Circuit parameters are optimised with a gradient-based (mini-batch gradient descent) routine minimising a composite loss (mutual information loss between quantum features and target + diversity penalty to reduce feature correlation). Quantum circuits and optimisation are implemented in PennyLane; feature selection and classical ML tools are via scikit-learn. The quantum-transformed features feed classical UQ-capable models: Bayesian Ridge Regression (BRR), Quantile Regression (QR), Random Forest (RF) and a Neural Network (NN). Model-specific UQ methods are: BRR—analytical aleatoric/epistemic decomposition from posterior covariance; QR—prediction interval widths (25th/75th quantiles); RF—variance across ensemble trees (100 trees); NN—Monte Carlo Dropout with dropout active at inference and 1,000 forward passes to estimate predictive variance. Classical NN architecture: two hidden layers (128 and 64 units) with 0.5 dropout, Adam optimizer, 200 epochs. Training uses 5-fold cross-validation. Evaluation includes standard predictive metrics (MSE, MAE, R2), Pearson correlation between uncertainty and absolute error, Prediction Interval Coverage Probability (PICP), two outlier detection methods (bound-based using intervals and uncertainty-based using IQR on predictive uncertainties), and statistical tests (Wilcoxon Signed-Rank for pairwise quantum vs classical on 4-qubit base, Kruskal-Wallis H test across qubit counts). Qubit scaling experiments vary the quantum feature circuit size from 4 to 16 qubits to observe effects on uncertainty propagation, outlier detection and a derived financial impact metric (RACE) computed as Actual - beta * Uncertainty for several risk-aversion betas.

**Algorithms used:** Variational Quantum Circuit (parameterized quantum circuit), RX/RY/RZ encoding, Hadamard and multi-qubit entanglers (CY, CRZ, CNOT, CZ), Gradient-based optimisation (mini-batch gradient descent) of circuit parameters, Mutual information loss with diversity penalty (custom cost), Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) with Gradient Boosting, Bayesian Ridge Regression, Quantile Regression, Random Forest (ensemble of decision trees), Neural Network with Monte Carlo Dropout, Monte Carlo Dropout (for UQ), Outlier detection using interval bounds and IQR, Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test, Kruskal-Wallis H Test

**Experimental setup:** Quantum circuits and feature engineering implemented in PennyLane (no specific QPU or cloud backend reported); classical models implemented with scikit-learn and TensorFlow. Qubit count varied across experiments (4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16).

**Dataset:** Synthetic supply-chain digital twin dataset generated by authors (features include product demand, inventory levels, lead time, order quantity, transportation/storage/production costs, sales price, supplier reliability, customer satisfaction, warehouse utilisation, weather conditions, market trends, supply_chain_disruption, economic_shocks, natural_disasters, cybersecurity_threats). ## Findings
- [supported] Quantum-classical hybrid models (quantum feature transformation + classical predictors) achieved comparable predictive accuracy to purely classical models on the synthetic supply-chain dataset (reported R2 ≈ 0.79, RMSE ≈ 0.78, MAE ≈ 0.75).
- [supported] Quantum-featured hybrids produced different uncertainty outputs than classical models: in many cases hybrid models yielded narrower predictive-uncertainty distributions.
- [supported] Hybrid models identified a different and in some cases larger set of outlier samples compared to classical models (outlier counts reported in Table II and Figures).
- [supported] For Bayesian ridge regression (BRR) and quantile regression (QR) the Wilcoxon Signed-Rank test shows highly significant differences in predictive uncertainties between quantum-hybrid and classical variants (BRR p = 6.08E-51; QR p = 9.33E-06), while prediction errors did not show significant differences.
- [supported] Increasing qubit count (4→16) produced statistically significant differences in predictive uncertainty across configurations (Kruskal–Wallis p < 0.05) but did not significantly change prediction errors (Kruskal–Wallis p > 0.05).
- [supported] Qubit scaling altered outlier-detection behaviour: HQBRR and HQQR showed reductions in detected OD samples as qubits increased (e.g., HQQR outliers reduced sharply from 15 to 3 after increasing qubits), while HQRF and HQNN showed less consistent change.
- [supported] Risk-adjusted cost-efficiency (RACE) computed with uncertainty penalties differs between quantum-hybrid and classical models; some hybrid models (HQRF, HQNN) showed notable dips in RACE around 10–12 qubits for high risk aversion (β = 0.75–1.0), whereas HQBRR and HQQR remained relatively stable across qubit counts.
- [speculative] Qubit scaling can be used as a general method to improve outlier detection and robustness of uncertainty quantification in hybrid models across real-world datasets and applications.
- [speculative] Quantum feature transformations (via VQA-style circuits) offer a scalable way to refine uncertainty quantification useful for high-stakes decision making (e.g., supply-chain resilience, financial risk), beyond the synthetic experiments shown.
- [speculative] Larger quantum feature spaces (more qubits) will generally improve data representation and generalisation in hybrid UQ tasks (paper cites literature supporting the potential but does not empirically prove generality here).

**Results summary:** This preprint reports experiments on synthetic supply-chain data comparing classical ML models to quantum-classical hybrid variants in which input features are transformed by parameterised quantum circuits (4–16 qubits) and then fed to classical predictors (BRR, QR, RF, NN). Predictive accuracy was similar between classical and hybrid models (R2 ≈ 0.79; RMSE ≈ 0.78; MAE ≈ 0.75), but uncertainty quantification behaviors differed: hybrid models often produced narrower uncertainty distributions and identified different (sometimes more) outliers. Statistical tests showed significant differences in uncertainty (not errors) for BRR and QR between hybrid and classical versions. Increasing qubit count changed uncertainty propagation (Kruskal–Wallis p < 0.05) and tended to reduce detected outliers for some models (notably HQBRR and HQQR). A simple risk-adjusted metric (RACE) demonstrated that these uncertainty differences can translate to different financial impacts under varying risk aversion levels, with some hybrids (HQRF, HQNN) showing sensitivity to qubit count around 10–12 qubits.

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** speculative

The paper does not demonstrate a clear quantum computational advantage (no superior predictive accuracy or runtime advantage on real hardware); it reports differences in uncertainty behaviour and outlier detection from quantum-feature transforms on synthetic data and simulations. Claims that qubit scaling improves UQ/outlier detection are presented as empirical observations in this synthetic study but are not established as a general quantum advantage for practical financial tasks.
## Limitations
- Use of a synthetically generated dataset rather than real-world supply chain or financial data (synthetic data may not capture real-world complexities or noise).
- Quantum circuits were applied only for feature transformation; the study did not evaluate hybrid quantum circuits used directly for prediction (authors note this as a direction for future work).
- Qubit exploration was limited to configurations between 4 and 16 qubits — results outside this range are unknown.
- [inferred] Experiments appear to be performed in simulation (PennyLane) with no evaluation on real quantum hardware or explicit modelling of device noise, so hardware noise and decoherence effects are not assessed.
- [inferred] Uncertainty quantification methods differ across models (BRR, QR, RF, NN use different UQ formalisms), which complicates direct comparison of uncertainty outputs between model types.
- [inferred] The financial impact metric (RACE) uses a simple linear penalty formulation for uncertainty; this may be an oversimplification of real economic risk modeling and may not reflect complex cost structures.
- [inferred] Limited analysis of circuit hyperparameters and design choices (encoding gates, entanglers, number of layers, optimisation details) and their specific effect on UQ — circuit design space exploration is narrow.
- [inferred] No systematic analysis of computational cost, training/inference time, or scalability overhead introduced by quantum feature transformations versus purely classical pipelines.
- [inferred] Limited model family selection (only BRR, QR, RF, NN) — other classical or probabilistic models and alternative Bayesian/ensemble approaches were not considered.
- [inferred] Lack of causal explanation or mechanistic insight for why some hybrid models (e.g., HQBRR, HQQR) showed stronger sensitivity to qubit scaling while others (HQRF, HQNN) did not.
- [inferred] No assessment of reproducibility on external/real datasets or cross-domain validation to confirm generalisability of the reported effects.
## Open questions
- Is there a qubit-count threshold beyond which adding more qubits no longer improves or may degrade uncertainty quantification performance?
- How do quantum feature transformations and qubit scaling behave under realistic quantum hardware noise and finite sampling (shots)?
- Do the observed uncertainty improvements (or shifts) generalise to real-world supply chain and financial datasets with richer dynamics and noise?
- Would using quantum circuits for direct prediction (instead of only feature transformation) produce different or better UQ outcomes?
- How sensitive are UQ outcomes to specific quantum circuit design choices (encoding schemes, entangling patterns, number of variational layers, measured observables)?
- Can quantum-aware methods (e.g., quantum conformal prediction, Bayesian QNNs) provide better-calibrated predictive intervals in hybrid settings compared to the classical UQ techniques used here?
- What are the root causes for the heterogeneous response of different model types (BRR, QR, RF, NN) to quantum feature transformations and qubit scaling?
- How should uncertainty outputs from heterogeneous UQ methods be normalized or compared to enable fair financial impact assessment across models?
- How robust are the hybrid models’ outlier detection capabilities in the presence of adversarial or structured distributional shifts common in financial/supply-chain data?
- How to translate the stylised RACE metric into realistic monetary risk measures and integrate it into operational decision-making frameworks?

**Future work:**
- Investigate the impact of qubit count on the performance of hybrid-quantum models using quantum circuits for direct prediction tasks rather than only for feature transformation.
- Identify thresholds beyond which additional qubits no longer contribute to improving results (i.e., find qubit-scaling limits or saturation points).
- Extend the testing of these UQ frameworks to more complex and realistic datasets.
