## Abstract summary
This preprint introduces the Quantum Walk Optimisation Algorithm (QWOA), a hybrid quantum-classical approach for discrete Markowitz portfolio optimisation aimed at NISQ devices. By indexing the feasible, non-degenerate solution subspace and implementing a continuous-time quantum walk mixer (chosen as a complete graph), the authors show QWOA substantially reduces the search space and outperforms QAOA and QAOAz in numerical simulations, achieving faster convergence to high-quality portfolios with lower parameter-optimisation variance.
## Methodology
The authors formulate discrete mean-variance (Markowitz) portfolio optimisation with ternary asset positions (long=+1, short=-1, no position=0) and encode each asset using two qubits. They compare three hybrid quantum-classical approximate optimisation approaches: (1) QAOA with a soft-penalty term added to the objective to enforce the total-position constraint, (2) QAOAz using a dual-parity-ring mixer that preserves Hamming weights (parity bands) and an initial superposition over degenerate valid states, and (3) QWOA which (a) computes an efficient bijective classical indexing id_{n,A} between canonical non-degenerate valid portfolios and indices 0..M-1, (b) prepares an equal superposition over only the M valid, non-degenerate solutions, and (c) implements a continuous-time quantum walk mixer on a chosen circulant graph (here the complete graph K_M) via U_QWOA(t)=U_# e^{-i t K_M} U_#^†, where U_# is the indexing unitary. They derive counting formulas for valid state cardinalities, describe recursive ranking/unranking algorithms for indexing/unindexing, and characterise circuit complexities (indexing O(n^2), walk simulation O(n) up to polylog factors). Numerical experiments (statevector-style quantum simulations) are run to optimise the variational parameters (γ, t) using classical optimisers (BFGS as primary, other methods tested) and evaluate expectation ⟨ψ_f|C|ψ_f⟩, state probabilities and derived financial metrics (expected annual return and risk). Comparisons are performed across p (number of alternating layers) to assess convergence, variability, and quality of solutions.

**Algorithms used:** QAOA, QAOAz, QWOA
**Frameworks:** QuOP MPI (quantum optimisation simulator), Yahoo Finance Python API (data retrieval), BFGS classical optimiser (used for variational parameter optimisation)

**Experimental setup:** Numerical simulation experiments using the QuOP MPI simulator (parallel distributed-memory simulation) run on HPC resources (Pawsey Supercomputing Centre). Classical optimisation of variational parameters used BFGS (other classical optimisers were tested for comparison). Repeated simulation trials with random initial variational parameters were performed.

**Dataset:** Two empirical datasets of daily adjusted close prices for n = 8 ASX-listed stocks obtained via Yahoo Finance. Data Set A: 2017-01-01 to 2018-12-31 (tickers: AMP, ANZ, AMC, BHP, BXB, CBA, CSL, IAG). Data Set B: 2020-03-24 to 2020-09-06 (tickers: FLT, QAN, WEB, REX, AIZ, SYD, SCG, CTD).

## Findings
- [supported] The Quantum Walk Optimisation Algorithm (QWOA) outperforms QAOA and QAOAz on the tested portfolio optimisation instances (n=8, A=4) in numerical simulations: faster convergence to high-quality solutions and lower run-to-run variance.
- [supported] QWOA restricts the search to canonical valid (non-degenerate) portfolios of size M(n,A), substantially reducing the effective search space compared with QAOA and QAOAz (numerical example: for n=8, A=4 the reported search-space sizes are QAOA=2^16, QAOAz=1820, QWOA=266).
- [supported] The authors present an explicit classical indexing/un-indexing procedure to bijectively map valid portfolio encodings to indices; the paper provides an analytic gate-complexity argument that the indexing circuit dominates mixing depth and leads to an overall mixing complexity O(n^2) (polylog factors omitted).
- [supported] In simulations QWOA amplifies the optimal portfolio probability substantially (reported >40% for Data Set A at p=19, ~20% for Data Set B at p=19), whereas QAOAz sometimes fails to amplify optimal (degenerate) solutions in its parity bands.
- [supported] QAOA with a soft penalty over the full 2^{2n} Hilbert space shows poorer average performance and higher variance in the experiments, attributed to the inclusion of invalid states and degeneracy.
- [supported] QAOAz (dual-parity ring mixer) creates disconnected parity-band components and a biased amplitude distribution over parity bands; this can prevent amplitude flow to some optimal solutions depending on which parity-band they lie in.
- [speculative] Using a complete-graph circulant mixer (as in QWOA) is likely an (near-)optimal mixing choice for combinatorial optimisation due to maximal symmetry and minimal diameter — argued by analogy to proven optimality in some quantum spatial search settings.
- [speculative] The polynomial (quadratic) increase in mixing circuit complexity for QWOA is worth the reduction in search space for NISQ-targeted use, because QWOA converges much more rapidly at low p (the paper asserts this as a practical advantage for near-term devices).
- [speculative] The QWOA approach and indexing idea generalise to other combinatorial optimisation problems with non-power-of-two feasible sets (e.g., the Travelling Salesman Problem), and similar advantages over QAOA-family methods are expected.
- [supported] The hybrid classical optimiser (BFGS used) suffers from the curse of dimensionality as p increases; therefore algorithms that reach good solutions at low p (QWOA in these simulations) are practically advantageous.

**Results summary:** The paper introduces and evaluates QWOA for discrete mean-variance (Markowitz) portfolio optimisation. Analytic counting shows QWOA restricts the search to M(n,A) canonical valid portfolios, reducing the search space compared to both QAOA (full 2^{2n} space) and QAOAz (includes degenerate encodings). A classical indexing/un-indexing circuit enables a continuous-time quantum-walk mixer (implemented over a circulant/complete graph), with overall mixing complexity argued to be O(n^2). Numerical simulations on two 8-asset datasets (A=4, λ=0.5) show QWOA converges to higher-quality portfolios at lower p, amplifies optimal solutions (reported >40% and ~20% probabilities for the two datasets at p=19), and exhibits smaller variance than QAOA and QAOAz. The authors argue these benefits make QWOA promising for NISQ-era combinatorial optimisation and that the approach generalises to other non-power-of-two feasible-solution problems.

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** speculative

The paper demonstrates improved performance of a quantum-heuristic (QWOA) over other quantum heuristics (QAOA, QAOAz) in classical numerical simulations for small problem instances, but does not demonstrate a provable or empirical advantage over classical algorithms nor demonstrate performance on actual quantum hardware. Claims of practical NISQ-era advantage and general optimality of the complete-graph mixer are presented as arguments or conjectures rather than proven or experimentally validated across broader problem instances.
## Limitations
- Classical parameter optimisation suffers from the curse of dimensionality as p increases; the classical optimiser deteriorates with growing number of variational parameters (author-stated).
- QAOA with soft penalty includes invalid states in the search space, leading to poorer average performance and large variance (author-stated).
- QAOAz mixing operator produces disconnected parity bands, asymmetric connectivity and degeneracy which can prevent amplitude transfer between bands and bias results (author-stated).
- QWOA mixing unitary (indexing + walk) has higher circuit complexity (reported as O(n^2) gate complexity for the indexing component) compared with O(n) for the other mixers — a polynomial (quadratic) increase in circuit depth per iteration (author-stated).
- [inferred] All results are from classical numerical simulation only (QuOP MPI); no experiments on real quantum hardware or noise models are presented.
- [inferred] No analysis of noise, decoherence or other NISQ hardware errors is included; robustness of QWOA under realistic NISQ noise is not evaluated.
- [inferred] Scalability beyond the tested problem size (n = 8) is not demonstrated; practical performance for larger portfolios is unclear.
- [inferred] The indexing/un-indexing unitary requires classical precomputation and ancilla registers and may have significant resource overheads in practice.
- [inferred] The claimed advantage relies on the ability to construct an efficient bijection id_{n,A}(x); constructing such an efficient indexing may be nontrivial for other constraint types or problem encodings.
- [inferred] The trade-off between reducing the search space and increasing circuit depth may be adverse in noisy devices — the benefit of search-space reduction might be offset by deeper circuits under noise.
- [inferred] Dependence on classical optimisation routines (BFGS was used here) introduces vulnerability to local minima and may not generalise; the classical optimiser choice and scaling behaviour are not fully explored.
- [inferred] The optimality of the complete-graph mixer for general combinatorial optimisation is asserted as likely but not proven; there may be problem instances where other mixers are better.
## Open questions
- How will QWOA perform on real quantum hardware (NISQ devices) subject to noise and gate errors?
- How does QWOA scale with increasing number of assets n and different constraint values A in practice (resource usage, convergence, and solution quality)?
- Can the indexing/un-indexing circuits be further optimised (gate count, ancilla usage, depth) to reduce the O(n^2) overhead and make QWOA more practical?
- Is the complete graph mixer always the best mixer choice for arbitrary combinatorial optimisation instances, or are there cases where other circulant or problem-specific mixers outperform it?
- How sensitive is QWOA’s advantage to the structure of degeneracy and parity-band distributions in other domains and encodings?
- Can classical parameter optimisation be improved (better optimisers, parameter initialisation strategies, warm-starts, parameter transfer between instances) to mitigate the curse of dimensionality for higher p?
- What are the concrete resource estimates (number of qubits, circuit depth, number of shots) and error thresholds necessary for QWOA to outperform classical or other quantum heuristics on practical portfolio sizes?
- Does QWOA maintain its advantage across a wider variety of financial datasets, risk-return parameters (λ), and different objective/constraint formulations?
- How do sampling noise and finite-shot estimation of expectation values affect the practical optimisation loop and convergence for all compared algorithms?
- How generalisable are the reported advantages of QWOA to other combinatorial optimisation problems (e.g., Traveling Salesman Problem) beyond the examples given?
