## Abstract summary
This paper presents computational results from a real-life implementation of a quantum computing application for investment analytics, building on a previously published algorithm. It reports speed-up experiments on idealized problems and large professional financial datasets, discusses implementation challenges, comparative results (including classical alternatives like GPUs), and explores using related data for analogous tasks such as implied graphs to inform factor selection and portfolio cuts.
## Methodology
The authors formulate portfolio selection as a graph partitioning (normalized graph cut) problem on a market graph whose nodes are assets and whose edge weights are absolute return correlations |rho_ij|, optionally mixed with fundamental GICS sector indicator information via a linear mixing parameter kappa. The normalized cut objective is solved by spectral methods (Laplacian eigenanalysis) and repeated bisection to produce K leaves (target 16 cuts for n=1000 illustrative universe). For the discrete combinatorial selection inside leaves the problem is reduced to a constrained combinatorial search (e.g., select 3 from top-5 per leaf) and mapped to a weighted MAX-CUT / Ising Hamiltonian by substituting binary variables x_i -> (1 - Z_i)/2. The experimental evaluation uses IBM Qiskit tooling to (a) construct the Ising Hamiltonian via the Qiskit optimization module, and (b) compare three solution approaches on small subuniverses: brute-force enumeration, a classical eigensolver-based approach, and a simulated quantum approach (Qiskit qasm_simulator). The experiments vary subuniverse size (starting at 12 assets and increasing up to 25) and measure elapsed runtimes for the Hamiltonian sampling step (max_cut.sample_most_likely). Practical implementation details include eigenvector selection heuristics (e.g., choose 17 eigenvectors out of 34 candidates, drop near-identical eigenvectors), memory/back-end engineering (initial use of aer statevector crashed due to upfront large allocation, thereafter qasm_simulator allocated memory on demand), and normalization of timing data for regression analysis. The study reports per-size runtimes, computes speedups relative to the 12-asset baseline, fits exponential growth models to the runtimes, and discusses scaling strategies (spectral subspace reduction, projected searches in m<<n dimensions) and future R&D directions (implied graphs, GNN embeddings, heuristics for incremental updates).

**Algorithms used:** Spectral graph cut / normalized cut (graph Laplacian eigenanalysis), Repeated bisection for multi-way partitioning, Graph construction via absolute correlation and GICS mixing (kappa * vij + (1-kappa) * wij), Eigenvalue decomposition (classical eigensolver), Brute-force enumeration (classical baseline), MAX-CUT mapping to Ising Hamiltonian, Quantum simulation of Ising Hamiltonian sampling (Qiskit qasm_simulator), Graph Neural Network (GNN) (discussed as application/future use), Cholesky decomposition / implied graph (discussed)
**Frameworks:** Qiskit (IBM Qiskit optimization module), qiskit-aer (aer_simulations_statevector - attempted), qiskit-aer qasm_simulator (used), ibmq_qasm_simulator (IBM cloud simulator - considered), cuQuantum (NVIDIA - attempted/considered), TensorFlow (mentioned as dependency in cuQuantum sample)

**Experimental setup:** Experiments run on an AWS t2.large VM (2 vCPU, 8 GB RAM). Quantum experiments were executed using Qiskit simulators: initially attempted aer statevector (crashed due to upfront full-state allocation), then switched to the qasm_simulator local backend which allocates memory on demand. IBM cloud ibmq_qasm_simulator was considered but not used due to queueing delays. Runtime measurements were taken for the Hamiltonian sampling step in Qiskit (max_cut.sample_most_likely). The experiments varied the number of assets in the subuniverse (12,13,14,15,18,21,24,25).

**Dataset:** A collected dataset of 945 large-cap US equities with 832 daily closing prices spanning Jan 2, 2020 to Apr 21, 2023. Stored fields include product id, product name, cob date, CLOSE price, SECTOR ID, and sub sector ID (GICS-like taxonomy). For algorithmic illustration the paper also discusses Russell 1000 (n=1000) examples, but the computational experiments used the 945-equity sample.

### Reproducibility
The paper does not provide public code or a public link to the specific dataset used. The experiments rely on standard Qiskit modules (Qiskit optimization and qiskit-aer qasm_simulator) and an internally collected equities dataset (945 tickers x 832 closing prices, 2020-01-02 to 2023-04-21). Reproduction would require (1) access to an equivalent price dataset and GICS-like sector IDs, (2) Qiskit and qiskit-aer installation, and (3) sufficient local memory or use of IBM cloud simulators/QPU. Practical reproducibility is limited by memory/back-end choices (the aer statevector backend caused crashes in the authors' 8 GB VM) and by the lack of explicit scripts, parameter files, and data sharing. The authors reference Qiskit tutorials (max-cut examples) which provide general guidance, but the exact experimental scripts and processed data are not published with the conference paper.
## Findings
- [supported] The authors implemented a portfolio 'graph-cut' approach (normalized cut via graph Laplacian eigenvectors) and mapped portfolio selection to a MAX-CUT / Ising formulation suitable for quantum optimization.
- [supported] Empirical runtimes (on a classical machine and IBM Qiskit backends) were measured: Hamiltonian step max_cut.sample_most_likely elapsed times (example): 10 assets 0.34s, 12 assets 0.34s, 15 assets 0.64s, 18 assets 2.44s, 21 assets 27.79s, 24 assets 325.94s, 25 assets 730.38s.
- [supported] Classical brute-force and eigensolver approaches were timed and produce similar scaling for the tested instance sizes (e.g., for 24 assets: brute-force 7050.94s, eigensolver 8524.13s), suggesting eigensolver gave limited run-time improvement in these experiments.
- [supported] The IBM statevector simulator (aer_simulations_statevector) exhausted local memory; the qasm_simulator (on local machine) was more memory-friendly and used for larger runs.
- [supported] Mixing fundamental sector adjacency (GICS) and absolute return correlations via a parameter κ changes the resulting cuts: κ=1 returns pure GICS cuts, κ≈0.9 still dominated by GICS, κ≈0.3 yields mixes influenced by correlations.
- [supported] For n≈1000 assets the authors report that keeping about 17 eigenvectors (from a set of ≈34 smooth eigenvectors) and sampling a few hundred combinations yields practical portfolio cuts meeting their size heuristics (max cut ≤125).
- [supported] Reducing the combinatorial search via domain heuristics (e.g., selecting 3 of top-5 picks per cut across 16 cuts) reduces possible portfolios from astronomic counts (∼1.6×10^150) to tractable counts (e.g., (5 choose 3)^16 ≈ 10^16).
- [speculative] The paper asserts that generating correlated random vectors is trivial on a quantum computer and can provide dramatic speedups for correlated sampling relative to classical approaches.
- [speculative] The authors propose that a real quantum computer (as opposed to a simulator) could reduce graph-cut computation for industrial-sized problems from hours/days to minutes/seconds and claim hardware would be thousands-to-millions times faster than simulators.
- [speculative] The authors fit an empirical growth model for the 'quantum approach' on the measured simulation data: ln(speed/base) ≈ 4(N−12) implying exponential growth of speed with N; they contrast this with an asserted O(e^{9N}) growth for the classical methods.
- [disputed] The paper claims 'at n = 24 the absolute time required for solving the problem on the quantum simulator begins to catch up with that for the brute-force algorithm', but the presented timings (QASM Sim 9222.86s vs Brute Force 7050.94s at 24 assets) do not support this statement.
- [speculative] The authors propose a practical hybrid strategy: compute only top 10–30 eigenpairs (O(n^3) cost), project to a reduced dimension m (≈10–25) and then search (2^m), yielding combined complexity O(n^3) + O(2^m) and tractable search for realistic m.
- [speculative] The paper outlines further R&D directions (implied graphs via Cholesky/SVD, embedding with GNNs, heuristics for incremental GNN updates via functional derivatives) as promising but not empirically validated in this work.

**Results summary:** This conference paper implements a graph-cut formulation for portfolio selection, maps it to an Ising/MAX-CUT problem, and runs comparative experiments using classical brute-force, eigensolver, and IBM Qiskit quantum simulators on small subproblems (up to 25 assets) drawn from a 945-stock dataset. Empirical measurements show rapid runtime growth and memory limits on simulators; the classical eigensolver showed limited improvement over brute force for the tested sizes. The quantum simulator runs were substantially slower than classical methods for the tested instances (simulator overhead and memory constraints), and the authors therefore present several speculative strategies (spectral projection, reduced subspace search, and eventual use of real quantum hardware) as paths to practical quantum advantage for larger industrial instances.

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** speculative

The paper presents a theoretical mapping of the finance combinatorial problem to quantum formulations and reports simulator experiments, but empirical simulator results do not demonstrate a speed advantage (simulator overhead and memory limits made the simulator slower than classical methods for tested sizes). Claims of large practical advantage rely on expectations about future or remote quantum hardware (thousands-to-millions× speedups) and on proposed dimensionality-reduction strategies; these remain speculative and were not empirically validated on real quantum processors in the paper.
## Limitations
- Experiments used quantum simulators (qasm_simulator) rather than execution on real quantum hardware; absolute simulator runtimes are not representative of real-hardware speedups.
- Empirical tests were limited to very small sub-problems (n ≤ 25) due to local machine memory constraints (AWS t2.large: 8 GB RAM, 2 vCPUs); full-scale experiments (n ~ 500) were not executed.
- Out-of-memory crashes encountered with older statevector simulator backend (aer_simulations_statevector) and practical upper limits on simulator size.
- Recorded runtimes and scaling behavior are from hybrid classical–simulator workflows on modest classical hardware, so extrapolation to production scale and real quantum devices is uncertain.
- No runs or performance/error-rate analysis on noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) hardware; hardware noise, error rates, and error mitigation effects are not addressed.
- No rigorous convergence or approximation bounds were derived for the quantumized random-sampling approach; applicability of classical combinatorial bounds to quantum equivalents remains unproven.
- Eigensolver approach yielded limited improvement over brute-force in the reported experiments, likely due to large-matrix inversion and exponential search components.
- Selection of spectral subspace/eigenvectors (heuristic choice of 17 out of 34) and sampling of only a tiny fraction of combinations (hundreds vs billions) lacks formal guarantees of solution quality.
- The GNN retraining requirement is recognized as impractical in continuous real-time streaming; the proposed Taylor-series based approximation is only valid for small input changes.
- Mixing parameter κ between fundamental sector info (GICS) and statistical correlations is heuristic; sensitivity and robustness to κ are not theoretically characterized.
- Queueing delays and resource management issues when using cloud quantum backends (ibmq_qasm_simulator) were noted as operational limitations.
- Compatibility and integration issues between Nvidia cuQuantum sample code and specific TensorFlow versions / AWS SageMaker environments prevented immediate GPU-based scaling.
- Current state-of-the-art quantum hardware qubit counts (cited 127–433 qubits) may be insufficient or impractical (given noise) for direct mapping of full-sized (n ~ hundreds) instances without problem reduction.

- [inferred] Cost, latency, and operational overhead (e.g., quantum back-end subscription cost and queue times) are not quantified and may be barriers for industrial adoption.

**Future work:**
- Develop and analyze smart searches within compressed, reduced spectral subspaces (project high-dimensional problem onto lower-dimensional manifold) to trade off tractability and approximation error; derive error bounds (analogs to Christofides) for these projections.
- Investigate Implied Graph constructions (best-fit graphs via reverse optimization on data) using Cholesky decompositions or SVD for directed graphs, and assess their utility for visualization and dimensionality reduction.

## Contradictions
- The paper reports simulated speedups on small subproblems but the experiments are simulator-only and limited to N ≤ 25, which contradicts any strong claim that the approach currently scales to real-world portfolio sizes (n ≈ 1000); authors explicitly note memory/backend and scaling challenges.
- While the paper frames quantum sampling as competitive, the comparison against classical baselines (brute-force and eigensolver) is performed on very small instances and using a simulator, leaving open the possibility that classical methods remain superior in practice for larger or production problems.
