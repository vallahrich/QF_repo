## Abstract summary
The paper introduces quantum algorithms to compute market risk (greeks) of financial derivatives, extending quantum amplitude estimation to achieve quadratic error scaling and applying quantum gradient estimation (Jordan/GAW) to obtain further quadratic improvement in the number of sensitivities. The authors propose a simulation-free second-order method (SFQG), use maximum likelihood postprocessing to improve estimates, and numerically demonstrate these approaches on European and path-dependent basket options, reporting substantially reduced resource estimates (e.g., lowering required logical clock rates).
## Methodology
The authors develop and evaluate quantum algorithms to compute gradients (greeks) of financial derivative prices. They build on Quantum Amplitude Estimation (QAE) and quantum gradient estimation techniques (Jordan's algorithm and the Gilyén-Arunachalam-Wiebe (GAW) framework) and introduce a new second-order, simulation-free construction (SFQG) tailored to derivative pricing. Two oracle construction approaches are considered: (i) block-encoding combined with coherent Hamiltonian simulation to implement fractional phase oracles from the QAE probability oracle, and (ii) a Simulation-Free Quantum Gradient (SFQG) m=1 construction that leverages the structure of the pricing amplitude-estimation oracle to implement the required phase kickback without Hamiltonian simulation. The authors numerically emulate the quantum gradient algorithms for two representative financial instances (a Black–Scholes European call and a path-dependent 3-asset basket option with a knock-in barrier). Emulation is performed by classically computing the amplitude arrays for the phase oracles over a discrete hypercube of input perturbations and applying classical inverse Fourier transforms to produce the output measurement distributions; phase simulation error is modeled by adding uniform noise in [-ϵ_phase, ϵ_phase]. They compare query complexities (oracle call counts) and resource estimates against classical finite-difference (with and without common random numbers) and a semi-classical quantum finite-difference method, and apply maximum-likelihood estimation (MLE) post-processing to sampled measurement outcomes to produce point estimates and confidence intervals. Finally, they map algorithmic costs to fault-tolerant resource metrics (T-depth, logical qubits) using previously published reparameterization resource models to estimate logical clock-rate requirements for advantage.

**Algorithms used:** Quantum Amplitude Estimation (QAE), Jordan's quantum gradient algorithm, GAW quantum gradient algorithm (Gilyén-Arunachalam-Wiebe), Simulation-Free Quantum Gradient (SFQG, m=1), Block-encoding + Hamiltonian simulation (coherent), Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) post-processing, Classical Monte Carlo (for benchmarks), Finite-difference (forward and central) with and without Common Random Numbers (CRN), Automatic Differentiation (conceptual quantum AD construction, Appendix B)

**Experimental setup:** All experiments are classical numerical emulations/simulations of the quantum algorithms. Emulation method: classically compute k * 2^n amplitude arrays for the phase oracle (Eq. (18)) for chosen m and spacing l; add uniform phase noise to model Hamiltonian simulation error; apply a classical inverse discrete Fourier transform to emulate the quantum inverse QFT; sample the resulting discrete probability distribution to emulate measurement outcomes; perform MLE post-processing on sampled outcomes to obtain gradient estimates and confidence intervals. No quantum hardware or specific quantum simulator software is reported.

**Dataset:** Synthetic financial data and models: (1) European (vanilla) call option priced under Black–Scholes–Merton model with chosen parameter point (S, r, σ, T) = (99.5, 1%, 20%, 0.1), K = 100; (2) Path-dependent basket option on 3 assets undergoing Geometric Brownian Motion (σ1=20%, σ2=20%, σ3=10%), spot prices (2.0,2.0,2.0), weights (0.5,0.3,0.2), observed at five times, strike K=1.0, knock-in barrier B=2.5, expiry T=3 years. Classical Monte Carlo (1e6 paths) used to generate benchmark option prices/greeks for the path-dependent basket.
## Experiment details
### Input
Source: synthetic/generated from analytic Black–Scholes formula (vanilla) and from simulated Geometric Brownian Motion paths (basket). Sizes: for basket option benchmarking, classical Monte Carlo with 1,000,000 paths used to produce reference prices and finite-difference greeks. Quantum-emulation inputs: discretization hypercubes of edge length l around evaluation point; N = 2^n points per dimension (n qubits per dimension). Example parameter choices used in simulations: vanilla: target gradient error ϵ = 2e-2, n = 6 qubits; basket: target error ϵ <= 0.0625, n = 4 qubits, m (central-difference order) varied (m=1..4) and optimized numerically. Preprocessing: discretize parameter perturbation hypercube, compute payoffs g(s, x) per scenario and probabilities p_j; compute phases θ(x)=arcsin(sqrt(f(x))) when needed; add uniform phase noise in [-ϵ_phase, ϵ_phase] to emulate Hamiltonian-simulation-induced phase error.

### Process
1) Define financial function f(x) (price) and target gradient components (greeks). 2) Build probability oracle A(x) (amplitude-encoding of payoffs) following the reparameterization approach from prior work. 3) Two phase-oracle construction approaches: (a) block-encoding U = A†(Z ⊗ I)A and apply coherent Hamiltonian simulation to implement fractional phase oracle OSf (cost per Eq. (24)-(25)); (b) SFQG (m=1): build O+ and O- using A evaluated at ±x (requires separate registers), apply controlled Q = A S0 A† Sψ0 repeatedly (πS times) to produce phases e^{±2πiSθ(x)}, then inverse QFT on input-registers and post-select on non-discarded measurements (50% success for SFQG's m=1 construction). 4) Emulation: classically compute the complex amplitudes for the chosen m, l over the discrete grid (k * 2^n array), add uniform phase noise to each phase to simulate ϵ_phase, then apply classical inverse discrete Fourier transform to obtain the outcome probability distribution. 5) Sample the emulated distribution M times (sampling counts chosen e.g., 30; doubled to 60 when SFQG discards 50% of shots) to produce measurement results. 6) Apply Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) on the sampled outcomes to obtain continuous gradient estimates and likelihood-based confidence intervals. 7) Compare oracle-call complexity (No from Eq. (25)), success probability, and resource counts versus baselines: classical finite-difference (CFD), CFD with common random numbers (CFD-CRN), and semi-classical quantum finite-difference (SQG). 8) Map chosen circuit/oracle costs to fault-tolerant resources (T-depth, logical qubits) using reparameterization cost models from Chakrabarti et al., and compute logical clock-rate requirements for parity with classical runtimes.

### Output
Outputs are discrete probability distributions over gradient estimates (per-dimension) from the emulated inverse QFT, sampled measurement outcomes, MLE point estimates for each gradient component and associated confidence intervals (likelihood-ratio-based). Performance metrics reported: oracle-call counts No, success probability (e.g., >=85%), required central-difference order m and spacing l, target gradient absolute error ϵ, Hamiltonian simulation phase error ϵ_phase used in simulations (e.g., 1e-4), comparisons to classical Monte Carlo path counts required for CFD and CFD-CRN, and fault-tolerant resource estimates (T-depth, logical qubit count) and resulting logical clock-rate requirements for parity with classical runtime baselines.

## Findings
- [speculative] Quantum amplitude estimation (QAE) yields a quadratic speedup in derivative pricing error scaling (O(1/ϵ) vs classical O(1/ϵ^2)).
- [speculative] That QAE-based acceleration for pricing can be extended to give a quadratic error-scaling advantage for market risk (gradients / greeks).
- [speculative] Quantum gradient algorithms (Jordan / GAW framework) can provide an additional quadratic improvement in the dependence on the number k of greeks (runtime scaling O(√k/ϵ) under smoothness assumptions).
- [supported] Numerical simulations of GAW-style gradient estimation on representative financial payoffs (European call; a path-dependent basket option with knock-in) successfully recovered greeks to target accuracies in the case studies.
- [supported] For the concrete case studies simulated, the empirical oracle/query complexity needed was much smaller (orders of magnitude) than the worst-case theoretical bounds used in existing proofs (the authors report e.g. ~125× and ~200× reductions in certain examples).
- [supported] They introduce a Simulation-Free Quantum Gradient (SFQG) construction for the m=1 (second-order) central-difference oracle that avoids block-encoding + Hamiltonian simulation, and it is cheaper to implement (numerically demonstrated on the basket option example).
- [supported] Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) post-processing of repeated circuit samples improves the gradient estimation (provides tighter estimates and concrete confidence intervals) compared to median-of-repetitions; using MLE allows smaller N (fewer Fourier qubits) and more samples.
- [supported] Concrete resource estimates: for the basket-option example and chosen parameters, SFQG + MLE gave small query counts (e.g., SFQG No ≈ 201; semi-classical No ≈ 256; numerically tuned GAW No ≈ 1600) and outperformed classical finite-difference Monte Carlo baselines in the examples (classical CFD-CRN and CFD required many more MC paths in that setting).
- [supported] Updated end-to-end resource estimate: calculating four greeks for the basket example to ϵ ≤ 2×10^-3 with their SFQG+MLE parameters leads to an overall T-depth × shots ≈ 5.5×10^7 and ≈12k logical qubits (authors' estimate).
- [supported] Based on the above, the authors estimate the logical quantum clock rate required for quantum advantage on such risk computations could be ≈7 MHz (a ~7× reduction from a previously cited 50 MHz estimate for pricing-only tasks), and that parallelization across up to 60 QPUs could reduce per-device logical clock to ≈100 kHz.
- [speculative] For classes of very smooth functions (Gevrey-type bounds assumed in GAW theorem) choosing higher-order central-difference (m ~ log(√k/ϵ)) can achieve the O(√k/ϵ) scaling; for even smoother or polynomial cases further improvements in k-dependence are possible in principle.
- [speculative] Combining quantum automatic-differentiation-like constructs (appendix) with multi-objective QAE could in some settings yield gradient readout whose runtime is independent of k (up to normalization issues), analogous to classical adjoint/AD speedups.
- [speculative] The practical quantum advantage (and the magnitude of speedups) will be highly dependent on the specific derivative payoffs and their smoothness / higher-derivative structure in the region of evaluation.

**Results summary:** This preprint studies quantum algorithms for computing market risk (greeks) of financial derivatives, extending QAE-based pricing speedups to gradient estimation. The authors analyze high-order quantum gradient methods (Jordan / GAW), introduce a practical second-order 'Simulation-Free Quantum Gradient' (SFQG) oracle that avoids block-encoding/Hamiltonian simulation overhead, and apply Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) post-processing. Numerical simulations on a vanilla European call and a representative path-dependent basket option show that the tuned quantum-gradient implementations can estimate greeks with far fewer oracle calls than worst-case theoretical bounds, and can be cheaper than classical finite-difference Monte Carlo baselines in these examples. Updated end-to-end resource estimates suggest a reduction in the required logical clock rate for achieving quantum advantage in these risk computations (from a referenced 50 MHz to ~7 MHz for the studied case) and potential gains from parallelizing across many QPUs.

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** speculative

The paper provides theoretical arguments and numerical simulations suggesting quantum algorithms (QAE combined with quantum gradient methods and the SFQG construction) can reduce asymptotic and practical costs for computing greeks versus classical Monte Carlo finite-difference in specific examples, and updates resource estimates (e.g., lowering required logical clock rates). However, these are numerical and resource-estimate results rather than experimental demonstrations on quantum hardware; the claimed advantage depends on model smoothness, chosen parameters, and significant fault-tolerant quantum resources, so the advantage remains speculative/theoretical rather than demonstrated.
## Limitations
- Theoretical guarantees (GAW Theorem 1) rely on strong smoothness assumptions (Gevrey class bound, Eq. (20)) which may not hold for many real-world derivative price functions.
- Numerical evaluation of the GAW algorithm was limited to small instances (practical simulations limited to k ≤ 4 and small n) due to exponential cost of classical emulation, so empirical scaling to large k remains untested.
- Block-encoding plus Hamiltonian simulation required to produce general phase oracles incur significant overheads (including post-selection in some approaches) which increases resource requirements substantially.
- The presented Simulation-Free Quantum Gradient (SFQG) construction is only explicit for second-order (m = 1); extensions to higher-order m > 1 are not provided.
- SFQG (m = 1) produces additional terms that cause 50% of measurement shots to be discarded (unless accounted for in post-processing), increasing the effective shot cost.
- Construction of the phase-kickback relies on a particular structure of the amplitude-estimation A operator (Section A); if probabilities pi or other registers are x-dependent or entangled, phase kickback can fail.
- Some resource estimates use optimistic / illustrative parameter choices (e.g., smoothness constant c = 1, phase-error ϵ_phase = 10^-4) whose validity for general financial contracts is not established.
- The chain-rule approach (computing gradients of θ then mapping to gradients of f) requires an independent accurate estimate of θ(x0) (i.e. a separate QAE evaluation) which adds resource cost.
- MLE post-processing is used to reduce repetitions (log factors) but the classical computational cost of this post-processing (and its scaling) is not fully quantified in the overall complexity analysis.
- Comparisons with classical automatic differentiation (AAD/A.D.) are preliminary; a full resource- and performance-level comparison (including memory/qubit trade-offs) is not provided.
- Resource estimates for quantum advantage (clock rates, T-depth, logical qubits) assume availability of large fault-tolerant QPUs and error-correction overheads; practical feasibility and full hardware-stack implications remain uncertain.
- Asymptotic bounds from the theory (Theorem 1) appear loose compared to the empirical numerical results, indicating uncertainty in how theoretical complexity translates to practical instances.
- [inferred] The numerical examples considered (vanilla option and a specific path-dependent basket option) may not be representative of the full diversity of derivatives used in practice, limiting generalizability of conclusions.
- [inferred] The SFQG method's requirement that πS = πN/(D l) be an integer (and the need to pick D accordingly) can constrain parameter choices and complicate implementation.
- [inferred] The multi-objective QAE / AD hybrid (Appendix B) trades off runtime independence of k against increased qubit and arithmetic resources; the precise memory (qubit) overheads and practical limits are not quantified.
- [inferred] Parallelization across many QPUs (e.g., up to 60) is assumed feasible, but practical orchestration, data movement and cross-device overheads are not analyzed.
## Open questions
- Which and how many practical financial derivative price functions satisfy the smoothness conditions required for the O(√k/ϵ) GAW scaling?
- Can the SFQG construction be extended to higher-order central-difference schemes (m > 1) without resorting to costly block-encoding and Hamiltonian simulation?
- How does the GAW quantum gradient algorithm scale empirically for larger k (hundreds to thousands), especially for common industry derivatives?
- How best to construct phase oracles for pricing functions in practice with minimal overhead (avoiding or reducing block-encoding / Hamiltonian simulation costs)?
- What are the precise qubit (memory) and gate-depth trade-offs when combining automatic differentiation (quantum-arithmetic AD) with multi-objective QAE for gradient estimation?
- What are the optimal parameter choices (e.g., central-difference order m, spacing l, Hamiltonian simulation error ϵ_phase, β in coherent simulation) in realistic instances, and how sensitive are resource estimates to these choices?
- How to reduce or eliminate the 50% measurement-discard / post-selection issue in the SFQG m = 1 construction, or more generally, how to handle the undesired eigenstate components efficiently?
- What is the full classical/quantum end-to-end cost (including MLE post-processing and any required classical pre/post work) and how does it compare to best classical approaches like AAD in realistic production settings?
- How robust are the quantum algorithms to model features common in finance (piecewise-linear payoffs, discontinuities, barriers, path-dependence, correlated multi-asset dynamics)?
- Can other important risk metrics (e.g., portfolio VaR, expected shortfall) be accelerated by similar quantum-gradient/QAE techniques, and what are the resource implications?
- How practical is the assumed parallelization across many QPUs (e.g., up to 60) in real deployment — what overheads and limitations arise in multi-device execution?
- How do hardware constraints (error correction overhead, achievable logical clock rates, connectivity) affect the threshold for quantum advantage in market-risk computations?
- [inferred] What are the impacts of entanglement between input registers and auxiliary registers on the phase-kickback mechanism in more complex pricing oracles?

**Future work:**
- Investigate whether the SFQG method can be generalized to construct higher-order phase oracles (m > 1) without block-encoding and Hamiltonian simulation.
- Perform detailed empirical studies of GAW and SFQG algorithms on larger and more diverse financial derivatives to assess practical scaling with k and ϵ.
- Develop and benchmark practical constructions of phase oracles for pricing oracles that minimize Hamiltonian-simulation overhead and post-selection costs.
- Carry out a thorough comparison between quantum gradient algorithms and classical automatic/adjoint differentiation methods (AAD/AD), including qubit/memory trade-offs and end-to-end costs.
- Explore improvements to the SFQG approach to reduce shot discard rates or integrate more efficient post-processing (e.g., refined MLE strategies).
- Characterize the smoothness properties of a wide range of financial price functions to determine applicability of theoretical guarantees like Theorem 1.
- Extend resource estimation to larger portfolio-level risk tasks (e.g., VaR, portfolio greeks) and study opportunities for further quantum advantage at higher aggregation levels.
- Investigate quantum implementations of automatic differentiation (reversible quantum arithmetic AD) and quantify their memory and runtime costs; automate AD for quantum circuits.
- Refine and validate hardware-level resource and clock-rate estimates under more conservative/realistic error-correction assumptions and multi-QPU orchestration costs.
- Study robustness of the phase-kickback and oracle constructions under more general oracle structures (entangled registers, x-dependent probability loading).
