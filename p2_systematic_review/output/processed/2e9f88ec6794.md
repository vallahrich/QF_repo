## Abstract summary
The paper develops quantum algorithms to approximate values in Lasserre's sum-of-squares (SOS) hierarchy for polynomial optimization by converting SOS relaxations into normalized SDPs and applying matrix multiplicative-weights / Hamiltonian-updates techniques with efficient block-encodings (no QRAM). Under natural structural and scaling assumptions the authors prove runtime bounds that for fixed relaxation order k give super-quadratic speedups in the problem dimension versus classical SDP methods; as an example they obtain an improved quantum algorithm for portfolio optimization with runtime O(n eps^{-4} + sqrt(n) eps^{-5}).
## Methodology
This is a theoretical algorithmic study that develops quantum algorithms to approximate values of Lasserre (sum-of-squares / moment) SDP relaxations of polynomial optimization problems. The authors transform SOS relaxations into a normalized standard SDP form with bounded (or constant) trace via coefficient rescaling and problem-dependent embeddings. They then apply a binary-search outer loop combined with a matrix-multiplicative-weights style feasibility solver called Hamiltonian updates (a quantum-friendly variant leveraging quantum Gibbs sampling and gentle measurement subroutines) to decide feasibility at each candidate objective value. The work provides explicit constructions of block-encodings for the constraint coefficient-matching matrices (B(γ) and block-diagonal B(γ)_g) without assuming QRAM, using reversible arithmetic and simple controlled-swap operations, and shows how to implement linear combinations of those unitaries (LCU) when needed. The paper analyzes sparsity (per-row nonzeros) and trace bounds of the converted SDPs, proves stability/error bounds relating SDP dual variables to approximation error, and produces detailed runtime complexity estimates for classical and quantum settings under various structural assumptions: (i) unconstrained POPs with exactness and an ℓ1-ball bound on optimizers, (ii) inequality-constrained POPs (including ball and simplex cases) with coefficient rescaling, and (iii) applications such as first-order SOS relaxation of portfolio optimization. The methodology is analytical and proof-based; no empirical simulations or experiments are reported.

**Algorithms used:** Matrix Multiplicative Weights (Hamiltonian updates), Binary search over objective value (outer loop), Lasserre's sum-of-squares (SOS) hierarchy, Moment relaxations (dual SDP), Block-encoding construction (projected-unitary + LCU), Quantum Gibbs sampling (as subroutine in Hamiltonian updates / quantum MMW), Classical preprocessing: Gaussian elimination and Gram–Schmidt

**Results summary:** This preprint develops and analyses quantum algorithms (based on matrix multiplicative weights / Hamiltonian updates with Gibbs sampling subroutines) for approximating Lasserre (sum-of-squares) SDP relaxations of polynomial optimization problems. By rescaling coefficients and exploiting structural properties (bounded-trace feasible region, sparsity of coefficient-matching matrices, and domain constraints such as balls or simplices), the authors derive explicit runtime and additive-accuracy bounds for several problem classes (unconstrained, inequality-constrained, simplex/ball-constrained). The central message is that, under those structural assumptions and for fixed relaxation order k, their quantum approach can achieve super-quadratic speedups in the problem dimension relative to classical interior-point or MMW-based methods, at the expense of a worse polynomial dependence on precision ε and dependence on condition numbers and model assumptions. They present a concrete portfolio-optimization example (first-order relaxation) with a stated quantum runtime O(n ε^{-4} + √n ε^{-5}), and they show how to construct the block encodings of the SOS coefficient-matching matrices without QRAM.

**Performance claims:**
- Unconstrained SOS (Algorithm 3): quantum runtime O(((n+2k choose n)^{1/2} + (1/ε)(n+k choose n)^{1/2}) * ε^{-4}) to approximate λ_k with additive accuracy ≲ ε/(1−r) under exactness and optimizer ℓ1-radius r (Section 4.1, Lemma 22 / Thm 1).
- Inequality-constrained SOS (Algorithm 4): quantum runtime O(s_g * ((n+2k choose n)^{1/2} + ( Σ_{i=0}^m (n+k−d_i choose n) )^{1/2} * (1/ε)) * ε^{-4}) with accuracy ≲ ε/(1−r) under exactness and optimizer ℓ1-radius r; s_g is sparsity of coefficient-matching rows (Section 4.2, Lemma 23 / Thm 2).
- Simplex / nonnegative orthant variant (Algorithm 5): same family of runtimes as inequality-constrained case, with additive accuracy bounds ≲ ε/(1−r) under simplex inclusion assumptions (Theorems 5–6).
- Portfolio optimization application (k=1): quantum runtime claim O(n ε^{-4} + √n ε^{-5}) to approximate optimal value to additive ε; classical interior-point comparison O(n^{ω+1} log(1/ε)) (Theorem 7).
- General Hamiltonian-updates quantum solver complexity (cited/refined): O( s * (√M + √N ε^{-1}) * ε^{-4} ), where s = max nonzeros per row, M = number of constraints, N = matrix dimension (Section 2.1 / Lemma 15).
- Classical MMW/binary-search complexity reference: O((M s N + N^{ω}) ε^{-2}) (Arora–Kale style) (Table 1 / Section 3.3).
- Accuracy bound for binary-search + Hamiltonian updates under strong duality: additive error ≤ ε (2 + ||ξ*||_1) where ξ* is a dual solution; specialized bounds replace ||ξ*||_1 by problem-dependent quantities (Lemma 14 and subsequent theorems).
- Block-encoding construction claim: coefficient-matching matrices B(γ) admit block-encodings without QRAM using O(n log k) quantum gates (Appendix F / Proposition 3).
- Positive lower bounds on smallest eigenvalues of moment/localizing matrices (used to justify rescalings): specific constructive bounds for Lebesgue measure on [0,1]^n given (Proposition 1 and Proposition 2 in Appendix C).
## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** theoretical

The paper proves theoretical runtime/accuracy bounds showing potential super-quadratic speedups in problem dimension (n-dependence) for computing fixed-order SOS relaxations under structural assumptions (bounded/constant-trace after rescaling, sparsity of coefficient-matching matrices, exactness of the relaxation or domain constraints like simplex/ball). These are analytical/theoretical results (no empirical evidence). The advantage trades off worse dependence on precision ε (high-degree polynomial in 1/ε) and depends on additional assumptions (existence of well-conditioned Slater points, availability of efficient block-encodings, sparsity), so the quantum speedup is a conditional, theoretical claim rather than an experimentally demonstrated advantage.
## Limitations
- Strong dependence on precision: the Hamiltonian-updates-based quantum algorithms have polynomial (often high-degree) dependence on 1/ε (e.g. ε^{-4}, ε^{-5}), which is much worse than interior-point methods' logarithmic dependence on 1/ε (author-stated).
- Many guarantees require structural assumptions such as exactness of the relaxation (λ_k = f*), or that the global optimum lies in a small ℓ1-ball (∥x*∥_{ℓ1} ≤ r < 1), or that S(g) lies in a simplex and corresponding constraints are present (author-stated).
- Results rely on Slater/strong duality conditions or the existence of strictly feasible (Slater) points (author-stated); several bounds depend on the condition number κ of such a Slater point.
- Some key bounds need a positive lower bound on the smallest eigenvalue of moment/localizing matrices associated with a supporting measure µ (Assumptions 1/2); establishing such bounds may require rescaling coefficients (author-stated).
- The quantum speedups require input sparsity assumptions (sg small) and efficient block-encodings of constraint/objective matrices; if the coefficient-matching matrices are not sparse the asymptotic advantages vanish (author-stated / implied).
- The SDP sizes and number of constraints still grow combinatorially in n and relaxation order k; although exponents are reduced by the quantum approach, exponential (or very large polynomial) scaling in n,k remains (author-stated).
- [inferred] Dependence on Slater point condition number κ: many accuracy bounds scale with κ. The paper notes that finding a Slater point is generally as hard as solving the SDP, and κ may grow with problem size, making those bounds uninformative in practice.
- [inferred] Practical data-access assumptions: although block encodings are constructed without QRAM for their specific matrices, the approach still assumes efficient oracle/access to polynomial coefficients and that the presented block-encoding constructions are implementable with low overhead and low noise in realistic quantum hardware.
- [inferred] Some favorable regimes require potentially expensive coefficient rescaling and normalization (to satisfy Assumptions 1–3); the cost and numerical effects of these rescalings are not fully characterized.
- [inferred] Certain structural assumptions (e.g., including all monomials x^α or many simplex-style constraints among the gis) can entail an exponential number of constraints; while the authors propose relaxations to mitigate this, those relaxations may degrade bounds and the tradeoffs are not fully explored.
- [inferred] For results that depend on combinatorial constants (e.g., LCM bounds or λ_min lower bounds computed via factorials), those constants grow extremely fast and lead to poor numerical guarantees for moderate-to-large n,k; asymptotic behaviours (e.g. of L_k,n for n≥2) are not known.
## Open questions

- What is the practical scaling of the Slater-point condition number κ with problem size for the relevant SDP relaxations, and can one efficiently construct or certify Slater points with controlled κ?
- How to obtain tighter, practically useful lower bounds for the smallest eigenvalues of moment and localizing matrices for general measures and higher dimensions (the paper gives some bounds but notes limitations and unknown asymptotics)?
- What are the concrete problem-instance thresholds (in n, k, sparsity sg, and ε) where quantum algorithms provide end-to-end advantages over classical methods, considering gate counts, noise, and overheads?
- How robust are the algorithms to realistic noise, finite sampling error in Gibbs sampling subroutines, and imprecise block encodings on near-term or noisy quantum hardware? (inference from reliance on quantum subroutines).
- Can alternative semidefinite relaxations (DSOS/SDSOS, bounded-degree moment relaxations) be combined with quantum SDP solvers to achieve better ε-scaling or lower overhead while preserving useful bounds (explicitly suggested)?
- What are the tradeoffs between introducing extra constraints (to get favorable structure or bounded-trace conditions) versus the resulting blow-up in M or N in practical settings?
- For the constrained variants (e.g., simplex inclusion requiring many constraints), what is the best way to avoid exponential constraint explosion while retaining tight bounds on the dual norms used in accuracy guarantees?
- How do the block-encoding constructions and associated gate counts behave in practice when polynomials have large supports or when coefficients are high-precision rationals?

- #limitation:data-encoding — Although QRAM is avoided, the approach requires explicit, potentially costly block-encoding constructions and sparsity/structure assumptions for coefficient matrices; these data-encoding/implementation costs are central to the claimed speedups.
- #idea:quantum-advantage — The proven speedups trade improved dependence on n for worse polynomial dependence on precision ε (dependence up to poly(1/ε^5)) and some dependence on condition numbers/Slater parameters.

## Contradictions
- #contradiction:scalability — The paper claims super-quadratic speedups in problem dimension n for fixed k, but also admits polynomially worse scaling in precision (poly(1/ε^4–5)) and dependence on condition numbers and structural assumptions (sparsity, bounded-trace). These trade-offs call into question whether the asymptotic n-speedup yields practical advantages for realistic precision and ill-conditioned instances.
- #contradiction:classical-vs-quantum — The authors compare their quantum asymptotics to classical interior-point/MMW complexities and report improvements under their assumptions, yet the lack of empirical benchmarks, large precision overheads, and nontrivial block-encoding costs mean the claimed superiority over classical methods is theoretical and contingent on strong problem structure; practical superiority remains unverified.
