## Abstract summary
This review examines applications of quantum computing to finance, focusing on portfolio optimization, derivative pricing, Monte Carlo methods and risk analysis, and argues that quantum algorithms (e.g., quantum annealing and amplitude estimation) can deliver substantial speedups and improved accuracy for large, complex problems. It also highlights practical limitations — immature hardware, algorithmic gaps and integration/regulatory challenges — and recommends that financial institutions begin preparing for gradual adoption and collaboration with academia and industry.
## Methodology
The paper uses a hybrid approach combining a literature/theory review with a conceptual/practical analysis of quantum computing techniques applied to finance. It first surveys finance problem domains (portfolio optimization, derivatives pricing, Monte Carlo risk assessment, fraud detection) and relevant quantum computing theory (superposition, entanglement). The author states that quantum techniques — specifically quantum annealing, quantum gradient descent, quantum amplitude estimation, quantum walks and quantum Monte Carlo approaches — are (conceptually) applied to representative problems and compared against classical methods. Comparative assessments focus on computation time, accuracy and scalability (for example a simplified timing comparison across increasing portfolio sizes) and Monte Carlo-based derivative pricing is discussed with quantum amplitude estimation as a replacement for classical sampling. The methodology also includes iterative comparison: when a favorable result is obtained with one quantum approach it is repeated with another approach and compared to classical baselines, and results are presented as tables and graphs alongside a literature-backed discussion.

## Limitations

- Current quantum systems have not been scaled to support the numbers of qubits required for many real-world financial problems.
- Lack of mature, finance-specific quantum algorithms: much of the research is still heuristic and not yet practically applicable.
- High experimental/early-stage status of many claimed quantum advantages — most results are theoretical or simulated rather than demonstrated on production hardware.
