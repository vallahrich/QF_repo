## Abstract summary
The paper investigates quantum machine learning approaches—specifically Quantum Graph Neural Networks (QGNNs) and Quantum Support Vector Machines (QSVMs)—for fraud detection in real-time payment (RTP) systems, implemented using Qiskit and Amazon Braket. Evaluated on the IEEE-CIS credit card fraud dataset and synthetic RTP logs, the hybrid quantum-classical models outperform classical baselines on recall, F1 and AUC under severe class imbalance, while noting current QPU hardware, latency, and explainability constraints and advocating hybrid deployment strategies.
## Methodology
The study implements a hybrid classical–quantum experimental pipeline to evaluate quantum machine learning (QML) approaches for fraud detection in real-time payment (RTP) systems. Three datasets were used (a large public IEEE‑CIS fraud dataset, an author‑generated ECB‑style synthetic RTP dataset, and a reduced Deloitte QML transaction log). Preprocessing included feature pruning (correlation-based), one‑hot encoding, normalization, and class‑imbalance handling via SMOTE plus random undersampling. Classical baselines (Logistic Regression, Random Forest, XGBoost, and a 3‑layer DNN) were trained with 10‑fold cross validation using scikit‑learn, XGBoost and PyTorch‑style settings on an NVIDIA A100 GPU. Quantum models implemented were a Quantum Support Vector Machine (QSVM) employing quantum kernels / ZZFeatureMap and a Quantum Graph Neural Network (QGNN) that maps accounts/transactions to graph nodes/edges and uses variational circuit embeddings. Quantum circuits were developed with Qiskit, PennyLane and Amazon Braket; simulations used Qiskit Aer and experiments were deployed to cloud QPUs (Amazon Braket / Rigetti and IBM backends referenced). Key quantum hyperparameters included small numbers of qubits (reported 2–4 in different sections), circuit depth tuned to 3, and optimizers SPSA and COBYLA. Model evaluation used Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1, AUC and inference latency per 1,000 transactions. Comparative experiments measured classical versus quantum performance across varying fraud prevalence (downsampling experiments to 0.05% fraud), and latency was measured using both simulator and cloud‑QPU logs.

**Algorithms used:** Quantum Support Vector Machine (QSVM) with quantum kernel / ZZFeatureMap, Quantum Graph Neural Network (QGNN), Variational Quantum Classifier (VQC) / variational circuits, Random Forest (RF), XGBoost (XGB), Deep Neural Network (3-layer DNN), Logistic Regression (LR), SMOTE (oversampling), Random undersampling, SPSA optimizer, COBYLA optimizer
**Frameworks:** Qiskit (including Qiskit Aer), PennyLane, Amazon Braket, scikit-learn, XGBoost

**Experimental setup:** Classical models trained on an NVIDIA A100 GPU. Quantum circuits were simulated with IBM Qiskit Aer and deployed/executed via Amazon Braket (Rigetti QPU); IBM hardware (IBM Q Lima) is referenced as a backend. Batching of transactions at 1,024 transactions per batch was used. Quantum circuit depth was set to 3 in experiments discussed; qubit counts reported in the paper are 2 (for an initial ZZFeatureMap description) and 4 (for later hyperparameter settings). Optimizers used for variational training were SPSA and COBYLA. Classical training used 10-fold cross validation.

**Dataset:** Three datasets: (1) IEEE‑CIS Fraud Detection (Kaggle) — 590,540 records, fraud ratio 0.17%, 434 engineered features (transaction metadata); (2) ECB‑simulated RTP (author‑generated synthetic) — 100,000 records, fraud ratio 1.50%, 35 features, time‑stamped RTP flows; (3) Deloitte QML Transaction Logs (reduced feature set from AWS QML blog) — 60,000 records, fraud ratio 0.10%, 17 features. All datasets exhibit severe class imbalance and were preprocessed with SMOTE + random undersampling, correlation‑based feature pruning, one‑hot encoding and normalization. Quantum feature encoding strategies included angle encoding, amplitude encoding (for smaller feature sets), and circuit‑based embeddings with depth tuning.

**Results summary:** The paper reports experimental comparisons between classical models (LR, RF, XGBoost, DNN) and quantum-enhanced models (QSVM, QGNN) on the IEEE-CIS fraud dataset and synthetic RTP logs. QSVM and especially QGNN achieved higher classification metrics (accuracy, precision, recall, F1, AUC) and showed greater robustness to extreme class imbalance (maintaining high F1 at fraud ratios down to 0.05%). The paper notes important hardware limitations and frames hybrid classical-quantum architectures as the most practical near-term path while projecting longer-term gains as quantum hardware matures.

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** demonstrated

The paper presents empirical results from simulations and QPU runs showing higher classification metrics (F1, recall, AUC) for QSVM and QGNN relative to classical baselines on the tested datasets and reports lower per-batch inference latency on QPU after compilation in their setup. The claim is therefore characterized here as 'demonstrated' within the scope of the authors' experiments; however, the paper also notes hardware limitations and does not claim broad, general-purpose production deployment readiness.
## Limitations
- Classical models: high latencies and low scalability in real-time/high-throughput RTP settings (author-stated).
- Classical models: poor performance on severely imbalanced datasets and reliance on hand-crafted feature engineering (author-stated).
- Quantum hardware limitations: limited qubit counts, short coherence times, high gate noise and noise rates restricting circuit depth/width (author-stated).
- Current QaaS/cloud overhead: latency and variability from cloud-based quantum services (e.g., Braket, Qiskit) can offset quantum speedups unless compilation is highly optimized (author-stated).
- Deployment readiness: public QPUs and current platforms are primarily pilot/testing/development and not production-ready for high-frequency real-time systems (author-stated).
- Explainability/regulatory constraints: quantum models are complex and less interpretable, raising compliance and transparency issues under financial AI regulations (author-stated).
- Graph-model overhead: QGNN pipeline overhead (graph construction/preprocessing) can dominate latency even if compiled inference is faster (author-stated).
- Dependence on simulation/backends: many results rely on simulators (Qiskit Aer) or small-QPU pilots, which may not reflect real-world production performance (author-stated/inferred as hybrid).
- Benchmarking gap: lack of systematic, exhaustive, cross-platform, real-time QPU benchmarking and standard benchmarks for RTP fraud tasks (author-stated).
- Privacy/operation constraints: need for privacy-preserving approaches (e.g., federated QML, quantum differential privacy) to comply with GDPR/CCPA — current approaches are nascent (author-stated).

- [inferred] Energy / cost-efficiency trade-offs: real QPU runs and constant hybrid orchestration may have unfavorable cost or energy profiles compared to optimized classical inference in some regimes.
