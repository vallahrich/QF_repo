## Abstract summary
The paper proposes a quantum algorithm for pricing European options that encodes asset values in a unary basis. The method uses a low-depth amplitude-distributor circuit (partial-SWAP/iSWAP chain) to load the terminal price distribution, a simple controlled-rotation payoff encoder, and Amplitude Estimation to extract the expected payoff. Although unary encoding needs linearly many qubits (worse asymptotic scaling than binary), it yields much shallower circuits, simpler connectivity, and a native post-selection error-mitigation check, making it potentially advantageous on near-term (NISQ) devices.
## Methodology
The authors design and evaluate a quantum algorithm for pricing European call options based on the Black–Scholes log-normal model using a unary encoding of discretized asset-price bins. The methodology has three stages: (1) amplitude distribution loading: prepare a unary basis state (single 1 in the central qubit) and redistribute amplitude to represent the discretized Black–Scholes log-normal probability across bins using a nearest-neighbor chain of parameterized partial-SWAP (or partial-iSWAP) gates; angles are solved analytically from the target probabilities, circuit depth ~ floor(n/2)+1; (2) payoff encoding: identify bins above the strike and encode their contribution to the expected payoff into an ancilla via controlled-Ry rotations with per-bin angles φi = 2 arcsin sqrt((Si − K)/(Smax − K)); in unary this requires only two-qubit controlled rotations and allows a simple post-selection check (only unary-basis outcomes accepted); (3) amplitude estimation (AE) without QPE: apply iterative AE by constructing Q = A S0 A† Sψ0 and running Q^m A with multiple m-values (authors use linear mj = j), measure ancilla outcomes, and combine measurements with a weighted-average/confidence-interval based procedure (algorithms given) to estimate the payoff amplitude. The authors compare unary and binary encodings in gate counts, depth, connectivity, and robustness under noise. They run classical simulations (Qiskit) of the full circuits with a simple noise model (depolarizing after gates, symmetric readout errors scaled 10× single-qubit error), varying gate/readout error rates and AE iterations, and evaluate: KL divergence between target and loaded distributions, percent error in option payoff relative to classical reference, and sampling/AE uncertainties. They also demonstrate native error mitigation via post-selection in the unary case and quantify trade-offs (precision vs. qubit count vs. noise). Code is published.

**Algorithms used:** Amplitude Estimation (iterative / without QPE), Unary amplitude distributor (chain of partial-SWAP / partial-iSWAP gates), Controlled-Ry payoff encoding, Quantum comparator (for binary case), Quantum Generative Adversarial Network (qGAN) referenced for binary distributor, Post-selection error mitigation
**Frameworks:** Qiskit

**Experimental setup:** All experiments are classical simulations implemented using Qiskit. Circuits (unary and binary versions) are simulated with injected noise: depolarizing channel after single- and two-qubit gates and symmetric readout errors (measurement error probability set to 10× single-qubit gate error). Thermal relaxation/dephasing were not included. Simulations include repetitions/ensembles to compute KL divergence, payoff error and uncertainties.

**Dataset:** Synthetic data generated from the Black–Scholes log-normal model (analytical), using parameters S0=2, σ=40%, r=5%, T=0.1 years, strike K=1.9; asset-price range truncated to ±3 standard deviations and discretized into bins (e.g., 8 bins used for comparisons; classical baseline uses up to 10^4 bins).

## Findings
- [supported] A quantum algorithm for European option pricing is proposed that uses a unary representation of the asset value (one-hot basis) and is composed of three parts: amplitude distribution (loading), payoﬀ computation, and Amplitude Estimation (AE).
- [supported] In the unary representation the amplitude-distributor circuit that loads the target probability distribution can be implemented with nearest-neighbor partial-SWAP (or partial-iSWAP) gates and has depth ⌊n/2⌋+1 for n unary qubits.
- [supported] The expected payoﬀ computation in the unary basis is simpler than in the binary basis: it requires only controlled single-qubit rotations (cRy) from basis qubits above the strike onto an ancilla, avoiding multi-controlled comparators required in binary encoding.
- [supported] Unary encoding enables a native post-selection (consistency) check because valid runs should remain in the unary subspace; rejecting non-unary outcomes provides an error-mitigation strategy that improves accuracy at the cost of reduced accepted shots.
- [supported] Gate counts and connectivity requirements: unary circuits require only nearest-neighbor interactions (plus one ancilla connected to many qubits) and no three-qubit gates, whereas the binary approach needs Toﬀoli-like multi-controlled gates and richer connectivity, causing larger gate overhead in practice on near-term devices.
- [supported] Classical simulations (with simple noise models) show the unary algorithm is more robust to depolarizing and measurement errors than a comparable binary implementation for the same number of bins (they compare 8-bin examples: 8-qubit unary vs. 3-qubit binary).
- [supported] The authors simulate iterative AE (AE without QPE) and show it reduces sampling uncertainty; in noisy simulations unary+AE remains useful up to realistic noise levels while binary+AE fails earlier.
- [supported] For practical precision targets (errors below ~1%), a modest number of bins (O(10–100)) is often sufficient; the paper identifies a cross-over in gate-count advantage where unary uses fewer gates than binary for a typical target bin count of order ≲100.
- [speculative] The unary approach, combined with iterative Amplitude Estimation, may provide a practical quantum advantage on NISQ devices for option pricing because of lower depth, simpler connectivity, and post-selection error mitigation.
- [supported] Theoretical trade-off: unary encoding requires O(n) qubits for n bins (linear scaling) whereas binary encoding requires O(log n) qubits; thus unary has worse asymptotic qubit scaling but can be advantageous at near-term (moderate) n due to lower gate/depth overhead and noise sensitivity.
- [supported] AE (Amplitude Estimation) gives a quadratic improvement in sample complexity in the ideal case, but resource (circuit-depth, QPE) and distribution-loading requirements affect practical realization; iterative AE variants avoid QPE and are more NISQ-friendly.
- [speculative] The overall conclusion that unary representation 'may bring a relevant advantage in practice for near-term devices' is claimed by the authors based on gate counting and noisy simulations, but not demonstrated experimentally.

**Results summary:** The paper introduces a unary-basis quantum algorithm to price European options under Black–Scholes dynamics. The algorithm has three modules: an amplitude-distributor (implemented by a shallow nearest-neighbor partial-SWAP network of depth ⌊n/2⌋+1), a payoﬀ encoder (simple controlled rotations onto an ancilla for qubits above the strike), and iterative Amplitude Estimation (AE) to extract the expected payoﬀ. Compared with a binary-encoded approach, unary encoding needs linearly many qubits for n bins but yields much simpler circuits (no multi-controlled Toﬀoli gates), shallower depth, and simpler connectivity. The unary scheme enables post-selection to discard non-unary measurement outcomes and thus mitigates errors. Classical-noise simulations and gate-count analysis indicate unary circuits are more robust to realistic depolarizing and read-out errors and that for practical precision targets (sub-1%) and moderate bin counts (O(10–100)) unary can be preferable in near-term devices. However, unary is asymptotically worse in qubit scaling and no experimental hardware demonstration is presented.

**Performance claims:**
- [supported] Amplitude-distributor circuit depth (unary) = ⌊n/2⌋+1.
- [supported] Unary representation uses n qubits for n bins; binary representation uses O(log n) qubits for n bins.
- [supported] Gate-count crossover: for typical parameters the unary implementation uses fewer gates than the binary implementation for bin counts up to roughly O(100) (paper identifies ~100 bins as a crossing scale).
- [supported] Simulation example: comparison for 8 bins (8-qubit unary vs 3-qubit binary) showed the binary KL divergence to the target distribution is about one order of magnitude larger than the unary KL divergence at the largest tested noise level (figure 10).
- [supported] Iterative AE robustness: unary+AE produced near-optimal results up to single-qubit error ~0.3% for M=2 AE iterations in the reported simulations (i.e., accuracy remained close to optimal under those noise levels).
- [supported] Classical binning: classical Monte Carlo with ~100 bins yields option-price errors below 1% in the worked example; the unary approach targets similar bin counts for competitive accuracy.
- [supported] Gate-scaling expression (unary, when device supports both partial-iSWAP and CNOT): total gates ≈ (4κ + 1) n + 1, with κ ∈ [0,1] depending on strike position; depth ≈ (4κ + 1/2) n (as reported in Table I).

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** speculative

The paper notes the known theoretical quadratic sample-complexity speedup from Amplitude Estimation, and argues (via gate counts and noisy classical simulations) that a unary encoding plus iterative AE may yield a practical advantage on NISQ hardware for moderate bin counts (O(10–100)) and realistic noise levels (e.g., single-qubit errors ≲0.3%). However, this is based on simulations and resource estimates rather than an experimental demonstration; moreover unary loses asymptotically due to linear qubit scaling, so the advantage is conditional and not empirically proven on hardware.
## Limitations
- Unary representation requires linearly many qubits (O(n) for n bins) versus logarithmic scaling for binary, yielding worse asymptotic scaling.
- Binary algorithms require efficient probability-distribution loading (e.g., qGAN) and the overhead/training of qGANs and pre-processing is not fully accounted for.
- Amplitude Estimation without Quantum Phase Estimation gives suboptimal scaling compared to the QPE-based AE (trade-off between resources and optimality).
- Post-selection in the unary approach reduces accepted samples and thus can increase sampling uncertainty and slow convergence.
- Gate-count and depth estimates assume ideal chip connectivity; limited connectivity in real hardware will increase SWAP overhead and worsen performance.
- Noise model in simulations excludes thermal relaxation/dephasing (T1/T2) and other realistic error sources; results may not fully reflect real-device behavior.
- The unary amplitude-distributor construction assumes either a known final distribution or the ability to solve a set of equations; when only the differential equation is known, other methods are required.
- The binary comparator and payoﬀ encoding require many multi-controlled gates (Toffolis), increasing circuit complexity and sensitivity to noise.
- The partial-SWAP angle determination scales with the number of bins and may be sensitive to numerical instability or implementation errors [inferred].
- There is a trade-off between depth/number-of-qubits and achievable precision; unary wins for moderate bin counts (roughly <100) but loses asymptotically — the exact cross-over depends on hardware and error rates.
- Amplitude Estimation effectiveness on noisy NISQ devices is limited; for the binary representation AE often fails beyond the trivial (M=0) case under realistic noise levels.
- Simulations and gate counts do not include full costs of qGAN training, noise in training, or classical precomputation time for distribution parameters [inferred].
- Post-selection mitigates some errors but may bias results if error patterns are non-uniform or correlated across runs [inferred].
- Results presented are for a single European option (single-asset, single-maturity); generalizations (e.g., path-dependent, multi-asset, American options) are not demonstrated.
## Open questions
- What is the practical cross-over point (number of bins/qubits) where binary representation outperforms unary on current and near-term hardware?
- How well does the unary algorithm perform on real experimental hardware (including full noise models, T1/T2, crosstalk, limited connectivity)?
- Can probability distributions be loaded efficiently into the binary representation at scale, avoiding exponentially costly precomputation steps?
- How to optimally select AE iteration schedule (mj) in noisy devices to maximize precision while accounting for post-selection losses?
- How many AE iterations are feasible in practice given realistic device error rates before quantum advantage is lost?
- How to balance post-selection acceptance rate against accuracy and precision to obtain best end-to-end performance?
- How to extend the unary approach to multi-asset options, path-dependent derivatives, and other financial instruments beyond the single European option studied?
- What are the effects of correlated and non-i.i.d. errors (e.g., crosstalk) on the unary post-selection mitigation strategy?
- How sensitive is the partial-SWAP angle-solution procedure to errors and to imperfect knowledge of the target distribution (e.g., only differential equation known)?
- What are the resource and performance implications of using partial-iSWAP native gates versus CNOT-native hardware in practical devices?
- How does the qGAN training overhead and its fidelity impact the overall advantage of binary AE approaches in practice?
- Can alternative error mitigation techniques (beyond post-selection) be combined with the unary scheme to further improve accuracy without excessive sample loss?

**Future work:**
- Increase precision by assigning qubits to represent non-equispaced elements of the probability distribution (adaptive/nonuniform binning).
- Extend the approach to multi-asset computations (multi-dimensional distributions).
- Experimentally test the unary option-pricing algorithm on recently presented quantum computers (hardware validation).
- Explore further improvements and refinements of the unary algorithm and its components (e.g., distributor, payoﬀ encoding, AE strategy).

## Contradictions
- The paper argues a near-term (NISQ) advantage for the unary approach due to shallower circuits and native post-selection, but also acknowledges that unary encoding has worse asymptotic qubit scaling (linear in bins). This creates a tension: claimed NISQ practicality does not extend to scalable/large-instance advantage, limiting long-term applicability (contradiction between short-term benefit and long-term scalability).
