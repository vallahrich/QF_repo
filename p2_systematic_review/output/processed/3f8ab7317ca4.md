## Abstract summary
The paper introduces an extrapolation scheme to determine the two parameters of the linear-ramp QAOA ansatz (and its depth) by optimizing on small random subinstances (4–10 qubits) and extrapolating to larger problem sizes (up to 28 qubits). Applied to portfolio optimization, feature selection, clustering and weighted MaxCut on a noiseless emulator, the method yields quantum runtime scalings that match or, in the case of portfolio optimization, outperform a classical benchmark (MQLib/Burer2002); the authors also propose a universal algebraic parameter-scaling that improves stability and performance across instance ensembles.
## Methodology
The authors evaluate the scaling of a fixed-schedule (linear-ramp) QAOA (LR-QAOA) approach for combinatorial optimization and compare it to several classical solvers. They formulate four problems (portfolio optimization, feature selection, clustering → MaxCut, and randomly weighted MaxCut) as QUBOs. Classical baselines (CPLEX, GUROBI, MQLib/Burer2002 heuristic, and Goemans-Williamson) are run to obtain runtime scaling. For the quantum approach they avoid variational parameter optimization by (i) sampling many small random subinstances (sizes ˜N = 4,6,8,10) from each full instance (N up to 28), (ii) performing an 11×11 grid search in log10(γ), log10(β) at an initial p (p0 = 6) for each sampled subinstance, (iii) fitting the resulting 2D performance landscape with a multivariate skew Gaussian to estimate the local optimum and widths, (iv) iteratively reducing/centering the grid for larger ˜N, (v) extrapolating the fitted optima (log-log linear fit in log10(˜N)) to predict (γ, β, Popt) for the full-size instance, and (vi) selecting an optimal QAOA depth p by minimizing an estimated total runtime metric D = d · Nshots where d ∝ p·N and Nshots is the median number of shots required based on Popt via Nshots = log(1/2)/log(1−Popt). They also derive a universal algebraic parameter-scaling law across instances by 1/D-weighted geometric averaging of extrapolated parameters and ﬁtting f(N)=a·N^b, and evaluate LR-QAOA performance (Popt, total depth D) with a noiseless quantum emulator (Qiskit-based) on full-size instances and compare exponential scaling exponents (D ∝ 2^{αN}) to classical baselines. Statistical treatment includes multiple random instances per N (typically 10) and geometric means across instances.

**Algorithms used:** Linear-ramp QAOA (LR-QAOA), QAOA (Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm), Goemans-Williamson (approximate MaxCut), MQLib/Burer2002 heuristic, CPLEX, GUROBI
**Frameworks:** Qiskit, SCI KIT-LEARN, MQLib

**Experimental setup:** Noiseless quantum emulator (statevector/ideal simulator) accessed through Qiskit. Classical experiments run with CPLEX, GUROBI, MQLib/Burer2002 and Goemans-Williamson implementations (Qiskit provides uniform QUBO interface). No physical QPU was used.

**Dataset:** Portfolio optimization: annualized returns and covariances from German DAX 30 (01/01/2016–31/12/2020); Feature selection: UCI German Credit Data (after one-hot encoding); Clustering: synthetic 'Moons' and 'Blobs' datasets from scikit-learn; MaxCut: fully connected graphs with random integer weights (0..1000).

## Findings
- [supported] The linear-ramp (LR) QAOA ansatz reduces the free parameter count to two continuous parameters (Δγ, Δβ) plus depth p, avoiding expensive high-dimensional variational optimization.
- [supported] The authors develop an extrapolation-based method that fits LR-QAOA parameter landscapes on small randomly sampled subinstances (Ñ ∈ {4,6,8,10}) and extrapolates parameters (Δγ, Δβ, p) to larger problem sizes (N up to 28).
- [supported] The extrapolation procedure uses a grid search on small subproblems, fits a multivariate skew Gaussian to locate optimal parameter regions, and performs log–log linear extrapolation of optimal parameters and Popt to predict parameters for larger N.
- [supported] Evaluations are performed on a noiseless quantum emulator for four combinatorial problem classes: portfolio optimization, feature selection, clustering (Moons/Blobs), and fully connected weighted MaxCut.
- [supported] Quantum runtime is quantified as total circuit depth D = d · Nshots (depth per circuit times number of repetitions) and popt is chosen to minimize estimated D based on extrapolated Popt.
- [supported] For portfolio optimization (N ≤ 28) LR-QAOA with extrapolated parameters shows a slower exponential runtime scaling than the classical heuristic MQLib/Burer2002: reported quantum exponent α ≈ 0.219 vs classical α_cl ≈ 0.323 (empirical median-depth scaling D ∝ 2^{αN}).
- [supported] For feature selection, clustering (Moons), and the random weighted fully-connected MaxCut ensembles, initial instance-wise extrapolation produced worse scaling than classical heuristics; however, using a ‘‘universal’’ algebraic parameter-scaling fit across instances improved LR-QAOA performance.
- [supported] Using universal algebraic scaling laws f(N) = a N^b for the LR-QAOA parameters (derived from 1/D-weighted geometric means over instances) reduces outliers and gives more stable performance across instances.
- [supported] With the universal parameter scaling the LR-QAOA scaling exponents for the four tested problems become close to each other (reported range ~0.16–0.22) and, for feature selection and the random-weighted MaxCut ensemble, achieve near-equality with the classical benchmark in these experiments.
- [supported] The median single-run success probability Popt (measuring the optimal solution) is on average ≳0.1 for the tested instances and appears approximately constant with N for portfolio optimization and MaxCut in these simulations, while showing a slight decrease for feature selection and clustering.
- [supported] Direct local optimization (starting from the extrapolated parameters) on selected larger instances (every fourth N) yields D values close to those obtained from the extrapolated parameters, validating the extrapolation approach in many cases.
- [supported] The study uses MQLib/Burer2002 as the classical runtime benchmark because it provided stable, low absolute runtimes and well-defined scaling behavior across their instance ensembles.
- [speculative] The observed combination of (i) algebraic scaling of QAOA depth p with N and (ii) approximately constant median Popt suggests the possibility that LR-QAOA median runtime could scale subexponentially (polynomially) for some problem ensembles if these trends hold to much larger N.
- [speculative] The paper suggests that universal parameter-scaling rules might generalize across instances and problem sizes, enabling practical LR-QAOA application without instance-specific parameter optimization.
- [speculative] Definitive claims of quantum runtime advantage require larger problem sizes (beyond N = 28) or fault-tolerant quantum hardware; the current findings are presented as an indication rather than a proof.
- [supported] Differences in reported scaling compared with prior work (Ref. [18]) are attributable to differences in instance selection (e.g., selecting classically hard instances) and QUBO normalization choices; appendix shows similar LR-QAOA behavior on the hard-instance set when parameters are adjusted.

**Results summary:** The authors present and empirically validate an extrapolation-based workflow to set linear-ramp QAOA parameters for combinatorial optimization problems by fitting parameter landscapes on small randomly sampled subinstances (Ñ = 4,6,8,10) and extrapolating to larger problem sizes (N up to 28). Using a noiseless quantum emulator they evaluate total quantum runtime (median total circuit depth to success) for portfolio optimization, feature selection, clustering, and fully connected weighted MaxCut, and compare to classical heuristics (MQLib/Burer2002). For portfolio optimization the LR-QAOA scaling exponent (α ≈ 0.219) is lower than the classical benchmark (α_cl ≈ 0.323), indicating a potential scaling advantage in these simulations. For the other problem classes, instance-wise extrapolation performed worse than the classical baseline, but using a universal algebraic parameter-scaling fit across instances stabilized performance and, for feature selection and the random-weighted MaxCut ensemble, brought LR-QAOA close to parity with the classical heuristic. Median single-shot success probabilities remained ≳0.1 on average for the tested ensembles. The authors stress that conclusions about broad quantum advantage remain speculative pending larger-N studies or fault-tolerant hardware.

**Performance claims:**
- Simulated problem sizes up to N = 28 qubits (noiseless emulator).
- Portfolio optimization: LR-QAOA quantum scaling exponent α ≈ 0.219 vs classical MQLib/Burer2002 α_cl ≈ 0.323 (median total depth D ∝ 2^{αN}).
- Feature selection (instance-wise extrapolation): quantum α ≈ 0.378 vs classical α_cl ≈ 0.197; after universal parameter scaling, LR-QAOA performance improves to near equality with classical benchmark.
- Clustering (Moons): instance-wise quantum α ≈ 0.248 vs classical α_cl ≈ 0.051; universal parameter scaling reduces outliers but classical scaling remains better in these experiments.
- Random fully connected weighted MaxCut: instance-wise quantum α ≈ 0.235 vs classical α_cl ≈ 0.156; with universal parameter scaling LR-QAOA reaches near-equality with classical benchmark (authors report universal α values in the ~0.16–0.22 range overall).
- Median single-run success probability Popt averaged over instances typically ≳ 0.1 and roughly constant with increasing N for portfolio optimization and MaxCut in these simulations.
- Parameter extrapolation is based on grid-search (11×11) and skew-Gaussian fits on small subproblems and log–log linear extrapolation of optimal parameters and Popt.
- Universal parameter scaling modeled as algebraic fits f(N) = a · N^{b} obtained from 1/D-weighted geometric means over instances.
## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** speculative

The paper presents empirical evidence (noiseless-simulator results up to N=28) that LR-QAOA with extrapolated/universal parameters exhibits superior exponential-scaling coefficients compared to a chosen classical heuristic for the portfolio optimization ensemble; however, this is limited to small sizes and a specific ensemble. The authors correctly frame this as an indication rather than a demonstrated, general quantum advantage and emphasize that verification requires larger problem sizes or fault-tolerant hardware, so the claim is speculative.
## Limitations
- Study limited to modest problem sizes (N up to 28 qubits); conclusions may not hold for larger N (author-stated).
- All quantum results obtained on a noiseless quantum emulator rather than on noisy near-term or fault-tolerant hardware (author-stated).
- Extrapolation method relies on subproblem optimizations for ˜N ∈ {4,6,8,10}; quality of extrapolation for larger or different subproblem choices is not guaranteed (author-stated / methodological).
- No theoretical justification for the assumed algebraic (log-log linear) scaling of parameters with subproblem size; assumption is empirical (author-stated).
- The runtime comparison omits the full cost of finding optimized circuit parameters: although the extrapolation uses small subproblems, the total classical/quantum overhead of parameter discovery is not fully integrated into the runtime scaling (author-stated / discussed).
- Statistical analysis focused on median runtime; other risk-relevant percentiles and full distributional behavior were not analyzed (author-stated).
- Classical benchmarking uses MQLib/Burer2002 as the main comparator; results may differ with other classical solvers or with different solver settings (author-stated / methodological).
- Problems and instances used are simplified versions of real-world problems (e.g., portfolio formulation choices, DAX30 sampling, fixed budget and risk parameter) and may not capture full industrial complexity (author-stated).
- [inferred] Results show outlier failures (extrapolation instability) for some problem instances (particularly clustering), indicating limited robustness of the extrapolation approach across instance types.
- [inferred] Use of a noiseless emulator ignores device noise, gate errors, connectivity restrictions and real gate-time overheads; scaling and Popt observed may degrade substantially on real hardware.
- [inferred] The grid-search and skew-Gaussian fitting procedure (even on small subproblems) can be computationally expensive and may become a bottleneck if repeated for many instance classes or parameter regimes.
- [inferred] Universal parameter scaling derived from the studied instance ensembles may not generalize to other instance distributions, different sparsity/connectivity patterns, or other QUBO formulations.
- [inferred] Shot-count model (median-based Nshots from Popt) assumes independent identically distributed sampling and ignores practical noise, readout errors, and circuit batching/overheads that affect real runtimes.

## Key ideas
- #idea:quantum-advantage — LR-QAOA with parameter extrapolation from small subinstances yields runtime scalings that match or (for portfolio optimization) outperform a classical benchmark (MQLib/Burer2002) on a noiseless emulator up to 28 qubits.
- #idea:hybrid-approach — Bypass expensive global variational optimization by (i) sampling many small random subinstances, (ii) performing grid searches on those, (iii) fitting multivariate skew-Gaussians to the landscapes, and (iv) extrapolating optimal parameters to larger instances.
- #idea:near-term-feasibility — Proposes a practical procedure (including selection of optimal QAOA depth by minimizing an estimated total-shot depth metric D) intended to predict runtime scaling and guide resource choices for N up to ~28 qubits.
- #limitation:simulation-only — All experiments run on a noiseless statevector/ideal emulator (Qiskit); no physical QPU experiments were performed.
- #limitation:no-empirical-validation — The extrapolation method and claimed speedups are not validated on real noisy hardware; sensitivity to noise and hardware constraints remains untested.
- #limitation:noise — Use of an ideal simulator and omission of noise modelling mean reported Popt and shot estimates likely optimistic; performance under realistic noise could invalidate scaling claims.
## Contradictions
- The paper reports a potential quantum scaling advantage for portfolio optimization (LR-QAOA α ≈ 0.219 vs classical α_cl ≈ 0.323) but simultaneously finds worse scaling for other problem classes (feature selection, clustering, MaxCut) under instance-wise extrapolation—only after applying a 'universal' algebraic parameter-scaling do some of those problems approach parity. This internally contradicts any broad claim of universal quantum superiority across combinatorial finance-related problems.
- The authors note differences from prior reported LR-QAOA behavior (Ref. [18]) are attributable to instance selection and QUBO normalization; thus favorable scaling observed here may not generalize to classically hard instance sets, contradicting stronger generalization/scalability claims.
