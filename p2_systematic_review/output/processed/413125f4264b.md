## Abstract summary
The paper introduces a class of regime-switching stochastic models driven by an observable finite-state Markov chain that are more amenable to quantum algorithms than popular continuous stochastic volatility models. It presents state-preparation circuits and amplitude-estimation workflows for credit risk and option pricing under these regimes, and demonstrates circuit accuracy and a Maximum Likelihood Amplitude Estimation (MLAE) example on IBM Torino and Honeywell H1 quantum processors using real data.

**Results summary:** The paper proposes regime-switching (finite-state Markov) models as a middle ground between simple Black–Scholes-like models and complex diffusive stochastic-volatility models (e.g., Heston) for quantum financial algorithms. The authors design state-preparation circuits for two-state Markov chains and apply them to static and dynamic credit-risk and European option pricing use-cases. They implement circuits on simulators and on IBM and Honeywell hardware, reporting measurement MSEs and circuit depths. They also run Maximum-Likelihood Amplitude Estimation (MLAE) for a practical three-step credit-risk example on real hardware. Empirical results show reasonably low state-preparation errors in several small-scale instances, with Honeywell H1 often producing lower MSEs (attributed to higher gate fidelity) but with smaller qubit counts than available superconducting devices. The paper argues that these discrete-state models are more amenable to near-term quantum implementation than diffusion-based stochastic volatility models, while noting that canonical QAE remains infeasible on current hardware and that further hardware advances are required for practical quantum advantage.

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** theoretical

The paper argues (theoretically) that Quantum Amplitude Estimation gives a quadratic speedup over classical Monte Carlo and that discrete-state Markov models may be more amenable to QAE-based workflows than continuous diffusions. However, canonical QAE was not demonstrated on hardware for the studied problems (too large), and only MLAE (a practical variant) was run; no incontrovertible practical quantum advantage is demonstrated on real devices. Some resource-use figures (oracle queries) for MLAE runs are given, but do not constitute a demonstrated end-to-end advantage at problem sizes of practical interest.
## Limitations
- Quantum computers are not yet capable of providing computational advantages for realistic stochastic diffusion models (e.g., Heston); models with latent continuous volatility remain infeasible.
- NISQ hardware limits: limited qubit counts, gate fidelities, connectivity constraints and circuit depth restrict practical implementations and accuracy.
- Canonical QAE (full amplitude estimation with phase estimation) is prohibitively large for the presented circuits; only MLAE (or iterative variants) is feasible on current hardware.
- Discretization bias and numerical issues of Euler/Milstein schemes for continuous-time diffusion models make those models hard to simulate accurately on coarse quantum time discretizations.
- The paper focuses on a two-state, observable Markov chain; this simplification avoids filtering/latent-state estimation and may not reflect real-time market observability.
- Scaling to more states (M>2) or many time steps quickly becomes infeasible on current processors; resource requirements explode (qubits, gates, depth).
- Register encoding trade-offs (one-hot vs binary) are unresolved in practice; the exact crossover point where binary encoding becomes preferable is not determined.
- State-preparation circuits and payoff mappings rely on approximations (e.g., small-angle/sinusoidal approximations) that introduce errors; choice of scaling constant c introduces tradeoffs between approximation and sampling error.
- Hardware noise invalidates ideal confidence intervals produced by MLAE/MLE; the reported CIs assume perfect circuit implementations.
- Routing and qubit-mapping/routing constraints (limited connectivity) greatly increase circuit depth; existing routing improvements yield only marginal reductions for these circuits.
- Demonstrations are limited to small, simplified examples and short horizons; not shown at scale for realistic portfolios or long time horizons.
- [inferred] Treating NBER business-cycle states as observable in real-time is a simplification; real-world detection of regime states involves delay/noise and would complicate implementation.
- [inferred] Performance and accuracy are sensitive to readout error and mitigation; dependence on readout mitigation is not fully quantified.
- [inferred] Precise resource estimates (qubits, depth, oracle calls) required for realistic multi-year, monthly-sampled regime-switching models remain unclear.
## Open questions
- What is the optimal choice of the scaling constant c in the payoff amplitude mapping when accounting for real hardware noise and finite sampling?
- At what number of Markov states M (and under which hardware constraints) does binary encoding outperform one-hot encoding in practice?
- Can trapped-ion processors scale in qubit count and fidelity to accommodate realistic regime-switching models, or will superconducting qubits remain the practical path?
- When will quantum hardware (qubit counts, fidelity and connectivity) be sufficient to run canonical QAE and realize the theoretical quadratic speedup for realistic financial models?
- How should latent (unobservable) Markov states be handled on quantum circuits (filtering/prior incorporation) in a practical, efficient way?
- How can discretization bias for continuous stochastic volatility models be reduced or avoided in quantum algorithms (is there a quantum-friendly alternative model class)?
- How robust are MLAE and other QAE variants to realistic noise, and how should confidence intervals be adjusted to reflect circuit imperfections?
- How much can routing and compilation improvements reduce depth for regime-switching state-preparation circuits given current connectivity topologies?
- What are accurate resource (qubit, gate, depth, oracle queries) estimates for realistic portfolios/time horizons (e.g., multi-year monthly sampling) when using these regime-switching models?
- How to extend the presented methodology to heterogeneous groups, non-homogeneous/time-varying transition matrices, and larger G (number of groups) in a scalable way?

**Future work:**

- Develop improved routing, compilation and circuit-optimization techniques to reduce depth and two-qubit gate counts for regime-switching circuits.
