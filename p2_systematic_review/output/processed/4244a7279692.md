## Abstract summary
The paper proposes a scheduler that combines multiple developers' quantum circuits into a single execution to improve qubit utilization, reduce queue waiting times, and lower execution costs. The approach is validated on IBM Quantum and Amazon Braket using a set of benchmark algorithms, showing large cost and time savings while producing results similar to individual circuit executions with only minor noise-related differences.
## Methodology
The authors designed and implemented a quantum circuit scheduling process and empirically validated it by comparing scheduled combined executions against individual circuit executions on real QPUs from two cloud providers. The scheduler accepts circuits (via Quirk URLs or repository-hosted Python code in Qiskit/Braket), places them in a FIFO execution queue, and groups circuits into a single scheduled job during fixed scheduling cycles until available qubits are filled. For each scheduled job the scheduler modifies circuit code to assign disjoint qubit index ranges to each submitted circuit, transpiles the combined circuit to the target device topology, and submits it for execution. After execution, a result "unscheduling" step splits bitstrings according to the assigned qubit ranges to recover per-circuit outcomes which are stored and made available via an API. For validation, 10 canonical quantum algorithms were taken from a public Zenodo repository and executed both individually and in scheduled batches on IBM Quantum (Eagle, Heron r1, Heron r2) and Amazon Braket (IQM Garnet) QPUs. The authors compared execution time and monetary cost between individual and scheduled execution and evaluated fidelity differences using the Wasserstein distance between output probability distributions. They also reported execution times and cost savings, and analyzed noise impacts (noting dependence on gate types and device size).

**Algorithms used:** Bernstein-Vazirani, Deutsch-Jozsa, Full Adder, Grover, Kickback, Phase Estimation, Quantum Walk, Shor, Simon, Travelling Salesman Problem (TSP)
**Frameworks:** Qiskit, Amazon Braket SDK, Quirk (circuit URL format)

**Experimental setup:** Real quantum hardware on cloud providers: Amazon Braket IQM Garnet (20 qubits); IBM Quantum Eagle (127 qubits), Heron r1 (133 qubits), Heron r2 (156 qubits). Circuits from a Zenodo public repository were submitted to the Scheduler API; circuits were scheduled into combined jobs according to FIFO until available qubits were filled, qubit index ranges were assigned per circuit, combined circuits were transpiled to the device topology and executed. Multiple shot configurations were used (not fully specified). Costs and execution times were recorded; output distributions were compared using the Wasserstein distance.

- Current implementation and evaluation support only IBM Quantum and Amazon Braket providers (author-stated).

## Open questions
- How well does the scheduling approach generalize to larger, deeper, or more entanglement-heavy circuits beyond the tested benchmarks?
- What are the optimal scheduling policies (tradeoffs among latency, noise, cost, fairness, and throughput) and how should they be selected or tuned?
- How can transpiler-induced extra gates and mappings be minimized or accounted for to reduce noise introduced by scheduling?
- How significant is qubit crosstalk or coupling-induced error when multiple circuits are packed into adjacent qubit regions, and how can it be mitigated?
- How can privacy and isolation between different users' circuits be guaranteed when executing combined jobs?
- What are fair and accurate methods for allocating execution cost/billing to individual users when jobs are merged?
- How does scheduling interact with existing error mitigation techniques — can mitigation be applied per-subcircuit in a combined run effectively?
- How to handle heterogenous hardware (different topologies and gate sets) at scale so that scheduling still yields net cost/time benefits?
- What is the effect of different arrival patterns and cycle timing choices (how long to wait to batch) on user-perceived latency and overall system utilization?
- How would the approach perform when integrated into provider-native schedulers or in multi-tenant, production cloud environments?

- #idea:near-term-feasibility — The scheduler was validated on current cloud QPUs (IBM Eagle/Heron series and Amazon Braket IQM Garnet), demonstrating that batching of circuits is a practical NISQ-era optimization offering immediate operational benefits.
- #idea:hybrid-approach — A classical scheduling layer assembles circuits into combined jobs (assigning contiguous, disjoint qubit index ranges), invokes provider-specific transpilation, and then post-processes measurement bitstrings to recover per-circuit results — an example of classical orchestration enabling better QPU utilization.
- #idea:quantum-advantage — Fidelity loss from batching was measured via the Wasserstein distance between individual and scheduled probability distributions; average reported difference ~0.07 (7%), with max reported up to 0.4, implying generally small but sometimes non-negligible noise effects introduced by combined execution.
- #idea:hybrid-approach — The approach depends on provider transpilation: merging circuits then transpiling to device topology can introduce SWAPs/extra gates, so gains come primarily from reduced queuing and amortized job overhead rather than lower circuit-level gate counts.
## Contradictions
- Claim vs. metric: The paper states scheduled executions produce results "similar to individual circuit executions with only minor noise-related differences", yet reports Wasserstein distances up to 0.4 for some cases — a value that can indicate substantial distributional difference in particular instances.
- Potential tension: The authors report large time/cost savings from batching, but note that provider-specific transpilation of the combined circuits can add SWAPs and extra gates (increasing noise). This raises a potential contradiction between observed operational gains and the possibility that transpilation overhead could, in some workloads/devices, offset fidelity or performance benefits.
