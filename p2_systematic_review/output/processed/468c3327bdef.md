## Abstract summary
This review examines the potential for quantum computing and quantum-enhanced AI to transform fraud prevention and financial crime detection in US banking by enabling real-time analysis of massive transaction datasets, improved anomaly detection, and novel cryptographic approaches. It also highlights major barriers—high implementation cost, scarce expertise, emerging quantum-enabled threats to current encryption, and regulatory/compliance challenges—and recommends early investment in post-quantum cryptography, industry–tech collaboration, and workforce training.
## Methodology
This paper is a narrative literature review / topical survey synthesizing existing research, technical articles, conference proceedings, preprints and industry commentary on the potential role of quantum computing in US banking for fraud prevention and financial crime detection. ## Limitations

- Need for specialized quantum hardware and engineering expertise; scarcity of trained personnel.
- Technology is at an early/NISQ stage — lack of large-scale, fault-tolerant quantum computers to deliver full promised advantages.
- Existing public-key cryptography (RSA, ECC) vulnerable to (future) quantum attacks, creating transitional risk.
- Post-quantum cryptography (PQC) adoption is nascent and standards/operational practices are not yet fully mature.
- Limited secure access to quantum resources (cloud providers) that may be unsuitable for sensitive financial data.
- Regulatory and compliance uncertainty — unclear guidelines for quantum use and liability in quantum-powered attacks.
- Integration challenges with legacy banking systems and operational workflows.
- Current fraud-detection systems exhibit high false positives/negatives and limited real-time processing at scale.
- Real-time payment systems increase exposure because transactions may be irreversible before fraud is detected.
- Insider threats and weak internal controls remain a non-technical source of vulnerability.
- Data privacy and cross-border data-sharing concerns complicate use of large datasets required by advanced models.
- Risk that adversaries could also exploit quantum capabilities to mount more powerful attacks.
