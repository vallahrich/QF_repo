## Abstract summary
This preprint presents a method for quantum Monte Carlo integration that preserves the full quadratic quantum speed-up while avoiding quantum arithmetic and quantum phase estimation, by decomposing the target function into a Fourier series and estimating each sinusoidal component via quantum amplitude estimation. The approach applies to expectations of single functions and products of functions of correlated variables, provides theoretical asymptotic guarantees and numerical illustrations, and targets minimal circuit depth suitable for near-term devices.
## Methodology
The paper proposes a theoretical method for quantum Monte Carlo integration (QMCI) that avoids quantum arithmetic while retaining the quadratic quantum advantage. The key idea is to (i) extend the target integrand to a smooth periodic, piecewise function on the support of the distribution, (ii) expand that periodic function in a Fourier series, and (iii) estimate each Fourier component (cosine and sine coefficients) separately using quantum amplitude estimation (QAE) applied to shallow circuits that prepare the probability-state and a single ancilla rotated by angles proportional to the sample value. The authors give a detailed asymptotic analysis showing how to allocate the total allowed number of uses of the state-preparation circuit across Fourier terms (qn ∝ n^{-κ}) and how to truncate the Fourier series (n_max ≈ ceil(q^(λ/4))) to achieve overall mean-squared-error scaling Θ(q^{-λ}), where λ is the convergence exponent of the employed QAE variant (λ=2 for conventional QAE). They also extend the approach to expectations of products of two functions of correlated variables by expanding both functions and using trigonometric identities to reduce cross-terms to single-frequency sine/cosine expectations estimable with QAE. A small numerical simulation illustrates the method on a synthetic 16-point univariate distribution, comparing Fourier QMCI against classical MCI, the rescaled-QMCI approach of prior work, and conventional QMCI with quantum arithmetic.

**Algorithms used:** Quantum Amplitude Estimation (QAE), Quantum Monte Carlo Integration (QMCI), Grover iterate / amplitude amplification (as subroutine)

**Experimental setup:** Simulated numerical experiment on a synthetic univariate 16-point probability distribution supported on integers {-8,...,7}; quantum state prepared via an explicit shallow circuit (diagram provided in paper). Methods compared: Classical Monte Carlo, rescaled QMCI (Stamatopoulos et al.), Fourier QMCI (this work), and conventional QMCI with quantum arithmetic. Results averaged over 500 independent runs; performance reported as RMSE versus total uses of the state-preparation circuit (q). No simulator name or hardware platform specified.

**Dataset:** Synthetic toy probability distribution (16-point univariate) shown in paper Figure 2(a); support on integers -8 to 7. Not a financial dataset.

**Results summary:** The paper proposes a Quantum Monte Carlo Integration (QMCI) method ("Fourier QMCI") that expands the integrand as a periodic piecewise function, decomposes it into a Fourier series, and uses quantum amplitude estimation (QAE) to estimate each Fourier component on shallow-depth circuits that only add small banks of single-qubit rotations to the state-preparation circuit. Theoretical analysis shows the overall mean estimate has MSE scaling Θ(q^{-λ}) where λ is the convergence exponent of the chosen QAE (λ=2 recovers the quadratic quantum advantage). ## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** theoretical

The paper provides a theoretical proof that, under assumptions (smoothness of f, available QAE with convergence λ, and exact/shallow state preparation of the distribution), the Fourier decomposition approach attains the same asymptotic MSE scaling as the underlying QAE (recovering the quadratic speed-up for λ=2). Numerical simulations on a small toy distribution support the asymptotic behavior but do not constitute experimental demonstration on hardware; practical advantage is conditional on shallow exact state preparation and classical computability of Fourier coefficients.
## Limitations
- Requires the target probability distribution to be preparable exactly as a quantum state in shallow depth (author-stated).
- The method assumes access to a circuit P that prepares |p⟩ and that the support (first point xl and spacing Δ) of the relevant marginal is known (author-stated).
- The function f (and g for multivariate case) must be extendable to a periodic, piecewise-smooth function that is continuous with continuous first derivative and piecewise-continuous, bounded second and third derivatives — i.e. a nontrivial smoothness requirement on the payoff/function (author-stated).
- The approach requires computing Fourier-series coefficients. If those coefficients cannot be obtained symbolically or precomputed to high accuracy, the required classical preprocessing (numerical integration) could offset the quantum speedup (author-stated).
- Truncation of the Fourier series is necessary; choosing truncation nmax and distributing the total query budget across components introduces resource-allocation complexity and constant-factor overheads (author-stated).
- The analysis assumes knowledge of the convergence rate λ of the QAE subroutine and relies on QAE performance (1 ≤ λ ≤ 2). The overall scaling claim depends on that parameter (author-stated).
- The example shows a non-negligible constant-factor slowdown relative to conventional QMCI with quantum arithmetic (≈1.8× in the numerical example) even though asymptotic scaling is preserved (author-stated).
- The method shifts computational work to many small amplitude-estimation calls; rounding of per-component query allocations to integers can add extra queries and complicates exact resource counting (author-stated).
- Preparing the class of distributions for which a shallow-depth P exists may be hard in practice; prior work shows no advantage for some common state-preparation approaches (Grover–Rudolph), so the state-preparation bottleneck remains (author-stated).
- [inferred] The algorithm's practical noise robustness on current or near-term hardware is uncertain: while it avoids deep arithmetic, it still uses many amplitude-estimation circuits and Grover-iterate depth, which may be sensitive to NISQ-era noise.
- [inferred] For high-dimensional multivariate problems, the double (or higher) Fourier summations could become numerically heavy or require many components, potentially reducing practical advantage.
- [inferred] The method presumes independence between the errors of different Fourier-component estimates (used in error analysis); correlations introduced by practical implementations or noise could affect the claimed bounds.
- [inferred] There may be nontrivial overheads (qubit count, gate complexity) when implementing the bank of rotations for high-frequency Fourier components, which could limit practical gains.
## Open questions
- Can practically-relevant probability distributions (for finance or other applications) be prepared exactly and in shallow circuit depth so that the method yields an end-to-end advantage?
- For which classes of commonly-used payoff/functions are the Fourier coefficients symbolically computable, and when does numerical precomputation become a prohibitive bottleneck?
- How does the method perform under realistic device noise and gate errors (NISQ and near-term hardware)? What are the noise thresholds for useful advantage?
- What are the optimal strategies for truncation (nmax) and allocation of the total query budget q among Fourier components (choice of qn, κ) in practice?
- How does biased QAE or non-ideal estimators affect the aggregate bias and MSE of the Fourier reconstruction in realistic settings?
- When accounting fully for state-preparation cost and Fourier-coefficient computation cost, does an end-to-end quantum advantage remain for target use-cases (e.g., option pricing)?
- How do constant-factor overheads (e.g., about 2× more Grover iterates in the example) trade off against the large circuit-depth cost of quantum arithmetic across different problem sizes and accuracies?
- How well does the method scale to expectations of products of three or more correlated random variables (higher-order moments), both in theory and practice?
- Can the periodic extension / piecewise completion required for arbitrary functions always be chosen so that the Fourier coefficients decay rapidly enough for efficient truncation?
- What is the impact of rounding per-component query allocations to integers in practice, and can adaptive allocation schemes mitigate extra costs?
- How does the method compare empirically (wall-clock runtime, total quantum resources, classical precomputation) to alternative quantum and classical approaches on benchmark financial problems?

**Future work:**
- Investigate and demonstrate shallow-depth state-preparation circuits for practically-relevant probability distributions to realize an end-to-end advantage.
- Evaluate the method's performance on realistic noisy quantum hardware (NISQ and intermediate devices), including noise-resilience experiments and error-mitigation strategies.
- Develop and optimize classical precomputation workflows for Fourier coefficients, including symbolic derivations for common functions (means, moments, payoffs) and efficient numerical methods when necessary.
- Design and test practical allocation strategies for distributing queries among Fourier components (optimize qn and truncation nmax) and study adaptive schemes.
- Quantify full resource trade-offs (qubits, gate counts, Toffoli counts, circuit depth) between Fourier QMCI and conventional QMCI with quantum arithmetic across problem instances and accuracy targets.
- Extend and benchmark the method for expectations of products of more than two correlated variables and for higher-dimensional multivariate distributions.
- Explore integration of the method into finance-specific applications (e.g., option pricing, risk measures) with full accounting of classical and quantum computational costs.
