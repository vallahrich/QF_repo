## Abstract summary
The paper proposes a variational quantum algorithm to price multi-asset derivatives by discretizing the Black–Scholes PDE with a finite-difference method, solving the resulting ODE via variational quantum simulation, and estimating the expected discounted payoff as an inner product between a solution state and a probability state using the SWAP test. This approach avoids the amplitude-extraction bottleneck of naive quantum methods, targets feasibility on small/noisy quantum devices, and is numerically validated on a single-asset double-barrier option while arguing favorable scaling in qubit requirements.
## Methodology
The authors propose a variational quantum algorithm to price multi-asset derivatives by solving the Black–Scholes PDE (BSPDE) after spatial discretization with the finite-difference method (FDM). They (1) discretize the asset-price domain into ngr = 2^{n d} grid points and map the BSPDE to an ODE system d/dτ V̄(τ) = F V̄(τ) + C(τ) where F and C(τ) are constructed from FDM stencils and boundary conditions; (2) decompose F and C(τ) into linear combinations of (few-qubit) unitary operators so the terms can be evaluated on a quantum device; (3) prepare a quantum state |p(tter)⟩ encoding the probability distribution of underlying asset prices at some intermediate time tter (either via variational simulation of the Fokker–Planck equation or quantum generative models); (4) prepare an (unnormalized) initial state encoding the payoff values at maturity |V̄(0)⟩ (they show via a classical fidelity-maximization that typical payoff functions can be well-approximated by an ansatz); (5) perform Variational Quantum Simulation (VQS) to evolve the ansatz parameters from τ=0 to τ=τter by applying McLachlan’s variational principle, measuring the metric matrix M and vector V terms using Hadamard-type circuits (and parameter-shift gradients) and integrating the resulting parameter ODEs classically (they use an Euler integrator for the parameter updates); and (6) estimate the inner product ⟨p(tter)|V̄(τter)⟩ via the SWAP test between the two (normalized) states to obtain the expected future payoff and discount by e^{-r tter} to produce the present derivative price. The paper also provides analytic bounds and complexity estimates for choosing tter and for the number of SWAP-test measurements, and reports classical noiseless numerical simulations (NumPy) for a single-asset double knock-out barrier option to validate feasibility.

**Algorithms used:** Variational Quantum Simulation (VQS), SWAP test, Finite Difference Method (FDM) discretization of BSPDE, Quantum generative models (proposed/optional for state preparation), Hadamard test (for evaluating matrix elements), Parameter-shift rule (for gradients), Euler method (classical integration of parameter ODE), L-BFGS-B optimizer (classical, used for fidelity maximization of initial state)
**Frameworks:** NumPy, SciPy

**Experimental setup:** Noisy hardware not used — classical noiseless simulation of quantum states using NumPy. Numerical experiment: single-asset double knock-out barrier option under Black–Scholes dynamics. Parameters: r=0.001, σ=0.3, T=1, S0=1, lower barrier l=0.5, upper barrier u=2.0, strike K=1. Time-integration step for parameter ODEs: Δτ = 2.5×10^{-5}. Ansatz: repeated layers of single-qubit RY rotations and CZ entangling layers (depth m, n qubits => n(m+1) parameters); example configurations: 4 qubits and 6 qubits with 4 layers (6 qubits × 4 layers → 30 parameters). Simulations assume ideal state preparation (initial states provided or obtained via classical optimization) and ignore sampling/noise.

**Dataset:** Synthetic / analytical financial model data: Black–Scholes model of geometric Brownian motion and closed-form analytical solution for a single-asset double-barrier (knock-out) option used as ground truth. No market or empirical datasets were used.

## Findings
- [speculative] The paper proposes a variational quantum algorithm that combines variational quantum simulation (VQS) to solve the discretized Black–Scholes PDE (BSPDE) and the SWAP test to estimate the expected discounted payoff, thereby avoiding the amplitude‑extraction measurement bottleneck of naive quantum PDE approaches.
- [speculative] The authors claim the method avoids complexity factors of the form (1/ϵ)^{O(d)} present in classical finite difference methods (FDM) and some prior quantum approaches; they state a complexity scaling with respect to precision ϵ of O(1/ϵ^2 (log(1/ϵ))^d) under stated assumptions.
- [speculative] The method encodes the solution and probability distribution across amplitudes and therefore reduces space complexity exponentially versus classical FDM: only O(d log(1/ϵ)) qubits are required to represent an O((1/ϵ)^{O(d)}) grid.
- [speculative] The number of SWAP‑test measurements (NSWAP) required to estimate the inner product is derived (their Eq. (62)); it does not contain a factor (1/ϵ)^{O(d)} but retains other dependence on d and model parameters and can still grow exponentially in d in some parameters.
- [supported] Numerical, noiseless simulations for a single‑asset double knock‑out barrier option show that VQS solutions approximate classical FDM outputs: a 4‑qubit ansatz approximates 16 grid‑point FDM; a 6‑qubit ansatz (with more layers) approximates a 64 grid‑point FDM solution more closely.
- [supported] They numerically demonstrated the existence of an ansatz that approximates the payoff initial state well: reported fidelity measure satisfies |1 - α_0^{-2} |⟨V(0)|ν(θ0)⟩|^2| ≤ 1.2×10^{-5} (for the single‑asset call payoff, with a 6‑qubit, 6‑layer ansatz, optimized classically).

- [speculative] They argue the approach can be run on small/near‑term quantum computers (because of modest qubit counts and few‑qubit gate decompositions) and remains beneficial in the presence of noise, but this was not demonstrated experimentally on hardware.
- [speculative] They recommend selecting a future evaluation time t_ter (tradeoff given by an explicit bound/formula) so that the expected discounted value at t_ter approximates the present price while maintaining significant overlap between the probability and solution states.

**Results summary:** The authors present a variational quantum approach to price multi‑asset derivatives by (i) embedding the forward probability distribution and payoff into quantum states, (ii) solving the discretized Black–Scholes PDE backward in time using variational quantum simulation, and (iii) estimating the discounted expected payoff at a future time via the SWAP test to avoid extracting a single tiny amplitude. They provide theoretical complexity estimates (showing removal of (1/ϵ)^{O(d)} factors), operator decompositions for implementation, and noiseless classical simulations for a single‑asset double‑barrier option that demonstrate feasibility and high fidelity of initial state encoding. The work is mainly theoretical/heuristic; empirical evidence is limited to classical simulator experiments for a one‑asset example and variational state optimization for initial state preparation.

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** theoretical

The paper provides theoretical complexity arguments and analytic expressions suggesting the proposed variational approach avoids the (1/ϵ)^{O(d)} dependence of classical FDM and prior amplitude‑extraction quantum approaches, and it achieves exponential space savings by amplitude encoding. However, these claims rely on heuristic assumptions (efficiency of state preparation, VQS convergence, and favorable parameter regimes) and are supported only by small‑scale noiseless simulations for a single‑asset example rather than by experimental quantum hardware or rigorous proofs; thus any claimed quantum advantage remains theoretical.
## Limitations
- No theoretical guarantee on overall computational complexity because of the heuristic nature of variational algorithms (author-stated).
- The method assumes availability of (unnormalized) initial states |p(tter)⟩ and |V(0)⟩ (probability distribution and terminal payoff) generated efficiently by variational/generative methods; preparation is assumed rather than demonstrated (author-stated).
- The SWAP-test measurement cost NSWAP still contains an exponential dependence on the number of assets d (although the dependence on ϵ is improved); NSWAP can be large in practice (author-stated).
- VQS requires decomposition of the PDE operator F and source |C(τ)⟩ into linear combinations of (few-qubit) unitaries and controlled implementations; this leads to circuits with nontrivial gate counts (O(d^2 n^4) unitaries, etc.) and assumes such decompositions are efficient (author-stated).
- The formulation can only handle linear boundary conditions with respect to si; log-transformations are avoided because they would produce non-linear boundary terms incompatible with the chosen decomposition (author-stated).
- Numerical validation is limited: simulations focus on a single-asset double knock-out barrier option; multi-asset performance is not empirically demonstrated (author-stated).
- Simulations ignore noise and statistical errors — results are idealized noiseless simulations (author-stated).
- Analysis assumes bounded normalization factors (|θ0(t)|) and bounding constants (ζ, B) used in measurement-count estimates; practical validity and magnitudes of these constants are not validated (author-stated).
- [inferred] Scalability to large d may be impeded by both measurement overhead (NSWAP) and the gate/ancilla overhead from operator decompositions (practical resource blow-up).
- [inferred] The variational ansatz expressibility, trainability and optimization landscape (e.g., barren plateau issues) are not addressed and may limit practical applicability.
- [inferred] The SWAP-test approach avoids amplitude readout bottleneck but can still require prohibitively many shots when the overlaps (α, β) are small — practical shot complexity may be large.
- [inferred] The paper assumes efficient quantum generative model or VQS solutions for Fokker–Planck; how well these scale or train in practice is not shown.
- [inferred] Implementation on near-term hardware may be limited by circuit depth, controlled unitaries, and ancilla requirements implied by the decompositions.
- [inferred] No direct comparison against classical Monte Carlo or other classical numerical approaches for realistic parameter regimes is provided, leaving practical advantage unclear.
- [inferred] Restricting to payoff types that admit linear boundary conditions omits many commonly used option types (e.g., American/Bermudan) that require different treatment.
## Open questions
- How can the required initial states |p(tter)⟩ (probability distribution) and |V(0)⟩ (terminal payoff) be prepared efficiently and robustly on real quantum hardware?
- How does the proposed variational approach perform on noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) devices, including the effect of gate noise and finite sampling?
- What is the empirical scaling of total resources (gates, qubits, shots) with the number of assets d and target error ϵ in realistic problem instances?
- What are practical values of the constants (ζ, B, α, β, etc.) in real-world derivatives problems, and how do they affect the shot complexity NSWAP?
- How to choose the optimal tter to balance the approximation error from truncating domain/boundary effects and the overlap (measurement efficiency)?
- How expressive and trainable must ansätze be to represent typical payoff and PDE solution states, and can efficient training algorithms be found (avoiding local minima and barren plateaus)?
- Can the measurement overhead (both for VQS—Mi,j and Vi estimation—and for the SWAP test) be reduced with improved estimators, error mitigation, or alternative inner-product evaluation methods?
- How to generalize the method to handle non-linear boundary conditions and more complex payoff structures (American/Bermudan/look-back options)?
- What are trade-offs, in practice, between this PDE-based quantum approach and classical Monte Carlo or advanced classical PDE techniques (sparse grids, spectral methods) for multiasset derivatives?
- How to implement the decompositions (F and |C(τ)⟩ as sums of unitaries) with minimal ancilla and gate overhead on near-term hardware?
