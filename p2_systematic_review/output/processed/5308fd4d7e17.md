## Abstract summary
This survey reviews the current state of quantum computing and its prospective applications in finance, focusing on optimization, simulation/Monte Carlo methods, and machine learning. It systematically examines platforms, algorithms, methodologies, and use cases (e.g., portfolio management, risk estimation, derivative pricing), providing a layered framework to assess implementations, required hardware, and the realistic potential and limitations of quantum approaches for the financial industry.
## Methodology
The authors performed a systematic literature search using Google Scholar to identify peer-reviewed papers up to 2021 that propose concrete applications or implementations of quantum computing for the financial industry. They excluded panel introductions, non-English/German papers, teaching cases and pedagogical papers. Inclusion required that a paper describe a concrete use case or an implementation on quantum hardware and be peer-reviewed and published by 2021. This selection yielded 13 papers. To analyze these works they developed a four-layer conceptual framework (neglecting vendor-specific APIs): (1) Use Case (specific financial problem and business benefit), (2) Methodology (broad computational approach such as optimization, Monte Carlo/simulation, or machine learning), (3) Quantum Algorithm (the quantum algorithm or family applied, e.g. searching/counting, linear-system solvers, approximate/variational optimization), and (4) Quantum Hardware (targeted device class: gate-based or quantum annealer and whether implementations were run on real hardware or simulators). The framework was applied to the selected set, summarized in a morphological box and a table, and used to compare methods, algorithms, and hardware readiness across the sampled literature.

**Algorithms used:** Grover's search, Quantum Amplitude Estimation (QAE), Quantum Phase Estimation (QPE), Approximate counting / amplitude amplification, Harrow-Hassidim-Lloyd (HHL) linear-system solver, quantum Principal Component Analysis (qPCA), Quantum Interior Point Method (QIPM), Gibbs sampling (quantum), Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE), Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA), Quantum Alternating Operator Ansatz, QUBO formulation / Ising model mapping, Quantum Annealing (adiabatic / D-Wave style)
**Frameworks:** Qiskit (IBM), Cirq (Google), pyQuil / Forest (Rigetti), ProjectQ (ETH Zürich), Quantum Development Kit (Microsoft QDK), Amazon Braket SDK, XACC (Eclipse), Quantum Inspire, Rigetti QVM / simulators, IBM Quantum Experience

## Findings
- [speculative] Quantum computers can solve specific problems that are infeasible on classical hardware and therefore have potential to change computation-heavy industries including finance.
- [supported] There is an active and growing body of research (hardware, algorithms, software) exploring quantum computing for finance, with multiple platforms and open-source toolchains available.
- [supported] Current physical implementations include superconducting qubits, photonic qubits, trapped ions, quantum dots, NMR, and proposals using anyons; demonstrations of computational advantage have been reported on superconducting and photonic platforms (device-level demonstrations).
- [supported] Classical simulation of n qubits requires 2^n complex amplitudes, i.e., exponential memory/time overhead, so simulators are feasible only for small n.
- [supported] Software techniques (e.g., SWAPs, compilation, and error mitigation) can partially mitigate hardware constraints but introduce overhead and additional noise.
- [speculative] Noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) devices might find useful finance applications before full fault-tolerant quantum computers are available, but concrete broad advantages remain unproven.
- [supported] The literature clusters finance use-cases into three methodological areas: optimization, Monte Carlo/simulation, and (less represented) machine learning.
- [supported] From the surveyed, most concrete proposals and implementations target optimization and Monte Carlo methods; comparatively few peer‑reviewed papers propose quantum-based machine learning solutions for finance (none in the selected 13).
- [supported] Many finance algorithms proposed for quantum speed-ups are built on known quantum subroutines: Grover/Amplitude Estimation, HHL (linear systems), QAOA/VQE (variational/approximate optimization), qPCA, and quantum interior-point approaches for SDPs/SOCPs.
- [supported] Quantum annealers (Ising/QUBO representation) have been used in experiments for finance-related combinatorial optimization problems; gate-based devices are the target for amplitude estimation and other Monte Carlo speed-ups.
- [supported] Portfolio optimization has multiple quantum proposals: (a) gate-based HHL approaches for unconstrained quadratic problems (theoretical); (b) QUBO-mapped annealer/hybrid approaches for discrete/weighted variants (small-device experiments exist).
- [supported] Certain small-scale quantum-annealer experiments (portfolio QUBO instances) can approach the performance of commercial classical solvers on the limited problem sizes tested.
- [supported] Reverse quantum annealing protocols have been reported to be empirically >100x faster than forward quantum annealing on the tested instances in at least one study (Venturelli & Kondratyev), though the scope is small and device/problem dependent.
- [speculative] Quantum interior-point methods for SOCPs/SDPs have theoretical asymptotic speed-ups, but the speed-ups depend on problem-dependent parameters and hardware that is not yet available.
- [supported] Hybrid quantum-classical heuristics have been proposed and implemented to extend variational methods (QAOA/VQE) to mixed binary optimization (MBO) problems such as transaction settlement; small cloud-device experiments were reported.
- [supported] For derivative pricing and risk estimation, amplitude-estimation based quantum algorithms promise quadratic improvements in sample complexity versus classical Monte Carlo (theoretical claim widely cited), and small experimental implementations / circuit demonstrations have been performed on gate-based devices.
- [supported] Empirical gate-based experiments for option-pricing/risk (on current noisy hardware) show significant errors but can benefit from simple error mitigation (example: observed average pricing error reduced from ~62% to ~21% with mitigation in one study).
- [supported] Practical, industry-scale applications (multi-asset, path-dependent derivatives, realistic VaR/CVaR instances) require substantially more qubits and/or much lower gate errors than currently available; circuit-depth requirements estimated in surveyed work exceed present hardware.
- [speculative] Predicting systemic events (financial crashes) via QUBO-network formulations has been proposed and small annealer-based implementations exist; whether such approaches yield reliable, back-testable predictions at scale is unproven.
- [supported] Overall conclusion of the review: there are promising theoretical algorithms and small-scale device demonstrations, but hardware (qubit counts, coherence, error rates) and end-to-end engineering constraints currently prevent a clear, general quantum advantage in finance.

**Results summary:** The paper surveys peer-reviewed proposals and small-scale experiments (13 selected works up to 2021) for quantum computing in finance using a four-layer framework (use case, methodology, quantum algorithm, hardware). Most concrete activity is in optimization (portfolio optimization, transaction settlement) and Monte Carlo/simulation (derivative pricing, VaR/CVaR), with only limited representation of quantum machine learning. Theoretical work indicates asymptotic or fundamental speed-ups for certain subroutines (amplitude estimation: quadratic sample-complexity improvement; quantum linear algebra and SDP/SOCP solvers: potential polynomial to superpolynomial improvements). Several small empirical demonstrations exist (QUBO on annealers; small gate-based circuits for pricing/risk), but they are constrained to tiny problem sizes and noisy hardware. The dominant conclusion is that quantum approaches are promising in theory and have partial proof-of-concept implementations, yet current hardware limits practical, economy‑scale quantum advantage in financial applications.

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** theoretical

The surveyed literature contains theoretical/algorithmic claims of asymptotic or provable speed-ups (notably amplitude estimation's quadratic improvement for Monte Carlo and quantum SDP/SOCP solvers). Small-scale device experiments and proof-of-concept implementations exist (QUBO on annealers; shallow gate circuits), but no general, practical, end-to-end quantum advantage in finance is demonstrated on industry‑relevant problem sizes due to current hardware limitations and problem-transformation overheads.
## Limitations
- Limited qubit counts on current hardware (NISQ devices) preventing practically relevant problem sizes (author-stated).
- High noise levels and short coherence times on existing quantum processors leading to unreliable/degraded computations (author-stated).
- Limited qubit connectivity/topology (e.g., nearest-neighbor) that increases required operations and error accumulation (author-stated).
- Need for large overhead to implement quantum error correction, requiring many more physical qubits (author-stated).
- Quantum simulators running on classical hardware scale exponentially in memory/time with qubit number, limiting classical emulation (author-stated).
- State preparation and data-loading costs (storing/accessing classical data on quantum devices) can be substantial and can limit end-to-end speed-ups (author-stated / implied in discussions of HHL and data access).
- Swap operations and hardware-mapping increases circuit depth and noise, reducing practical performance (author-stated).
- Many quantum algorithms (e.g., HHL, qPCA, some QML) require full-scale, fault-tolerant devices and/or QRAM, which are currently unavailable (author-stated).
- Some key quantum subroutines historically rely on expensive controlled operations or QPE (e.g., amplitude estimation variants), increasing resource demands (author-stated).
- Quantum optimization approaches (QAOA, VQE, annealing) often target QUBO formulations; many real-world financial problems have inequality or mixed discrete/continuous constraints (MBO), complicating mapping (author-stated).
- Theoretical quantum speed-ups depend on problem-dependent parameters and restrictive assumptions; end-to-end, practical speed-ups are often unproven (author-stated).
- Classical algorithmic advances and massive parallelization of classical Monte Carlo can reduce or eliminate potential quantum advantages in practice (author-stated).
- Existing experimental demonstrations are mostly small toy examples; measured errors remain large and current hardware exhausts capacity for realistic instances (author-stated).
- Quantum annealers and gate-model devices have different strengths/weaknesses; it is uncertain which hardware family (or hybrid) will be best for particular financial use cases (author-stated).
- No reliable estimates yet for the operational/economic costs of deploying and running quantum computing infrastructure at scale (author-stated).
- Breaking current public-key cryptography (Shor-like algorithms) would have severe societal impacts, implying substantial transitional risks (author-stated).

## Contradictions
- The survey highlights theoretical quantum speed-ups (e.g., amplitude estimation for Monte Carlo) but also emphasizes that NISQ-era implementations are noisy and depth-limited, contradicting optimistic claims of near-term practical advantage.
- Some small annealer experiments report substantial empirical speed-ups (e.g., reverse quantum annealing reported >100x on tested instances by Venturelli & Kondratyev), yet the survey stresses these results are device- and instance-specific and do not generalize, creating a tension between reported empirical gains and the claim that broad quantum superiority remains unproven.
- While HHL and related linear-system proposals suggest asymptotic improvements for portfolio optimization, the survey notes circuit-depth, qubit counts, and condition-number dependencies that undercut practical scalability — contradicting blanket claims of ready applicability to realistic financial problems.
