## Abstract summary
The paper introduces quantum machine learning algorithms for detecting anomalies in quantum data (quantum states), by formulating quantum versions of kernel PCA and one-class SVM. For pure states the authors show these algorithms can run with resources logarithmic in both the state dimension and the number of training states, and for mixed states with resources logarithmic in the dimension, potentially reducing the need for full state tomography. The methods use swap-test variants, quantum PCA, and quantum matrix inversion (HHL) techniques and avoid reliance on quantum RAM.
## Methodology
This is a theoretical development of quantum machine-learning algorithms for anomaly detection on quantum data (quantum states). The authors formulate quantum analogues of two classical anomaly-detection approaches: kernel Principal Component Analysis (kernel PCA) and the one-class Support Vector Machine (one-class SVM). The work assumes access to state preparation unitaries Ui that produce training pure states |ψi> and a control unitary UC = Σ_i |i><i| ⊗ Ui which enables creation of label–state superpositions. For kernel PCA on pure states they define a centroid state |ψc>, construct centered states |zi> = Ni(|ψi> − (1/(M Nc))|ψc>), and express the covariance matrix C as a (scaled) density operator. A proximity measure f(ψ0)=⟨z0|(1−C)|z0⟩ is used to quantify anomalousness. Two resource regimes are analysed: (i) an O(poly(M log d)) method based on estimating inner products (via a modified swap test that recovers real and imaginary parts) and classical postprocessing, and (ii) an O(log M, log d) method that prepares a superposition |χc> ∝ Σ_i (1/Ni)|i>|zi> so that tr1(|χc><χc|) ∝ C and then evaluates tr(C |z0><z0|) with a swap test. For the one-class SVM on pure states they define a kernel matrix K with entries |<ψi|ψj>|^2 (viewed as a density-like matrix) and solve (K + PT M I) α = r e. To obtain α in the large-M regime they propose to use the quantum matrix inversion (HHL) algorithm after simulating exp(−i K t). They show how to exponentiate K by relating it to K0 (matrix with elements <ψi|ψj>), using a custom three-register operator S' and a modified quantum PCA technique that effectively simulates evolution under the Hadamard (elementwise) product K = K0^T * K0 via multiple copies of K0. After obtaining |α> they prepare states |φ1>, |φ2> (constructed from training states, |ψ0>, and |α>) and estimate their overlap using controlled superposition + swap-test-like procedures to compute the SVM proximity measure f(ψ0). The paper also generalizes both schemes to mixed states: defining an inner-product-like quantity ⟨ρi, ρj⟩ based on Kraus decompositions and using the superfidelity as a kernel; a modified ancilla-based test is described to estimate these mixed-state inner products. Resource and error analyses are provided throughout (sample complexity O(1/ε^2) for Bernoulli measurement estimates; in several places they argue O(log d) scaling if errors ε ~ 1/log d; HHL requirements such as condition number and low-rank assumptions are discussed). Important stated assumptions include availability of Ui and UC (or at least Ui), the ability to prepare multiple copies of required states/density matrices, and that kernel/covariance matrices are low-rank so that density-matrix exponentiation and matrix inversion are efficient. The authors explicitly note that no quantum random-access memory (QRAM) is required because inputs are given as quantum states from devices.

**Algorithms used:** Quantum kernel PCA, Quantum one-class SVM (least-squares formulation), Quantum PCA / density matrix exponentiation, HHL quantum matrix inversion, Swap test (standard and modified variants), Phase estimation (as subroutine for HHL)

## Limitations
- Requires access to the unitaries {Ui} that prepare the training states and the test state, and to a control unitary UC = Σ_i |i⟩⟨i| ⊗ Ui to create superpositions of training states (author-stated).
- Assumes it is possible to generate a controlled unitary of the form |0⟩⟨0|⊗u1 + |1⟩⟨1|⊗u2 for arbitrary pairs of unitaries (author-stated).
- Multiple identical copies of each quantum state are required (author-stated).
- Quantum PCA exponentiation method requires the kernel matrix K to be low-rank; simulation exp(−iKt) requires O(t^2/ε) copies of K and thus low-rank structure for efficiency (author-stated).
- Quantum matrix inversion (HHL) use requires several conditions: efficient preparation of the RHS, ability to implement controlled unitaries for the matrix exponential, a favorable condition number (smallest eigenvalue ≳ 1/O(log M)), and that measurement of the result needs O(log M) repetitions (author-stated).
- If UC is not available, computing the kernel or required inner products may revert to classical matrix inversion costing O(M^3), losing the M-scaling advantage (author-stated).
- For mixed states, achieving a logarithmic-in-M speedup is unclear because it is not obvious how to efficiently generate multiple copies of the density matrix proportional to the mixed-state kernel matrix (author-stated).
- Measurement/sample complexity scales as O(1/ε^2) for Bernoulli-type estimates (swap tests / modified swap tests). The stated poly(log d) resource claims rely on tolerating relatively large errors (e.g., O(1/log d)), implying limited precision for the efficient regime (author-stated).
- Preparation success probabilities (e.g., for centroid/centered states) rely on training states having high mutual fidelity; if training states are not similar, success probabilities and thus efficiency may degrade (author-stated).
- [inferred] The need to construct and control UC across many labels and states could be experimentally demanding or infeasible on near-term hardware.
- [inferred] The algorithms assume ideal state preparation and low noise; realistic noise/decoherence will increase required copies and may break the claimed resource scalings.
- [inferred] HHL-related caveats (condition number, precision, circuit depth, and required ancilla/postselection) may negate asymptotic speedups in practical settings.
- [inferred] The requirement 1/PT ≲ O(log M) (i.e., PT not too small) may be unrealistic for some datasets, limiting applicability.
- [inferred] If the kernel matrix is not low-rank (or has many small nonzero eigenvalues), simulation and inversion costs may grow substantially, removing the logarithmic-in-M advantage.
- [inferred] The overall advantage hinges on availability of controlled multi-register unitaries and efficient generation of specific superposition states; those are nontrivial resources that are effectively assumed but not cost-accounted.
