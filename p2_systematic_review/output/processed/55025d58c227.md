## Abstract summary
The preprint presents a modular quantum decoherence simulator that implements Lindblad master equation and Monte Carlo wavefunction unravelings, and introduces a Fractal Correction Engine (FCE) which analyzes eigenvalue-trajectory geometry using Frenet–Serret curvature and Higuchi fractal dimension to predict future state evolution. Applied to a 3-qubit GHZ state evolving under a transverse-field Ising Hamiltonian with phase damping, the FCE predicts purity with high accuracy (Pearson r ≈ 0.9979, mean absolute error ≈ 2.3%).
## Methodology
The study implements a modular Python-based quantum decoherence simulator and a Fractal Correction Engine (FCE). The simulator evolves multi-qubit density matrices using both deterministic Lindblad master-equation integration and stochastic Monte Carlo wavefunction (MCWF / quantum-jump) unravelings. Lindblad propagation is performed by vectorizing the density matrix and solving the time-local master equation with SciPy's solve_ivp (RK45, rtol=1e-9, atol=1e-12). MCWF trajectories use an effective non-Hermitian Hamiltonian and per-step jump probabilities computed from Lindblad operators; no-jump evolution uses scipy.linalg.expm and trajectories are renormalized after each step. At each time step the code records eigenvalue decompositions (numpy.linalg.eigh) and computes information measures (purity, von Neumann entropy, L1 coherence, concurrence, Bures fidelity via scipy.linalg.sqrtm). The FCE operates on eigenvalue time series: it estimates local derivatives by central differences, computes Frenet–Serret curvature, estimates Higuchi fractal dimension of dominant eigenvalue series, and applies a T-based fractal correction that mixes Taylor-based prediction with curvature- and fractal-dimension-modulated damping. Predicted eigenvalues are projected back to the probability simplex and used with latest eigenvectors to reconstruct density matrices; FCE also computes derived quantities such as an interference wavelength and a predicted purity decay. Validation metrics reported include Pearson correlation, MAE, RMSE, and MAPE between FCE predictions and master-equation purity, and internal unit/scientific tests. The primary demonstrated experiment uses a 3-qubit GHZ initial state evolving under a transverse-field Ising Hamiltonian with independent phase-damping noise, comparing the deterministic master-equation result to an ensemble of MCWF trajectories and to FCE predictions.

**Algorithms used:** Lindblad master equation integration (RK45 solve_ivp), Monte Carlo wavefunction (quantum jump) unraveling, Stochastic Schrödinger equation (mentioned), Eigenvalue decomposition (numpy.linalg.eigh), Frenet–Serret curvature estimation (central differences), Higuchi fractal dimension estimation, T-based fractal correction / Taylor + fractal modulation predictor
**Frameworks:** Python, NumPy, SciPy

**Experimental setup:** Software-only simulation. Python simulator (enhanced_quantum_simulator.py) with modular components for states, noise channels, measurements, advanced dynamics, FCE and visualization. Deterministic evolution via SciPy.solve_ivp (RK45, rtol=1e-9, atol=1e-12); MCWF uses scipy.linalg.expm and numpy.random.default_rng for RNG; eigen-decompositions via numpy.linalg.eigh; matrix square roots via scipy.linalg.sqrtm. Typical run used 20 trajectories (1 master-equation + 19 MCWF), time range t in [0,10], 300 time steps (dt≈0.033).

(6) At each saved time step perform eigen-decomposition of density matrix, record eigenvalues/eigenvectors and compute metrics (purity, entropy, L1 coherence, concurrence, fidelity). (7) Feed eigenvalue time series into FCE: estimate first/second derivatives by central differences, compute Frenet–Serret curvature, estimate Higuchi fractal dimension, perform T-based fractal-corrected prediction of future eigenvalues, reconstruct predicted density matrix and predicted purity. (8) Compare FCE predictions to master-equation ground truth and MCWF ensemble average; compute Pearson r, MAE, RMSE, MAPE and other diagnostics. ## Findings
- [supported] A modular quantum decoherence simulator was implemented supporting deterministic Lindblad master equation integration and stochastic Monte Carlo wavefunction (quantum jump) unravelings.
- [supported] The simulator was demonstrated on a 3-qubit GHZ initial state evolving under a transverse-field Ising Hamiltonian with independent phase-damping noise.
- [supported] The simulator reproduces expected Lindblad/Kraus dephasing consistency and ensemble-averaged MCWF trajectories converge to the master-equation result.
- [supported] The simulation reveals three phases in the quantum-to-classical transition for the test case: (1) a quantum regime, (2) a transition regime with oscillatory-decay behavior, and (3) a classical emergence regime dominated by a few large eigenvalues (pointer states).
- [supported] The Fractal Correction Engine (FCE) can track eigenvalue trajectories and predict purity evolution with high correlation to the deterministic solution in this test case.
- [supported] Quantitative FCE performance on the master-equation trajectory: Pearson correlation r = 0.9979, MAE = 0.023, RMSE = 0.028, MAPE = 3.59%.
- [supported] The FCE's geometric analysis (Frenet-Serret curvature, Higuchi fractal dimension) produced a curvature profile for the master-equation trajectory (mean curvature ~0.149, range reported) and a Higuchi fractal dimension D = 1.0 for this smooth deterministic trajectory.
- [supported] For the 3-qubit GHZ under the specified Hamiltonian and phase damping, the dominant eigenvalue decreased from 1.0 to ≈0.548 at t = 10 while the second eigenvalue rose to ≈0.270, indicating emergence of a two-state classical mixture.
- [supported] Purity decay of the deterministic trajectory was fit to an exponential form yielding a fitted purity decay time T_purity ≈ 4.95 time units; a simple theoretical off-diagonal decay estimate for the GHZ under independent phase damping gives ∼6.67, and the paper attributes the difference to Hamiltonian-driven redistribution of coherence.
- [supported] Monte Carlo wavefunction statistics in the run: ≈79% of trajectories experienced zero jumps, ≈21% experienced one or more jumps, consistent with low phase-damping rates and Poisson statistics for the chosen parameters.
- [speculative] The FCE principle that 'any orbit, wave, wavelength, or waveform' can be characterized and predicted by extracting a fractal path via local curvature and T-based modulation is presented as a general framework but is not broadly validated beyond the provided test case.
- [speculative] Elements of the FCE design (for example the specific T-based mixing factor ϵ = 0.5 + 0.1 sin(π · Δκ) and the claimed naturalness of a factor of 7 from Bloch-sphere periodicity) are heuristic/modeling choices rather than derived from general theoretical proofs.
- [speculative] The authors propose that the FCE can be extended to non-Markovian dynamics, experimental data, and feedback-enhanced control, but these are suggested future directions and not empirically demonstrated here.
- [supported] Code and simulation artifacts (trajectories, unit tests, configuration, logs) are reported as available (Zenodo deposition) enabling reproducibility of the presented experiments.

**Results summary:** The paper presents a classical, Python-based quantum decoherence simulator and a proposed Fractal Correction Engine (FCE) that analyzes eigenvalue trajectories using curvature and fractal-dimension measures to predict future state evolution. Applied to a 3-qubit GHZ state under a transverse-field Ising Hamiltonian with independent phase damping, the simulator reproduces expected Lindblad and MCWF behavior and reveals a three-phase quantum-to-classical transition. The FCE, operating on the deterministic master-equation trajectory, produced high-fidelity purity predictions (Pearson r ≈ 0.998, MAE ≈ 0.023) and provided geometric diagnostics (curvature profile, Higuchi dimension D = 1) that correlate with dynamical regimes. The work is primarily a classical-simulation and algorithm-development contribution; broader claims about universal applicability of the FCE remain speculative and are proposed as future work.

## Limitations
- Early-stage prediction accuracy is lower (MAE ~0.043 in t<2) because the FCE requires sufficient trajectory history to build reliable curvature estimates (author-stated).
- The FCE shows a slight systematic underestimate of purity throughout the run (author-stated).
- The reported validation and results are for a single canonical test case (3-qubit GHZ state under a transverse-field Ising Hamiltonian with independent phase damping); generality to other system sizes, states, Hamiltonians, and noise models is not demonstrated (inferred).
- The main published runs use a small ensemble (20 trajectories: 1 master + 19 quantum-jump), limiting demonstrated Monte Carlo convergence and statistical robustness of ensemble claims (inferred).
- The FCE parameters and functional forms (e.g., mixing parameter & = 0.5 + 0.1 sin(7·Δκ), damping factor 0.1 in the exponential) appear ad hoc and lack rigorous justification or sensitivity analysis (inferred).
- The FCE fractal-dimension estimator yields D = 1.0 for the deterministic Lindblad trajectory; thus the method has not been stressed by irregular/discontinuous trajectories (quantum jumps) or experimental noise in the reported master-equation results (author-stated/inferred).
- Scalability to larger Hilbert spaces is not demonstrated; eigenvalue decomposition and per-eigenvalue curvature/fractal computations will become more expensive and may be impractical for many-qubit systems (inferred).
- Prediction reconstructs a density matrix from predicted eigenvalues and the most recent eigenvectors; this may misrepresent the true future state if eigenvectors evolve significantly between steps (inferred).
- Non-Markovian dynamics and memory effects are not tested in the reported results, although proposed as future work (author-stated).
- No application to experimental data is presented, so robustness to measurement noise, calibration errors, and real-device non-idealities is untested (author-stated/inferred).
- Higuchi fractal dimension is clamped to [1,2], which may limit the method's expressive power or its handling of trajectories with higher effective irregularity due to noise or measurement artifacts (inferred).
- Choice of fractal depth (set to 5) and other hyperparameters is not explored; sensitivity to these choices and guidelines for tuning are not provided (inferred).
- Uncertainty quantification is limited to point metrics (MAE, RMSE, Pearson); no confidence intervals or probabilistic calibration of predictions are provided (inferred).
- The theoretical connection between Frenet-Serret curvature/Higuchi dimension and physical decoherence rates is heuristic in the paper; formal guarantees or bounds on prediction error are not provided (inferred).
- Reported ensemble-averaging convergence (statement that 200 trajectories achieve agreement within tolerance 0.15) indicates non-negligible discrepancy at moderate ensemble sizes; the required trajectory counts for practical convergence are not fully characterized (inferred).
## Open questions
- How well does the FCE generalize to larger systems (more qubits) and higher-dimensional Hilbert spaces?
- How sensitive are FCE predictions to the choice of its ad-hoc parameters (e.g., the 7 scaling in the sine modulation, 0.1 damping factor, fractal depth)?
- Can the FCE reliably handle stochastic quantum-jump trajectories and experimental data with measurement noise, where Higuchi fractal dimension D > 1 is expected?
- How does the FCE perform under other types of noise channels (amplitude damping, depolarizing) and correlated or non-independent noise across qubits?
- What is the theoretical justification linking Frenet-Serret curvature and Higuchi fractal dimension to decoherence rates and purity evolution, and can error bounds be derived?
- How should one choose the trajectory history length required for stable curvature estimation and accurate prediction in different dynamical regimes?
- Can the FCE predict eigenvector evolution as well as eigenvalues, and if not, how does using the most recent eigenvectors limit state reconstruction fidelity?
- How robust are FCE predictions to model mismatch (e.g., incorrect assumed Hamiltonian or Lindblad operators) and to imperfect knowledge of system parameters?
- What computational resources and scaling behavior (time/memory) are required to run the FCE in practice on systems beyond few qubits?
- How does the FCE interact with feedback and control in a closed-loop setting — can it be used in real time on experimental platforms to slow or redirect decoherence?
- How does the FCE perform on non-Markovian dynamics where memory effects make eigenvalue trajectories more complex?
- Can the FCE be integrated with Quantum Darwinism analyses to detect and quantify pointer-state redundancy in the environment?
- What are the limits of the Higuchi estimator in quantum-eigenvalue time series that are short, noisy, or highly discontinuous, and are alternative fractal/complexity measures better suited?
- How many Monte Carlo trajectories are required for reliable ensemble recovery of the master equation for different noise strengths and system sizes?
- Can the systematic bias (underestimate of purity) be characterized and corrected analytically or via calibration?

**Future work:**
- Non-Markovian FCE analysis: Applying the FCE to non-Markovian dynamics where memory effects create more complex eigenvalue trajectories (D > 1).
- Quantum Darwinism integration: Using the FCE to track pointer state emergence and redundant encoding of classical information in the environment.
- Experimental data: Applying the FCE to real experimental decoherence data from superconducting qubits or trapped ions.
- Feedback-enhanced prediction: Combining FCE predictions with adaptive feedback control to actively slow or redirect the decoherence process.

## Contradictions
- The manuscript claims the FCE generalizes to arbitrary eigenvalue/waveform orbits and to non-Markovian or experimental data, but provides validation only on a single 3-qubit simulated GHZ scenario — a gap between generalization claims and demonstrated scalability/robustness.
- A simple theoretical GHZ coherence-decay estimate (T_GHZ ≈ 6.67) differs from the fitted purity-decay time measured in simulation (T_purity ≈ 4.95); the paper discusses possible reasons for the discrepancy but the difference highlights incompletely explained dynamics between theory and measured simulation.
- The FCE uses ad hoc correction terms (mixing parameter 0.5 + 0.1 sin(T * Delta_k) and a constant factor '7') motivated heuristically by geometry but not derived from first principles, which contradicts the strength of claims about physical interpretation and general applicability.
