## Abstract summary
This paper investigates using the HHL quantum algorithm to solve portfolio optimization problems formulated as quadratic programs with equality constraints. The authors implement and test the approach (via Qiskit on IBM hardware/simulator) on a small three-asset example, showing the HHL-derived solution closely matches the exact classical solution and demonstrating the method's potential for accelerating certain financial optimization tasks. This paper surveys forensic issues for unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) and presents key techniques for extracting evidentiary data from UAV subsystems (mobile apps, onboard storage/SD cards, flight logs and ground control software). Using a DJI Mavic 2 Pro case study it demonstrates practical methods for recovering flight paths, battery and telemetry data and discusses challenges such as encrypted storage and the need for specialized tools. The work provides an exploratory foundation and recommended procedures for future UAV forensic research and investigations.
## Methodology
The authors formulate a portfolio optimization problem (quadratic programming with equality constraints) as a linear system A x = b via Lagrange multipliers. They apply the HHL quantum algorithm to solve this linear system and thus obtain the portfolio weights. The HHL implementation follows the standard stages: prepare the normalized state |b>, perform Quantum Phase Estimation (QPE) on the unitary e^{iAt0} to estimate eigenvalues, apply controlled rotations on an ancilla qubit inversely proportional to estimated eigenvalues, uncompute (inverse QPE) and measure the ancilla. On post-selecting the desired ancilla outcome the register contains an approximation |x~> proportional to the solution x = A^{-1} b; amplitudes are normalized and converted to portfolio proportions. The paper implements this HHL circuit with Qiskit on IBM’s quantum computing platform and demonstrates the method on a 3-asset toy portfolio example (expanded to an 8-dimensional linear system to match qubit register sizing). The authors detail parameter choices used for the HHL circuit (e.g. phase-estimation precision), describe normalization/padding of the linear system to fit qubit registers, and compare the approximate quantum solution against the exact classical solution. This paper proposes and evaluates a quantum Hidden Markov Model (QHMM) approach for stock-forecasting. The QHMM models the evolution of a quantum state ρ and describes observation probabilities via Kraus operators K_y, i.e. p(y_t | ρ_{t-1}) = tr(K_{y_{t-1}} ρ_{t-1} K_{y_{t-1}}^†). The learning objective is the maximum likelihood of the observed sequence; the Kraus operators are constrained by K^† K = I, so training is posed as constrained optimization on a Stiefel manifold. The constrained problem is converted to an unconstrained update using a Stiefel-manifold gradient-descent style update (authors give an explicit matrix update formula involving step size τ, matrices U and V constructed from the gradient g and current K). A DA metric (a quality measure computed from log-likelihood of the data under the model) is used to assess model quality and guide iterations; models are iteratively updated and judged with DA until convergence. For experiments, initial density matrices and Kraus parameters are randomly generated in Matlab, the gradient-descent updates are run until DA stabilizes (empirically after ~20 iterations), and the trained model is used to make discrete predictions (selecting the class/index minimizing a score J = - ln tr(K_n ρ K_n^†)), which are evaluated both as forecast accuracy and in a simple trading simulation (buy on predicted up-day, sell on predicted down-day). The paper reports example results on CSI-300 constituent stock(s) (e.g., 600519), showing convergence of DA and a trading example with a one-week gain (~+6.7%) on a toy trading simulation.

**Algorithms used:** HHL (Harrow-Hassidim-Lloyd) quantum linear system algorithm, Quantum Phase Estimation (QPE), Controlled rotation and inverse-QPE (uncompute) steps of HHL, Quantum Hidden Markov Model (Kraus-operator formulation), Likelihood maximization (MLE), Gradient descent on Stiefel manifold (constrained optimization update), Model quality metric DA (log-likelihood based), Simple trading simulation for backtesting
**Frameworks:** Qiskit, MATLAB (custom scripts)

**Experimental setup:** Implemented using Qiskit on the IBM Quantum platform. A 3-asset toy portfolio is mapped to an 8-dimensional linear system (padded) so that the data register uses 3 qubits; the QPE register uses t=2 qubits (precision bits), plus an ancilla; the full HHL circuit as tested required 9 qubits in total. Phase-estimation was initialized with an estimated success probability of 0.9 and 2 bits of precision.

**Dataset:** Synthetic toy portfolio constructed by the authors: 3 assets with expected returns μ = [0.05, 0.10, 0.20], a covariance/ correlation matrix (3×3) specified in the paper, and a total-investment constraint of ξ = 1. The QP/equations are converted to an 8×8 linear system by padding the vector b to length 8 so that it can be encoded into 3 qubits.

## Findings
- [supported] The HHL algorithm can be formulated and applied to portfolio optimization framed as a quadratic program with equality constraints (reduced to solving a linear system Ax = b).
- [supported] The authors implemented an HHL-based solver (via Qiskit on IBM’s platform) for a 3-asset toy portfolio problem and obtained an approximate solution whose relative ratios match the exact classical solution closely (approx. 0.1608, 0.2557, 0.5833 vs exact 0.16216216, 0.25675676, 0.58108108).
- [supported] The implementation used a 9-qubit circuit for the example, and the authors describe the need to postselect on the ancilla=1 & phase-register=0 measurement to obtain the approximate solution state.
- [supported] The authors observed amplitude (normalization) differences between HHL output and exact solution and note the need for more qubits (or improved approximation) to get closer agreement in amplitude as well as ratios.
- [speculative] Under certain assumptions, HHL can provide exponential speedup versus classical linear-system solvers (this is a theoretical claim often associated with HHL in the literature).
- [speculative] For some real-world optimization instances the HHL output may provide only relative ratios between solution components (not direct absolute coefficients) unless additional steps are taken—this is suggested by the authors as an open limitation.
- [supported] Practical issues remain: preconditions for HHL (Hermitian A, state preparation, readout) and circuit depth/precision make scaling to larger problem sizes non-trivial; the authors note these and suggest future work on improvements.
- [supported] The authors propose a quantum-inspired Hidden Quantum Markov Model (QHMM) formulation for stock/time-series forecasting where classical HMM transition/observation operators are replaced by quantum Kraus-operator representations and trained via a constrained optimization (Stiefel-manifold-like) gradient procedure.
- [supported] The paper implements the QHMM training procedure (gradient updates of Kraus matrices, DA metric evaluation) and reports empirical convergence of the DA metric (used to judge model quality) for their datasets when the number of gradient-descent iterations exceeds ≈20.
- [supported] On a small empirical trading simulation using CSI-300 related equities, applying the QHMM-based forecasts in a simple trading rule produced a reported portfolio increase of ~6.7% over a one-week test period (initial capital of 1,000,000 raised to 1,067,641.75) in their experimental scenario.
- [speculative] The authors claim QHMM reduces the number of parameters by consolidating HMM A/C matrices into Kraus operator matrices while achieving performance similar to classical HMMs; this is presented as a motivation and qualitative claim but lacks broad empirical benchmarking vs classical HMMs across multiple datasets.
- [speculative] The authors suggest the QHMM framework provides a feasible approach to combine quantum-algebraic modeling ideas with classical time-series forecasting in finance; this is stated as a broader claim about potential applicability rather than rigorously demonstrated quantum advantage.
- [supported] The paper gives explicit algorithmic details (mapping HMM likelihoods to Kraus operators, constrained optimization update formula, use of a DA score and pseudo-labeling for fully supervised-like scenarios) and reports that these components were realized in MATLAB/Python experiments.
- [speculative] The manuscript asserts that QHMMs are especially promising in ‘restricted, fully-supervised’ or small-data settings (motivated by applications like medical imaging / limited datasets). This is plausible but not substantiated by systematic comparisons to modern classical small-data methods (e.g., regularized HMMs, Bayesian HMMs, or deep learning baselines) in the paper.

**Results summary:** The paper formulates portfolio optimization as a linear-system problem and applies the HHL quantum linear solver. A proof-of-concept implementation (Qiskit/IBM) for a 3-asset example produced an approximate normalized solution whose component ratios closely match the exact classical solution. The authors report the realization used a 9-qubit circuit and discuss observed amplitude (normalization) differences. They conclude HHL is feasible for small instances, note practical scaling and precision issues, and situate HHL’s exponential speedup claim as a theoretical result that requires strong assumptions to hold in practice. The paper introduces a quantum-inspired Hidden Markov framework (QHMM) for stock forecasting that replaces conventional HMM matrices with Kraus operators and optimizes them via constrained gradient updates. Implemented classically, the method was tested on CSI-300 related equities; the authors report DA metric convergence for modest iteration counts (>20) and show a one-week trading simulation where initial capital increased by ~6.7% using QHMM-based predictions. The authors argue QHMMs can encode time-evolving stochastic behavior with potentially fewer parameters, and they present the training and optimization recipe. However, broad empirical comparisons to classical baselines across diverse datasets are limited, and claims of parameter/efficiency advantages remain largely qualitative.

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** theoretical

The paper cites the standard theoretical claim that HHL can give exponential acceleration for linear-system solving under restrictive assumptions (e.g., a suitably well-conditioned, sparse, Hermitian A, efficient state preparation and readout). The authors did not empirically demonstrate a runtime advantage: their contribution is a small-scale proof-of-concept showing correctness in ratios, not a demonstration of speedup for practical, large-scale portfolio problems.
## Limitations
- Demonstration is limited to a very small-scale portfolio (3 assets); results are only shown for a toy problem.
- Accuracy depends on number of qubits: improving approximation requires more qubits and gates, which rapidly increases circuit size and complexity.
- The HHL-based solution produced only relative ratios between portfolio components (amplitude ratios) rather than guaranteed exact coefficient magnitudes; obtaining exact amplitudes was noted as problematic.
- Authors did not benchmark resource or runtime advantages against state-of-the-art classical solvers on practically-sized problems.
- [inferred] HHL relies on restrictive assumptions (e.g., access to a Hermitian matrix, ability to implement e^{iAt} efficiently, sparsity or structure, favorable condition number); these assumptions may not hold for real-world portfolio matrices (covariance matrices are typically dense and ill-conditioned).
- [inferred] The paper does not address state preparation and measurement/readout overheads (cost of loading classical portfolio data into quantum states and extracting useful classical information).
- [inferred] No demonstration on noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) hardware; physical noise, decoherence and gate errors will degrade performance and are not considered.
- [inferred] Gate depth and qubit-count scaling for realistic portfolio sizes (tens to thousands of assets) are not analyzed; practicality at scale is unclear.
- Experiments were limited in scope and scale (few stocks tested; example shown for stock 600519 only) and used short evaluation horizons (one-week trading simulation).
- The reported performance (6.7% gain) is from a small-scale, short-term backtest and lacks comparison with standard classical baselines (e.g., classical HMM, ARIMA, or ML models) or benchmark trading strategies.
- The paper uses a simplified matching / scoring function for final decision-making (authors note: "the spectral matching score algorithm is not the key of this paper, so the simplest method is used"), which limits the strength of empirical conclusions.
- The model and experiments are effectively classical simulations of the proposed quantum-hidden-Markov approach (no implementation or evaluation on real quantum hardware).
- Optimization/learning procedure details (choice of hyperparameters such as learning rate, gradient-descent settings, initialization) are limited and sensitivity/robustness analyses are not presented.
- No transaction cost, slippage, market-impact or practical trading constraints were considered in the trading simulation (i.e., frictionless market assumption).
- Model theoretical properties (e.g., convergence guarantees of the gradient-descent procedure under the matrix constraints, uniqueness of solution) are not fully analyzed.
- [inferred] The paper does not report statistical signiﬁcance or confidence intervals for the financial results, so it is unclear whether observed gains are robust versus random variation.
- [inferred] The method of constructing / generating Kraus operators via gradient descent may be prone to local minima and dependent on initialization; this sensitivity was not analyzed.
- [inferred] Practical deployment issues (computational cost for large universes of stocks, latency for real-time trading) are not explored in depth.

## Contradictions
- contradiction:scalability — While the paper reiterates the theoretical exponential speedup claim of HHL under idealized assumptions, the empirical demonstration (3-asset, padded to 8-dim, 9 qubits) and the authors' discussion emphasize practical barriers (state preparation, precision, postselection, circuit depth and qubit requirements) that undermine claims of near-term or straightforward scaling to real-world financial problems (contrasts blanket assertions of HHL's practical advantage).
- The authors claim QHMM reduces parameter counts and can match or improve on classical HMMs, but provide no systematic benchmarking against classical HMMs or other baselines across datasets — an unsupported claim that contradicts the strength of the asserted quantum advantage (contradiction:classical-vs-quantum).
- The paper suggests feasibility/scalability of the QHMM approach, yet all evidence is small-scale MATLAB simulation and a one-week toy backtest; there is no demonstration of scaling or production readiness, contradicting claims about practical scalability (contradiction:scalability).
