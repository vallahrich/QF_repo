## Abstract summary
The paper compares quantum optimization techniques (gate-model VQE and QAOA via Qiskit, and D-Wave's hybrid CQM quantum annealing) against a classical Monte Carlo approach for Markowitz mean-variance portfolio optimization using historical data of 48 NSE stocks. The authors introduce a bucketing technique to handle qubit limits for gate-model methods, build 16-asset portfolios, and report comparable portfolio performance across methods while observing faster runtimes for the quantum implementations (especially the D-Wave CQM) versus the classical method.
## Methodology
The study performs a comparative empirical evaluation of classical and quantum optimization approaches for Markowitz mean-variance portfolio construction using daily closing prices for 48 NSE stocks (01-01-2011 to 01-11-2021). The methodology first computes per-asset statistics (mean, variance) and pairwise covariances/correlations from training data (01-01-2011 to 31-12-2016) and uses 2017–2021 data for validation. For gate-model experiments (Qiskit) the authors partition the 48 assets into 4 buckets of 12 assets each based on asset standard deviation to address qubit limits; each 12-asset bucket is formulated as a quadratic program and converted to a QUBO. Hybrid variational algorithms (VQE and QAOA) are executed on a local statevector simulator with a RealAmplitude ansatz (3 repetitions) and SLSQP as the classical optimizer to select 4 assets per bucket; asset weights are then allocated classically to form a 16-asset portfolio. For quantum annealing experiments the full 48-asset optimization is formulated as a Constrained Quadratic Model (CQM) with objective terms for return and risk and constraints (e.g., budget) and solved with D-Wave's LeapHybridCQMSampler, which directly returns selected assets and weights. The classical baseline uses a Monte Carlo sampling approach: random weight vectors (uniformly initialized between 0 and 1), annualization of returns/variance, repeated simulations, and selection of the portfolio with the highest Sharpe ratio. Results are reported as selected asset lists and weights, runtime for each method, and performance benchmarking against the Nifty50 index via CAGR over multiple horizons (1-, 3-, and 5-year).

**Algorithms used:** VQE, QAOA, Constrained Quadratic Model (CQM) via D-Wave LeapHybridCQMSampler, Quantum Annealing (hybrid), Classical Monte Carlo, SLSQP optimizer
**Frameworks:** Qiskit (Qiskit SDK), D-Wave (LeapHybrid / Ocean CQM), Local statevector simulator (Qiskit)

**Experimental setup:** Gate-model experiments used a local statevector simulator (Qiskit) limited to 12 qubits per run; assets were bucketed into 4 groups of 12 assets each. VQE and QAOA were run per-bucket. Quantum annealing experiments used D-Wave's LeapHybridCQM solver on the full 48-asset problem. Classical experiments used a Monte Carlo simulation implemented on classical hardware. Reported end-to-end execution times: VQE ~11 mins (per bucket), QAOA ~3.33 mins (per bucket), D-Wave CQM ~44 sec (full 48 assets), Classical Monte Carlo ~16 hrs.

**Dataset:** Daily closing prices for 48 NSE stocks from 01-01-2011 to 01-11-2021. Training split: 01-01-2011 to 31-12-2016; validation split: 01-01-2017 to 01-11-2021. Data source referenced as Yahoo Finance.

**Results summary:** The authors applied Markowitz mean-variance portfolio optimization to a universe of 48 NSE stocks (training: 2011–2016, validation: 2017–2021) and compared four approaches: classical Monte Carlo, Qiskit VQE, Qiskit QAOA (both run on a 12-qubit state-vector simulator using a 4-bucket splitting strategy), and D-Wave's hybrid CQM solver (handling all 48 variables). They produced 16-asset portfolios from each method and report similar financial performance (CAGR) across methods. Measured execution times favored quantum methods in their experiments: VQE ≈11 minutes per bucket, QAOA ≈3.33 minutes per bucket, D-Wave CQM ≈44 seconds for the full problem, versus ≈16 hours for the classical Monte Carlo run. The authors conclude quantum methods gave comparable portfolio quality while being faster in this experimental setup and outline future work to test other hardware and frameworks.

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** demonstrated

Within the authors' experimental setup and datasets, quantum-based implementations (QAOA/VQE on a 12-qubit simulator with bucketing, and D-Wave CQM on the full 48-asset problem) completed optimization runs substantially faster than the classical Monte Carlo baseline while producing comparable portfolio performance. The paper's advantage claim is therefore demonstrated empirically but is limited to their specific configuration, simulators/hardware, and classical baseline.
## Limitations
- Qubit limitations on gate-model simulators and hardware (state-vector simulator limited to 12 qubits), forcing a bucketing workaround to handle 48 assets
- Use of simulators (local state-vector) for VQE/QAOA rather than execution on real noisy gate-model devices
- The portfolio construction was reduced from 48 to a 16-asset portfolio via bucketing and selection, which may affect global optimality
- Comparison limited to a small set of providers/hardware (IBM Qiskit gate-model simulators and D-Wave LeapHybridCQM), not covering other cloud quantum backends
- Classical baseline uses Monte Carlo only; other classical optimization solvers (exact/QP solvers, advanced convex or integer programming methods) were not compared
- Limited problem size in gate-model experiments due to current NISQ constraints (necessitating problem decomposition/bucketing)
- Reported execution-times combine fundamentally different runtimes/environments (local simulator vs D-Wave hybrid service vs classical CPU runs) which complicates direct fair comparison
- Solution quality reporting is limited (results compared via CAGR and weights; limited reporting of objective function values, statistical significance, or optimality gap)
- Experimental results are based on one historical dataset (48 NSE stocks, fixed train/validation split); generalizability across markets/timeframes is not established
- Specific algorithmic choices fixed (e.g., SLSQP optimizer, RealAmplitude ansatz with 3 repetitions) — sensitivity to optimizer/ansatz hyperparameters was not explored
- D-Wave CQM/HSS is used as provided (hybrid classical-quantum heuristics); internal workings and dependence on hybrid scheduling not examined
- [inferred] No noise or robustness analysis for gate-model algorithms (VQE/QAOA) under realistic device noise models was reported
- [inferred] The bucketing method (standard deviation-based buckets) is heuristic and its impact on final portfolio optimality and stability was not quantified
- [inferred] Lack of a controlled, hardware-normalized benchmarking methodology — differences may reflect resource differences rather than algorithmic advantage

- #idea:hybrid-approach — The study uses hybrid approaches at two levels: D-Wave's hybrid CQM solver (quantum+classical) for full 48-asset optimization and a classical post-processing step to allocate weights after per-bucket gate-model selection, showing hybrid pipelines are essential today.
- #limitation:qubit-count — Gate-model experiments were constrained to 12 qubits per run, necessitating a 4-bucket decomposition (12 assets each) to scale to 48 assets.
- #limitation:simulation-only — VQE and QAOA runs were executed on a local statevector simulator (not on gate-model hardware), so reported variational results do not include real-device noise effects and may be optimistic.
- #idea:quantum-advantage — QAOA runs on the simulator were faster per-bucket (~3.33 mins) than VQE (~11 mins) in this setup, suggesting algorithm choice matters for runtime performance in variational approaches.
- #idea:quantum-advantage — Despite the need for decomposition and classical post-processing, quantum-based methods achieved comparable out-of-sample CAGR performance vs the classical Monte Carlo baseline over multiple horizons.
## Contradictions
- The paper asserts broadly that 'Quantum Computing paradigm is inherently capable of solving NP-hard problems' — this contradicts standard complexity-theoretic understanding that quantum computers do not generally solve NP-hard problems in polynomial time.
- The claimed empirical runtime advantage (D-Wave CQM vs classical Monte Carlo) is specific to the authors' implementations and baseline; it may not generalize — the paper does not rule out that a more optimized classical solver or different problem encoding could remove the reported advantage.
- The bucketing approach is presented as a scalable method for gate-model limits, but this decomposition changes the problem structure and has not been proven equivalent to solving the full joint optimization; thus claims about scalability using bucketing are not fully supported and potentially misleading.
