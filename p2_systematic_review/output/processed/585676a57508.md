## Abstract summary
The paper presents a computational model to compute ground-state energies of neutral and charged excitons (trions) in semiconductor quantum dots using a variational Quantum Monte Carlo method combined with effective-mass Hamiltonians. The method employs a Newton–Raphson optimization of variational parameters, optional OpenMP parallelization, and provides Fortran programs with illustrative calculations for CdSe nanoplatelets; results match full variational calculations and outperform configuration interaction approaches in efficiency.
## Methodology
The authors develop a computational approach combining variational Quantum Monte Carlo (VQMC) with effective-mass Hamiltonians to compute ground-state energies of excitons and trions in semiconductor quantum dots (specifically cuboidal nanoplatelets). Single-particle states are modelled with particle-in-a-box envelope functions (analytic cosines) and interactions are included via dielectric-aware Coulomb and self-energy potentials. Correlation is introduced through compact Jastrow factors (short-range Slater and Padé forms) with a small set of variational parameters. The VQMC uses Metropolis importance sampling (with trial moves applied preferentially in relative coordinates to handle short-range Coulomb singularities) and a local-energy estimator that includes both accepted and rejected trial moves to reduce variance. Kinetic-energy contributions are evaluated using expressions based on gradients of the log wavefunction to keep variance low. Optimization of variational parameters is performed with an iterative Newton–Raphson solver using analytically-derived estimates of the energy gradient and Hessian (with symmetrization and covariance forms to reduce stochastic variance). Computational implementation is in Fortran 90 (program vqmc-ema) with optional OpenMP parallelization that distributes Monte Carlo walkers across threads. The method is benchmarked on CdSe nanoplatelets, comparing VQMC results to exact variational integration and configuration-interaction (CI) calculations, and reporting energies, binding energies, and computational scaling (time vs threads/walkers).

**Algorithms used:** Variational Quantum Monte Carlo (VQMC), Metropolis-Hastings Monte Carlo (importance sampling), Newton–Raphson optimization (for variational parameters), Jastrow-correlated trial wavefunctions (Slater and Padé forms), Particle-in-a-box single-particle basis (analytic envelope functions), Configuration Interaction (CI) — used as baseline/comparison
**Frameworks:** vqmc-ema (custom Fortran 90 code), Fortran 90, OpenMP, Intel Fortran compiler (used in experiments)

**Experimental setup:** Computations performed on a workstation with Dual Intel Xeon E5-2660v4 CPUs (2.2 GHz) supporting up to 40 threads. The code was compiled with the Intel Fortran compiler and linked against OpenMP (libomp). Benchmarks varied number of Monte Carlo walkers and threads to measure wall-clock time per Newton–Raphson iteration.

## Findings
- [supported] A variational Quantum Monte Carlo (VQMC) method integrated with effective-mass Hamiltonians and a Newton–Raphson optimizer can be used to compute ground-state energies of neutral and charged excitons (trions) in semiconductor quantum dots/nanoplatelets.
- [supported] The authors supply Fortran 90 codes (vqmc-ema) implementing the method, including optional OpenMP parallelization of random walkers.
- [supported] By using short-range Jastrow (Slater/Padé) correlating factors the trial wavefunctions capture both weak- and strong-interaction limits and satisfy Kato-cusp behavior in large-platelet limits.
- [supported] The VQMC results match full variational integration reference calculations to sub-meV accuracy for the studied CdSe nanoplatelet cases (with sufficient Monte Carlo sampling).
- [supported] The VQMC approach yields significantly better accuracy than configuration-interaction (CI) calculations for exciton and trion binding energies in the presented examples; CI with tested basis sizes underestimates binding energies by tens of meV.
- [supported] The VQMC implementation is computationally efficient and parallelizes well with OpenMP: typical runs on the tested workstation take a few minutes per Newton–Raphson optimization (vs. CI runs that took days for analogous problems).
- [supported] Dielectric mismatch (ǫ_out < ǫ_in) increases excitonic energies in the studied nanoplatelets primarily via self-energy (image-charge) repulsion; the effect is more pronounced for trions due to more particles.
- [supported] For the example platelet (Lx, Ly, Lz)=(30,10,1.4) nm, the optimal variational parameter α minimizing exciton energy is ≈0.72; α→1 in the weak-confinement (large lateral size) limit consistent with 2D cusp expectations.
- [speculative] The authors state the model can be easily extended to other QD shapes, confinement potentials and material parameters within the effective-mass/envelope-function framework (extension not demonstrated in this work).

**Results summary:** The paper introduces a VQMC method combined with effective-mass Hamiltonians and a Newton–Raphson variational optimizer to compute ground-state energies of excitons and trions in semiconductor nanoplatelets. Using simple analytical single-particle envelopes and short-range Jastrow correlators, the method attains sub-meV agreement with full variational reference results for CdSe nanoplatelets when sufficiently sampled, and captures short-range correlations and cusp behavior. Benchmarks show the approach is substantially more accurate than CI with the tested basis sets and is computationally efficient (minutes on a multi-core workstation with OpenMP), scalable with the number of walkers/threads, and requires minimal memory. The method captures dielectric-confinement effects and can optimize multiple variational parameters for neutral and charged excitonic complexes.

**Performance claims:**
- Sub-meV agreement with full variational integration references for exciton energies in studied CdSe nanoplatelets when Monte Carlo sampling is increased (reported convergence when total sample points N increased from 10^7 to 10^9).
- Typical VQMC execution times of a few minutes per Newton–Raphson optimization on a Dual Intel Xeon E5-2660v4 workstation (parallelized with OpenMP).
- Configuration-interaction (CI) calculations for comparable systems required several days and produced binding-energy errors of tens of meV versus the VQMC reference in the presented examples.
- For the example nanoplatelet (30×10×1.4 nm) the optimal exciton variational parameter α ≈ 0.72 (with α→1 in the large-platelet limit).
- Trion VQMC runtimes are about 1.5× longer than exciton runtimes for the same sampling due to additional coordinates and parameters.
- Near-ideal parallel scaling with number of OpenMP threads up to the tested 40 threads; near-linear scaling with number of walkers.

## Limitations
- VQMC provides a description of the lowest state of each symmetry only (it does not directly yield excited states or excited-state spectra).
- The variational energy is fundamentally limited by the choice of the trial wave function; accuracy depends on the chosen Jastrow/envelope parametrization.
- Estimation of the derivatives required by the Newton–Raphson optimization (Eqs. 18–20) is the most demanding step and a primary source of computational cost/variance.
- Trade-off between simplicity and accuracy in the chosen trial functions: the authors intentionally use a small number of variational parameters (e.g., simple Slater and Padé Jastrow forms) which may limit capture of more complex correlations compared to more sophisticated trial functions.
- [inferred] The model relies on single-band effective-mass / envelope-function (k·p) Hamiltonians and neglects atomistic, multiband, non-parabolic, strain or spin–orbit effects, which may be important in some materials or regimes.
- [inferred] The implementation used hard-wall (infinite) confinement and particular simplifications (e.g., Jastrow factors that consider in-plane correlations only) that may reduce accuracy when those approximations are not well justified (e.g., when thickness is not negligible).
- [inferred] Dielectric polarization and self-energy are modeled using quantum-well based approximations; this modeling choice may introduce errors for geometries or dielectric environments that deviate from the assumptions.
- [inferred] The provided parallelization is shared-memory (OpenMP) only, limiting straightforward scaling to large multi-node HPC systems (no MPI implementation provided).
- [inferred] The method is focused on ground-state energies and variational parameters; other properties of interest (e.g., radiative lifetimes, detailed optical spectra, dynamical properties) are not addressed in the present work and would require extensions.
## Open questions
- How can excited states (beyond the ground state of a given symmetry) be obtained within this framework or with reasonable extensions?
- How transferable and accurate is the VQMC–effective-mass approach for other materials, shapes, boundary conditions and regimes beyond the CdSe nanoplatelets studied here?
- To what extent would more sophisticated trial wave functions (with more variational parameters or different functional forms) improve accuracy for trions and larger excitonic complexes, and what is the computational trade-off?
- How well do the VQMC predictions (energies, binding energies) compare directly with experimental measurements across a range of dielectric environments and geometries?
- How important are multiband, non-parabolic, strain, spin–orbit and atomistic effects for the excitonic complexes studied, and how can they be incorporated into the present VQMC framework?
- Would moving from variational QMC to diffusion QMC or other QMC variants produce significant accuracy gains for these systems that justify the additional computational cost?
- How does the approximation for self-energy and polarization (quantum-well-style image-charge sums) affect quantitative results for more complex geometries or non-uniform dielectric surroundings?
- What are the practical scaling limits of the approach for larger systems (more particles, larger volumes) and what strategies (algorithmic or parallelization) are required to handle them?
