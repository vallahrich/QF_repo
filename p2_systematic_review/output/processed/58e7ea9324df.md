## Abstract summary
This technical report (NEASQC D5.4) develops and evaluates quantum algorithms for derivative pricing and Value at Risk (VaR), focusing on Quantum Accelerated Monte Carlo (QAMC). It introduces a new encoding protocol that can handle negative payoffs and a novel Real Quantum Amplitude Estimation (RQAE) algorithm that is sensitive to amplitude sign/phases; experiments show RQAE performs comparably to state-of-the-art AE methods but the new encoding incurs a large Npaths factor that can negate speedups and the overall approach is not yet feasible on current NISQ hardware. The authors also provide an accompanying QQuantLib software library and benchmark results.
## Methodology
The authors developed and implemented quantum algorithms for pricing and VaR estimation, focusing on two contributions: a new quantum encoding protocol that can represent payoffs with negative values, and a new amplitude-estimation routine (RQAE) that recovers signed amplitudes. They implemented a software library (QQuantLib) in Python (using Atos Quantum Learning Machine (QLM) 1.5.1) that provides data-loading routines, Grover-like amplitude-amplification construction, phase-estimation and amplitude-estimation algorithms (CQPEAE, IQPEAE, IQAE, MLAE, and RQAE). Experiments were simulation-based: they encoded Black–Scholes terminal distributions (via Euler–Maruyama discretization) into quantum oracles using either the standard encoding or the new encoding, executed amplitude-estimation runs with different algorithms and parameter settings, and measured the absolute error between the estimated expectation (via the quantum routine) and the Riemann-sum reference. The evaluation varied algorithmic parameters (auxiliary qubits, phase-estimation bits, epsilons, confidence levels, MLAE schedules, RQAE amplification ratios q, shots, and repetitions) and used both the Atos QLM simulator and an HPC node (FinisTerrae III) to run larger experiments. Results are reported as absolute error vs number of oracle calls (Noracle) and comparisons to theoretical scalings.

**Algorithms used:** Classical Monte Carlo (CMC), Euler-Maruyama (SDE discretization), Quantum Accelerated Monte Carlo (QAMC), Grover-like amplitude amplification, Classical quantum-phase-estimation based AE (CQPEAE), Iterative quantum-phase-estimation based AE (IQPEAE), Iterative Quantum Amplitude Estimation (IQAE), Maximum Likelihood Amplitude Estimation (MLAE), Real Quantum Amplitude Estimation (RQAE), Walsh-Hadamard transform (state preparation / Hadamard)
**Frameworks:** Atos Quantum Learning Machine (QLM) 1.5.1, QQuantLib (custom Python library developed by authors), Python 3.9.9

**Experimental setup:** Simulated experiments using Atos QLM (quantum circuit simulator, up to 30 qubits, 96 cores, 1.5 TB RAM) and a FinisTerrae III compute node (Intel Xeon Ice Lake 835Y, variable cores and 256 GB RAM). The QQuantLib library implemented oracles and AE algorithms. Experiments used repeats per configuration (varied by algorithm), shots typically 100 for sampling-based routines, and measured absolute error vs number of oracle calls. Both standard and new encodings were evaluated across multiple payoff types (European call/put, digital call/put, futures) under Black–Scholes dynamics.

**Dataset:** Synthetic data: simulated terminal asset price distributions from a Black–Scholes model (SDE sampled via Euler–Maruyama). No real-market dataset was used.

## Findings
- [theoretical] Quantum Accelerated Monte Carlo (QAMC) offers a quadratic improvement in sampling error scaling over Classical Monte Carlo (CMC): CMC error ∼ 1/sqrt(C) vs QAMC error ∼ 1/C (where C is cost / number of SDE evaluations).
- [supported] Standard amplitude-encoding AE pipelines (the 'standard encoding') return quantities proportional to the sum of absolute values of p(Si)V(Si) and therefore fail to recover correct prices when the payoff can be negative (example shown for payoff V(S)=S−K).
- [supported] A new encoding protocol (additional qubit + Hadamard transforms + modified US/UV) can encode signed Riemann sums and in simulator experiments converges to the correct expectation even when payoffs are negative.
- [supported] The new encoding protocol incurs a large multiplicative factor proportional to Npaths in the relationship between the measured amplitude and the desired expectation (Equation (3.9)), which in practice degrades performance and can erase the theoretical quantum speedup.
- [supported] The authors implemented a new Amplitude Estimation algorithm, Real Quantum Amplitude Estimation (RQAE), which is able to recover signed amplitudes (i.e., phase/sign information) and in their simulations is the only algorithm tested that converges to the correct sign of negative payoffs.
- [theoretical] RQAE has provably tighter convergence bounds than prior AE methods as shown in Manzano et al. (2022) (cited/proven in that companion work).
- [supported] In the reported numerical experiments RQAE is competitive with state-of-the-art AE algorithms (MLAE, IQAE, CQPEAE, IQPEAE): it achieves comparable error behavior though it may require more oracle calls in some settings.
- [supported] Among existing AE variants tested, Maximum Likelihood AE (MLAE) gives the best performance when carefully tuned; Iterative Quantum AE (IQAE) is the most stable without fine tuning.
- [supported] Practical deployment is not feasible on current NISQ hardware: implementing US (oracle preparing SDE path superposition) requires many qubits; Grover-like AE subroutines require deep circuits and large numbers of gates, exceeding current decoherence and error rates.
- [supported] The authors produced and released a QQuantLib software library (FinancialApplications repo) implementing the encodings, multiple AE algorithms (MLAE, IQAE, CQPEAE, IQPEAE, RQAE), and benchmark workflows.
- [speculative] To make QAMC practically useful one must reduce both depth and qubit count by developing more efficient quantum methods to simulate the SDE (open research direction / recommended focus).
- [supported] RQAE exposes an amplification-ratio hyperparameter q controlling depth vs convergence: larger q → shallower circuits but slower convergence; smaller q → deeper circuits but faster convergence (demonstrated in experiments with q values such as 2 and 20).

**Results summary:** This technical report evaluates quantum-accelerated Monte Carlo approaches for option pricing and VaR, introduces a new data-encoding protocol that can represent signed payoffs, and presents a novel Amplitude Estimation algorithm (RQAE) able to recover signed amplitudes. Simulations (using a released QQuantLib library) show that standard AE pipelines fail for payoffs that can be negative while the new encoding + RQAE recover correct signed expectations. However, the new encoding imposes a multiplicative Npaths factor that degrades practical performance. RQAE is empirically competitive with established AE variants (MLAE, IQAE, CQPEAE, IQPEAE) and is provably tight in convergence in a companion theoretical work. The work concludes that despite theoretical quadratic speedups, current algorithms remain impractical on NISQ hardware because of qubit and depth requirements, and recommends further research on efficient SDE encodings and lower-depth oracles.

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** theoretical

The report reiterates the literature result that QAMC yields a quadratic improvement in sampling error (1/C vs 1/sqrt(C)) and demonstrates algorithmic primitives (encodings and RQAE) in simulators; however, no practical quantum advantage is demonstrated on current hardware—resource (qubit/depth/error) requirements make real-world advantage speculative at present.
## Limitations
- New encoding algorithm has a large multiplicative factor (Npaths) that can negate or eliminate the theoretical quantum speedup.
- Proposed methods (encoding + AE) are not yet applicable to current NISQ hardware because they require deep circuits and many qubits.
- Amplitude estimation routines (Grover-like / QPE-based) demand circuit depths that exceed current decoherence times and gate fidelities.
- Implementation of the oracle US (state preparation / SDE simulation) as described requires an excessively large number of qubits in practice.
- Total gate counts (oracle + AE) demand error rates beyond current hardware capabilities; error correction would be required.
- The standard AE approaches estimate probabilities (squared amplitudes) and fail to recover sign information for payoffs that can be negative; motivating RQAE.
- RQAE, while novel and proven to have tight convergence bounds theoretically, does not outperform the best existing AE methods in all empirical settings and may require more oracle calls in practice.
- The work assumes that operators Up and UV (state preparation and payoff-loading unitaries) can be implemented efficiently; practical efficient constructions may be unavailable for many realistic distributions and payoffs.
- Experimental results are obtained on simulators (QLM / HPC nodes); effects of noise and other hardware errors on real QPUs are not evaluated in this report.
- [inferred] The choice of the RQAE amplification ratio parameter q trades circuit depth for convergence speed, and there is no general prescription for selecting q optimally across problems.
- [inferred] The scaling analysis and experiments focus on single-asset Black–Scholes-like setups; extension to multi-asset (higher-dimensional) SDEs and the associated state-preparation complexity is not solved and may be substantially harder.
- [inferred] The proposed new encoding requires one additional qubit and extra transforms (Hadamards, double encodings) which may introduce further circuit overhead and noise sensitivity.
- [inferred] Efficient implementation of step-function oracles used for VaR (indicator functions) may be nontrivial and expensive in resources for fine thresholds.
- [inferred] Interplay between discretization error (Euler–Maruyama / Riemann) and quantum estimation error (AE) and how to optimally allocate classical vs quantum resources is not fully characterized.
## Open questions
- How can the oracle US (state preparation / SDE simulation) be implemented with far fewer qubits and shallower circuits while preserving the required accuracy?
- Can new encoding strategies be developed that avoid the Npaths multiplicative penalty and restore a practical quantum advantage?
- What algorithmic modifications or error-mitigation strategies would make AE routines (including RQAE) practical on NISQ hardware?
- How should the RQAE amplification ratio q be chosen in practice to optimally balance circuit depth and convergence for varied financial problems?
- How do the theoretical convergence bounds of RQAE translate into practical performance under realistic noise models on physical QPUs?
- What are the resource thresholds (qubits, depth, gate fidelity) at which the proposed quantum approaches become competitive with classical Monte Carlo in realistic financial workloads?
- How to efficiently implement payoff-loading unitaries (UV) and probability-loading unitaries (Up) for complex payoff structures and high-dimensional distributions?
- How does the approach scale to multi-asset portfolios, higher-dimensional SDEs, and path-dependent derivatives, both in qubit count and circuit depth?
- What is the best way to combine discretization (Euler–Maruyama / quadrature) choices with quantum estimation to minimize total cost for a target error?
- Can AE algorithms be adapted to directly handle signed amplitudes without large overheads, or are there alternative paradigms better suited for signed-payoff estimation?
- How effective are the proposed algorithms (encoding + RQAE) in computing risk measures like VaR/expected shortfall for realistic portfolios when executed on noisy devices?
- What practical state-preparation primitives (approximate loading, variational forms, compression) can be used to reduce preparation cost while controlling bias?

**Future work:**
- Develop and evaluate new encoding methods to remove or reduce the Npaths factor and improve practical performance.
- Design techniques to efficiently solve the SDE associated with pricing (state preparation) that reduce both the number of qubits and circuit depth required for the oracle.
- Adapt and improve AE algorithms (including RQAE) to be implementable on current QPUs, e.g., via circuit depth control, error mitigation, or hybrid schemes.
- Benchmark and validate the proposed methods on real quantum hardware (QPU) to assess noise impact and empirical resource needs.
- Investigate trade-offs between discretization error (time / domain discretization) and quantum estimation error to optimize total computational cost.
- Continue theoretical and empirical study of RQAE, including parameter selection (q), practical sample complexity, and robustness to noise.
- Explore scalable constructions for Up and UV for more complex distributions, multi-asset settings, and path-dependent payoffs.
- Focus Use Case 5 research efforts on SDE-efficient techniques to make oracle implementation feasible in near- to mid-term quantum architectures.

## Contradictions
- The report highlights a key contradiction with common claims of practical quantum superiority: although QAMC offers a theoretical 1/N advantage, the new encoding needed to handle signed payoffs introduces an Npaths factor that can eliminate the speedup in practice, contradicting simplistic claims that amplitude-estimation directly yields practical quadratic speedups for pricing/VaR.
- The paper contradicts optimistic scaling narratives by showing that resource overheads (encoding, oracle construction, large numbers of oracle calls) and current NISQ limitations prevent scaling these methods to realistic problem sizes; empirical simulation results show no clear, practical advantage over classical Monte Carlo once encoding and resource costs are included.
