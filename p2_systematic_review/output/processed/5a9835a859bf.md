## Abstract summary
This paper introduces PortFawn, an open-source Python library for building and backtesting mean-variance portfolios with support for classical (real-valued weights) and quantum annealing (binary weights) optimization. The tool provides configurable APIs, data collection, optimization, and backtesting pipelines, and the authors demonstrate its use on a multi-window backtest of nine ETFs (2015–2020) to compare minimum-volatility, maximum-return, Sharpe-maximizing and binary-optimized portfolios.
## Methodology
The authors developed PortFawn, an open-source Python library to build and backtest mean-variance portfolios using both classical solvers (real-valued weights) and a quantum-annealing approach (binary weights). The pipeline collects historical daily price data via yfinance, computes asset returns, and estimates expected returns and covariance using one of three methods (full-window mean/covariance, random sub-sampling with median aggregation, or weighted recent-return schemes). PortFawn formulates multiple portfolio objectives (minimum volatility, maximum return, maximum Sharpe ratio, multi-objective mean-variance, equal-weighted and market-cap) under long-only and full-investment (sum-to-one) constraints and optional per-asset bounds. For quantum processing the multi-objective cost is mapped into a QUBO (linear terms from returns, quadratic terms from covariance) to create a binary selection portfolio (BMOP) run via quantum annealing. Performance is evaluated with rolling overlapping backtests (train/test windows), calculating metrics such as cumulative returns, daily return distributions, annualized return/volatility and Sharpe ratio, and comparing classical portfolios (MVP, MRP, MSRP, EWP) against the binary QUBO portfolio. The illustrative experiment uses 9 iShares ETFs over 2015-01-01 to 2020-12-31 and backtests with 40-day training and 5-day testing windows.

**Algorithms used:** Mean-Variance Optimization (Markowitz), Quadratic Programming / Quadratic Objective Minimization, Multi-objective mean-variance (risk-return tradeoff with lambda), QUBO formulation, Quantum Annealing (D-Wave)
**Frameworks:** yfinance, D-Wave Ocean, PortFawn (custom Python package)

**Experimental setup:** Backtesting with overlapping rolling windows: 40 days training, 5 days testing. Classical mean-variance optimization solved on classical hardware. Quantum approach implemented by mapping the multi-objective mean-variance cost into a QUBO and submitting to a D-Wave quantum annealer via the Ocean SDK (developer account).

**Dataset:** Historical daily price data for nine iShares ETFs (IVV, IJR, ACWX, IEMG, REET, IYR, HYG, AGG, IAU) covering 2015-01-01 to 2020-12-31, retrieved via yfinance.

## Findings
- [supported] PortFawn is an open-source Python library (MIT) that implements the mean-variance portfolio optimization pipeline (data collection, expected-return and covariance estimation, optimization, backtesting, visualization) and supports both classical (real-valued weights) and quantum-annealing (binary-weight QUBO) workflows.
- [supported] PortFawn collects market data via yfinance, caches price data, computes returns, cumulative returns, correlations, covariance matrices, and provides visualization and logging; it also includes unit and integration tests.
- [supported] The tool implements three methods for estimating expected returns and risks: full (use all data), random (median of repeated random windows), and weighted (more weight to recent returns) to increase robustness of inputs to optimization.
- [supported] PortFawn supports various portfolio objectives/constraints: equal-weighted (EWP), minimum volatility portfolio (MVP), maximum return portfolio (MRP), maximum Sharpe ratio portfolio (MSRP), and a multi-objective quadratic formulation; it also supports market-cap and equal-weight baselines.
- [supported] The authors performed an empirical backtest (Jan 1, 2015 – Dec 31, 2020) on nine iShares ETFs using 40-day training and 5-day testing rolling windows and observed expected behaviors: MRP produced the highest returns, MVP the lowest volatility, and MSRP an intermediate tradeoff.
- [supported] A Binary Multi-Objective Portfolio (BMOP) was implemented via a QUBO mapping and run on a D-Wave quantum annealer (developer account); in the illustrative backtest BMOP produced reasonable performance and was described as working well compared to classical portfolios.
- [supported] The paper documents and re-emphasizes standard limitations of the classical mean-variance approach: unrealistic liquidity assumptions, omission of transaction/management costs, statistical assumptions (i.i.d. normal returns; mean and covariance suffice), ignoring dividends, and tendency to produce concentrated portfolios.
- [speculative] The authors state that quantum annealing can have a greater chance of finding global minima than classical solvers by exploiting quantum tunneling (i.e., potential advantage in escaping local minima).
- [speculative] The paper formalizes a QUBO mapping of the multi-objective mean-variance scalar objective (−λ r^T w + w^T Σ w) to a QUBO energy (identifying Qii and Qij with linear and quadratic terms) and uses normalization to enforce long-only and full-investment constraints; this mapping is presented as a practical route to run binary-weight portfolio optimization on quantum annealers.
- [speculative] The authors claim PortFawn demonstrates the 'potential' of quantum computers for portfolio optimization but do not claim or provide quantitative evidence of superior performance or speedup over classical methods.

**Results summary:** The paper introduces PortFawn, an open-source Python library implementing mean-variance portfolio construction, evaluation and backtesting with flexible input estimators and constraints. It supports classical continuous-weight mean-variance optimization and a binary-weight QUBO formulation solvable on a quantum annealer. An illustrative empirical backtest on nine ETFs (2015–2020) using rolling 40-day training / 5-day testing windows shows expected results: maximum-return portfolios have the highest returns, minimum-volatility portfolios the lowest volatility, and the multi-objective (Sharpe-like) portfolios trade off between them. A binary QUBO portfolio (BMOP) solved on a D-Wave system yielded reasonable outcomes in the example, which the authors interpret as indicating potential for quantum annealing in portfolio optimization. The paper also reiterates known limitations of mean-variance models and provides implementation details and APIs for reproducible experiments.
## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** speculative

The authors report that BMOP run on a D-Wave quantum annealer produced reasonable performance in their illustrative backtest and assert that quantum annealing may better escape local minima via tunneling; however they provide no quantitative metrics, no demonstrated speedup, nor clear superiority over classical solvers, so any advantage is presented only as potential/theoretical rather than empirically demonstrated.
## Limitations
- Asset liquidity assumption: mean-variance model assumes all assets are fully liquid and tradable at any time (author-stated).
- Costs ignored: turn-over, transaction, and management costs are not considered in the mean-variance formulation (author-stated).
- Statistical assumptions: returns are assumed i.i.d. and (implicitly) normal; portfolios are summarized only by expected returns and covariance, and expected returns are assumed to be reliable predictors of future returns (author-stated).
- Dividends excluded: calculations only consider price changes and ignore dividends (author-stated).
- Asset concentration: mean-variance optimization tends to produce highly concentrated portfolios in a few assets (author-stated).
- PortFawn is intended solely for education and research and is not an investment analysis/advice tool (author-stated/disclaimer).
- Binary-weight quantum formulation (BMOP) restricts allocations to binary selection (no fractional weights) which is a modeling limitation of QUBO-based quantum annealing approach (author-stated).
- Literature gap on practical implications: prior work often focuses on internal/abstract performance of quantum computers rather than their practical implications for portfolio optimization (author-stated observation).
- [inferred] Sensitivity to estimation error: mean-variance outputs (and PortFawn results) are sensitive to estimation errors in expected returns and covariance matrices; robustness is a concern.
- [inferred] Backtest overfitting and generalizability: backtesting on historical rolling windows may not generalize to future market regimes; risk of overfitting to historical data.
- [inferred] Normalization workaround for binary portfolios may not fully or naturally enforce investment constraints (e.g., exact budget, cardinality, min/max weights) within the QUBO formulation.
- [inferred] Scalability and hardware constraints for quantum annealing: mapping realistic-size portfolio problems to current quantum annealers may be limited by qubit counts, connectivity, embedding overhead, and noise.
- [inferred] Lack of transaction-costs, liquidity, and market-impact modelling in the quantum (QUBO) formulation used.
- [inferred] Small illustrative evaluation: the empirical demonstration used a small universe (nine ETFs) and a particular backtest setup, which limits conclusions about general applicability and performance.
## Open questions
- Can quantum annealing provide practical, economic advantages over classical optimization methods for real-world portfolio optimization problems (solution quality, speed, robustness) given current hardware?
- How should one incorporate transaction costs, turnover constraints, market impact, and liquidity considerations into QUBO/quantum formulations for portfolio optimization?
- What are effective ways to represent fractional (continuous) asset weights or fine-grained allocation granularity in quantum annealing frameworks without losing practical feasibility?
- How robust are portfolios produced by mean-variance and QUBO-based quantum approaches to estimation errors in expected returns and covariance, and how can robustness be improved?
- How does the normalization strategy used to satisfy long-only and budget constraints in binary QUBO portfolios affect feasibility, optimality, and risk characteristics in practice?
- How do embedding overhead, limited connectivity, noise, and finite qubit counts of current quantum annealers affect the end-to-end performance and scalability of quantum portfolio optimization?
- Does quantum tunnelling (in practical hardware) meaningfully increase the probability of finding global minima for financial QUBO instances compared with classical heuristics?
- How should one benchmark and compare quantum annealers against modern classical solvers (including heuristic and exact methods) on realistic financial datasets, accounting for all classical/quantum overheads?
- How can the mean-variance framework be extended (within PortFawn or similar tooling) to include dividends, multi-period rebalancing, non-Gaussian return distributions, and alternative risk measures?
- What is the effect of binary (selection) vs continuous-weight formulations on portfolio diversification, turnover, and real-world implementability?
