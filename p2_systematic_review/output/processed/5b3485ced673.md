## Abstract summary
This November 2025 industry risk assessment evaluates quantum computing, communications, and sensing and concludes the sector is HIGH RISK because current valuations depend on delivery of fault-tolerant quantum computing (FTQC) by 2029. It documents a large divergence between headline 'value-at-stake' forecasts (~$97B by 2035) and present operational revenues (~$1.5–3B), and identifies critical constraints — PQC standardization eliminating enterprise QKD TAM, helium and cryogenic supply bottlenecks, export controls fragmenting supply chains, and concentrated speculative investment — that materially threaten near-term commercialization and valuations.
## Methodology
The study employed a systematic constraint analysis methodology designed to identify structural barriers that conventional due diligence overlooks. Researchers enumerated and categorized binding constraints (C1–C7) across technical (FTQC timelines, QEC status), market (value‑at‑stake vs. revenue), supply chain (helium, dilution refrigerators), regulatory (export controls, PQC standardization), funding (headline vs. pure‑quantum allocation), and unit‑economics (quantum sensing) dimensions. For each constraint the team: (1) collected and triangulated primary and secondary evidence from government reports, peer‑reviewed literature, corporate disclosures, regulatory documents, and market data (orders, revenues, patent counts); (2) quantified impacts where possible (e.g., IEA helium 2–3 year delay, 2024 booked revenues, 2024 commercial order values and unit price collapse); (3) constructed feasibility windows mapping when multiple constraints must resolve simultaneously for commercial viability; and (4) ran scenario and sensitivity analyses (optimistic/base/pessimistic) including NPV sensitivity to FTQC timeline slips and capture‑rate assumptions. The methodology also defined red‑flag investment patterns, conditional acceptance criteria, and alternative investment strategies, with probabilistic outcome buckets assigned to FTQC delivery (success/delay/failure). Verification level was high‑confidence triangulation rather than primary experimentation; the output is a diagnostic risk assessment and investment decision framework rather than an empirical experimental study.

## Findings
- [supported] NIST finalized Post-Quantum Cryptography standards (FIPS 203/204/205) in August 2024 and major agencies (NSA, UK NCSC) endorse PQC for enterprise deployment.
- [supported] Quantum Key Distribution (QKD) enterprise Total Addressable Market is effectively eliminated for general commercial use; QKD persists primarily as sovereign/defense infrastructure.
- [supported] IBM publicly published a roadmap (Quantum Starling) targeting 2029 for a system capable of ~100 million gates on ~200 logical qubits; industry plans for utility-scale FTQC are anchored to similar late-2020s milestones.
- [supported] Current commercial/operational revenue in the sector is small relative to long-term forecasts: 2024 aggregate sector revenue estimated at ~$1.5B–$3.1B (QC $1.16–2.70B, QS $375M, QComm negligible).
- [supported] Individual commercial quantum vendors have reported very low near-term revenues (example cited: $62,000 in Q4 2024 for one vendor).
- [supported] International Energy Agency (IEA) warned that persistent helium shortages could delay quantum computing adoption by 2–3 years.
- [supported] Cryogenic infrastructure (dilution refrigerator) supply chain exhibits prolonged lead times in some markets (Japan example); domestic manufacturing initiatives underway (e.g., ULVAC+IBM collaboration).
- [supported] US Bureau of Industry and Security implemented export controls on certain quantum computing items in September 2024; similar EU dual-use list updates expected and planned, institutionalizing geopolitical fragmentation of supply chains.
- [supported] Sovereign QKD infrastructure programs exist and are being funded (China space-ground network demonstrations, EuroQCI funding allocations ~€90M for 2024–2025); sovereign spending sustains QKD niche.
- [supported] Quantum sensing is the only pillar with material current commercial revenue (~$375M in 2024) but unit economics (e.g., gravimeters ~ $500k per unit) limit mass-market adoption without 10× cost reduction.
- [supported] Alternative non-cryogenic quantum modalities (trapped ions, neutral atoms) avoid helium/ultra-cryogenic dependencies and therefore have structural operational advantages versus superconducting qubits.
- [speculative] The headline $97B aggregate market by 2035 is a 'value-at-stake' estimate (economic gains to end users if FTQC arrives) rather than a booked vendor revenue forecast; capture rates of that value by vendors are estimated (e.g., 10–15%) but uncertain.
- [speculative] Current high valuations and mega-deals (>$500M rounds, SPACs) are buying optionality on delivery of FTQC around 2029 and thus are speculative and exposed to a binary milestone risk.
- [speculative] If FTQC delivery slips 2–3 years due to supply chain/regulatory/technical issues, net present value of projected outcomes could collapse by 20–50% and cause a valuation correction analogous to late-1990s fibre-optic overinvestment.
- [speculative] The sector faces an industry-wide 'pilot program trap' in which customers pay for future optionality, skills development, and R&D access rather than immediate production workloads.
- [speculative] Quantum communications' commercial TAM will not recover for enterprise even over the medium term because PQC provides a low-cost, scalable alternative; QKD will remain primarily sovereign-funded.
- [speculative] Quantum sensing mass-market penetration requires manufacturing scale and engineering maturation by ~2028–2032 to drive unit cost down by ~10×; until then, sensing will remain defense/industrial niche.
- [speculative] Probability-weighted scenarios provided (e.g., 30–40% success by 2030–31; 40–50% delays to 2032–34; 10–20% failure) are projection-based risk estimates rather than empirical frequencies.
- [speculative] Export controls and geopolitical hardening will produce a bifurcated global quantum ecosystem (Western vs China-led stacks) with limited interoperability and increased compliance/TCO for cross-border activity.
- [supported] Public funding headline figures (e.g., Japan ¥1.05T, China 1T yuan) are often co-mingled with other priorities (semiconductor/AI) and the pure-quantum allocation can be substantially smaller than headlines imply.

**Results summary:** The report concludes that the quantum technology sector (computing, communications, sensing) is high risk for investors because current operational revenue (low billions in 2024) is far below headline long-term projections (e.g., aggregate $97B by 2035). Many empirical constraints are cited and verified (NIST PQC standardization, IEA helium shortage warning, US export controls, IBM public roadmap and sovereign QKD programs). The central argument is that commercial utility for quantum computing is binary on timely delivery of fault-tolerant quantum computing (FTQC) circa 2029; absent that milestone, vendor revenue capture of projected economic 'value-at-stake' is speculative and valuations are vulnerable. Quantum communications' enterprise market has been undercut by PQC standards, leaving QKD as a sovereign/defense niche. Quantum sensing shows nearer-term commercial traction but requires large cost reductions for mass markets. The report recommends avoiding most direct QC plays unless investors accept long horizons and high loss probabilities, favoring instead PQC services, classical alternatives, component suppliers, and quantum-adjacent software for superior risk-adjusted returns.

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** speculative

The report notes narrow, domain-specific beyond-classical demonstrations (e.g., Google demonstration with large speedup on a constrained problem) but emphasizes that general-purpose, economically relevant quantum advantage is not yet demonstrated; achievement depends on FTQC delivery (roadmapped to 2029) and is therefore speculative for broad commercial impact.

## Open questions
- Will fault-tolerant quantum computing (FTQC) at the utility scale (e.g., 100M gates on ~200 logical qubits) be delivered by 2029, 2031, or later?
- What is the credible, empirically-supported capture rate of value-at-stake that quantum hardware/software/service vendors can realize (i.e., what fraction of end-user economic gains becomes vendor revenue)?
- Can helium production, recovery, or substitution scale fast enough to avoid the 2–3 year delay projected by IEA for superconducting systems?
- To what extent will dilution refrigerator (DR) manufacturing lead times normalize, and what are realistic timelines for domestic localization programs to deliver capacity?
- Will Western export controls harmonize across allies in a way that preserves coordinated R&D/supply chains, or will they permanently fragment the global quantum ecosystem?
- Can Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC) deployment fully preclude enterprise QKD adoption, or are hybrid enterprise use-cases for QKD feasible despite PQC standardization?
- Which quantum modality (superconducting, trapped ions, neutral atoms, photonic, topological, etc.) will achieve practical scale with the best economics and supply-chain resilience?
- What are the detailed, publishable error budgets and physical-qubit-to-logical-qubit resource requirements required to run economically relevant circuits (100M gates) for target applications?
- How sensitive are valuations and NPV projections to 1–4 year slips in FTQC timelines under different discount rates and capture-rate assumptions?
- What is the realistic pathway (engineering, manufacturing, and market adoption) for quantum sensing to reduce unit costs from ~$500K to ~$50K at scale, and what volumes are required?
- How will classical computing breakthroughs (neuromorphic, analog, specialized accelerators, quantum-inspired algorithms) change the addressable market and timeline for quantum advantage?
- What is the probability distribution over the success/delay/failure scenarios presented (i.e., can the qualitative probabilities be supported with quantitative models)?
- How will standards and interoperability efforts (ETSI, IEEE, others) evolve and affect multi-vendor ecosystems and vendor capture rates?
- What are the realistic impacts on talent mobility and R&D productivity arising from 'deemed export' and other export-control-related hiring restrictions?
- How will government funding allocations evolve in practice (pure quantum vs co-mingled programs) across major players (China, US, Japan, EU) in subsequent budget cycles?

- #limitation:no-empirical-validation — This is a triangulated industry risk assessment rather than an experimental study; many projections are scenario/probability-based rather than empirically validated on hardware.

## Contradictions
- The report challenges optimistic market-size and valuation forecasts (e.g., the $97B 'value-at-stake' by 2035), arguing they conflate end-user economic value with vendor revenues and are highly sensitive to FTQC timing and capture-rate assumptions.
- It contradicts claims that QKD will become a broad commercial solution by showing that PQC standardization (NIST FIPS and agency endorsements) effectively eliminates enterprise QKD TAM, leaving QKD as primarily sovereign/defence infrastructure.
- The report disputes aggressive FTQC timeline claims by highlighting supply-chain (helium, cryogenics), regulatory (export controls), and technical (QEC) constraints that could produce multi-year delays and lower realized capture of projected value.
