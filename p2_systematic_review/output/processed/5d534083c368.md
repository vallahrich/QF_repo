## Abstract summary
The paper proposes a framework called non-native hybrid algorithms (NNHA) that integrates quantum variational samplers with classical post-processing to solve combinatorial optimization problems whose solution spaces or constraints are not natively represented by quantum bitstrings. It categorizes classical co-processing into types (post-processing, expectation-based hints, distribution reservoirs, and recursive refinement) and demonstrates prototype NNHA implementations on MaxCut/Max k-Cut and maximum independent set problems using a neutral-atom quantum device, reporting cases of improved performance over a classical-only limit (termed a comparative advantage).
## Methodology
The paper proposes and evaluates a framework called non-native hybrid algorithms (NNHA) in which a quantum variational sampler (hardware-native or emulated) generates bit-string samples P(z|α) and classical co-processing maps ensembles of these samples to valid problem solutions via a function F({z}). Classical variational optimization (over parameters α and some classical weights) is used to maximize the expected objective value ⟨C(F({z}))⟩. The framework distinguishes four classical-co-processing types: (1) post-processing individual samples (warm-start + greedy/local heuristics); (2) using expectation values and connected correlations as a ‘quantum hint’ (spectral methods); (3) using the full sample distribution as a reservoir for probabilistic updates (cluster/parallel-tempering style); (4) iterative recursive fixing (not implemented here). Implementations in the paper include: (a) a type-1 QAOA + greedy flip on ensembles of random 3-regular graphs (emulated state-vector QAOA, fixed angles for ν-regular graphs, greedy 1-local flip post-processing); (b) a type-2 spectral-clustering approach to Max k-Cut using eigenvectors of a weighted sum of connected correlation matrices produced from three Rydberg-quench ansatzes, followed by k-means clustering and greedy local post-processing; (c) type-1/type-3 approaches to Maximum Independent Set (MIS) using Rydberg adiabatic/annealing state preparation on unit-disk graphs with greedy add/remove post-processing and a cluster-update simulated-annealing that uses quantum samples as a high-temperature reservoir. Classical variational optimization used gradient-free optimizers (COBYLA) and Bayesian search for some parameters; bounded stochastic searches were used for quantum parameter exploration. Typical measurement budgets were small (order 100 shots per evaluation, ~30 optimization iterations, ~3000 total shots reported for variational runs). Comparative baselines were explicit classical-only limits (no-quantum variants) and classical heuristics to evaluate whether adding quantum sampling improved results (termed comparative advantage).

**Algorithms used:** QAOA, Adiabatic quantum annealing (Rydberg annealing / quenches), Rydberg quench dynamics (ansatz), Spectral clustering, k-means clustering, Greedy flip heuristic (1-local greedy), Greedy add/remove heuristic for MIS, Cluster-update simulated annealing (Metropolis-Hastings), Parallel tempering style reservoir sampling (conceptual), COBYLA (classical optimizer), Bayesian optimization (for classical weights), Bounded stochastic search
**Frameworks:** QuEra Aquila (neutral-atom platform), Amazon Braket (cloud access), State-vector emulation (simulator; in-house / standard sv simulator not named explicitly)

**Experimental setup:** Hybrid experiments use either state-vector emulation (QAOA MaxCut experiments) or the QuEra Aquila 256-qubit neutral-atom quantum processor accessed via Amazon Braket (Max k-Cut spectral-clustering and MIS experiments). Typical measurement budgets per estimate: ~100 shots; optimization iterations reported ~30; total shots per variational run ~3000. QAOA experiments used fixed angles for ν-regular graphs; Rydberg experiments used piecewise-linear adiabatic or short quenches with tunable time and detuning.

## Findings
- [speculative] The paper proposes the "non-native hybrid algorithms" (NNHA) framework in which a quantum variational sampler produces measurement bit strings that are treated as a resource for classical routines (post-processing, spectral features, or as a sample reservoir) rather than requiring bit strings to directly encode final solutions.
- [supported] In practice, using measurement outcomes as inputs to classical post-processing or clustering can improve solution quality versus the corresponding classical-only baseline in selected instances (they demonstrate this empirically for Max k-Cut spectral clustering and for a cluster-update MIS routine).
- [supported] A type-1 NNHA (QAOA + greedy 1-local post-processing) increases per-shot objective values relative to raw QAOA samples and in simulation begins to outperform the p=0 classical limit around QAOA depth p ≈ 4 on their 3-regular 16-vertex graph ensemble.
- [supported] A type-2 NNHA (using connected correlation matrices from short quenches on neutral-atom hardware as features for spectral clustering) was implemented on QuEra's Aquila device for Max 3-Cut on geometric/unit-disk graphs and outperformed the corresponding classical-only limit in their experiments.
- [supported] A type-1 post-processing of Rydberg-annealing measurement outcomes (the Ebadi22 style greedy repair → greedy add pipeline) can be outperformed by a classical-only baseline on some instances; they report an instance where the classical-only heuristic gave a larger average MIS than the quantum-assisted pipeline.
- [supported] A type-3 NNHA that uses quantum samples as a high-temperature reservoir for cluster-update simulated annealing (cluster updates chosen by a sandpile model) showed slightly faster convergence / marginally better average MIS size than the classical-only variant in their experiment (performance ratio ≈ 1.005).
- [supported] Including quantum resources does not guarantee improved outcomes; hybrid/quantum resources can either help, be neutral, or hinder optimization performance depending on ansatz parameters and instance details (empirical observation in their MIS examples).
- [supported] On neutral-atom hardware they used ≈100 measurement shots per objective estimate and report good convergence in their variational examples within ≈30 optimizer iterations (≈3000 total shots); they also note that few-shot estimates (they mention ~10) can sometimes be sufficient empirically on this hardware class.
- [supported] They introduce a pragmatic comparative metric (compare hybrid to an explicit no-quantum/classical limit of the same algorithmic pipeline) to claim a "comparative advantage" when quantum resources improve performance relative to that classical limit.
- [supported] Theoretical/complexity observations cited: sampling from certain QAOA distributions can be #P-hard (cited prior literature), and classical local heuristics can outperform low-depth QAOA on some problems (cited literature and their simulations).
- [speculative] The authors suggest extensions (e.g., recursive/fix-and-reduce approaches, stitching subgraph quantum solves into multilevel schemes) enabling NNHA to tackle much larger problems than fit on the quantum device, but these are presented as proposals rather than demonstrated here.

**Results summary:** The paper introduces non-native hybrid algorithms (NNHA), a framework where quantum variational samplers produce measurement outcomes that are fed into classical routines (post-processing, spectral clustering from connected correlations, or as a sample reservoir) to produce problem solutions even when problem structure is non-native to the hardware. The authors implement prototype NNHA instances: (i) QAOA + greedy post-processing for MaxCut (simulation), (ii) spectral clustering using connected correlations from short neutral-atom quenches for Max k-Cut (hardware on QuEra Aquila), and (iii) MIS solved via Rydberg-annealing samples plus cluster-update simulated annealing (hardware). They report empirical "comparative advantage" in select cases (Max k-Cut spectral clustering; cluster-update MIS shows marginal improvement), but also document instances where quantum-assisted pipelines performed worse than classical-only baselines. They emphasize that NNHA can be a practical co-processor model for optimization but does not automatically imply a general quantum advantage.

**Performance claims:**
- MaxCut (simulation, ensemble of 256 3-regular 16-vertex graphs): hybrid (QAOA + greedy) per-shot objective distribution strictly ≥ raw QAOA; hybrid begins to exceed the p=0 classical-only limit around QAOA depth p ≈ 4 (no single numeric average reported in text).
- Max k-Cut (hardware, Aquila, unit-disk/King-subgraph instances, k=3): the NNHA spectral-clustering pipeline "outperforms the classical-only limit" in their experiments (no precise numeric average reported in text).
- MIS greedy post-processing (hardware example): classical-only algorithm average MIS size = 45.178; hybrid (quantum-assisted) average MIS size = 44.282; hybrid/classical performance ratio = 0.9801; classical finds MIS size 47 with probability 1.80% while the hybrid did not find that size in those runs.
- MIS cluster-update (hardware example with variational parameters): hybrid average MIS size = 45.72; classical-only average = 45.48; hybrid/classical performance ratio ≈ 1.005; hybrid samples an equal-or-better solution with probability ≈ 83%.
- Shot/optimization resource claim (hardware): used M = 100 shots per cost estimate, observed good convergence within ~30 iterations and ≈3000 total shots across experiments; they state that on neutral-atom hardware few-shot estimates (order 10) can sometimes be sufficient empirically.
## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** not-applicable

The authors do not claim a general or provable quantum advantage. They introduce and empirically demonstrate "comparative advantage" (quantum resources improving a specific hybrid pipeline relative to its classical/no-quantum limit) in some examples, but they also show counterexamples where quantum resources reduce performance. Thus no broad quantum advantage is demonstrated or claimed.

## Open questions

- Can NNHA approaches be generalized beyond the specific problem-instance constraints used in the demonstrations (e.g., beyond 2D unit-disk graphs for Rydberg encodings)?
- How should one systematically design or choose the classical co-processing function F for arbitrary non-native problems to maximize hybrid benefit?
- What are rigorous metrics and benchmarks for ‘comparative advantage’ that avoid dependence on arbitrary classical baselines?
- How robust are NNHA methods to realistic hardware noise, limited coherence, SPAM errors, and finite-shot sampling in larger, noisier devices?
- Can quantum resources in NNHA provide provable (rather than heuristic) advantages for natural optimization problems, and if so, what are the theoretical guarantees?
- How effectively can samples from non-thermal or hard-to-characterize quantum distributions be used as reservoirs for classical sampling techniques like parallel tempering or cluster updates?
- What are the optimal variational optimization strategies and shot allocation methods for NNHA across different hardware platforms (analog vs. digital)?

- Which ansatz families (hardware-native vs. problem-structured) are most promising as hybrid samplers for different classes of non-native combinatorial problems?
- What is the interplay between the depth/complexity of quantum evolution and the efficacy of the classical post-processing steps (e.g., do deeper circuits always improve net hybrid performance)?
