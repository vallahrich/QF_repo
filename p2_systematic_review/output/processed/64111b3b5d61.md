## Abstract summary
Survey chapter introducing quantum computing fundamentals, contrasting adiabatic (quantum annealing) and universal architectures, and explaining key challenges such as qubit coherence and error correction. It summarizes the principal quantum algorithms (Shor, Grover), current hardware efforts (D-Wave, Google Bristlecone, IBM, Intel), and near- to mid-term applications for finance (Monte Carlo derivative pricing, portfolio optimization, high-frequency trading, and trade/clearing use cases), emphasizing that practical quantum advantage is plausible but still years away due to engineering and error‑resilience constraints. This industry report (appendices) profiles AI-related investments, products, capabilities, and strategic initiatives across major technology companies and investors, including SoftBank Vision Fund, Amazon, Google, Apple, Facebook, and Microsoft. It summarizes holdings, AI services (cloud, cognitive services, vision, speech, and NLP), applications in finance, advertising, IoT, autonomous vehicles, robotics, and corporate M&A activity, and highlights market positions and future development potentials as of April 2019.

## Limitations
- Qubit coherence / error rates remain the principal technical hurdle for useful quantum computation (physical qubits are noisy; logical qubits require many physical qubits).
- Adiabatic / annealing machines (e.g., D‑Wave) currently lack general-purpose error‑correction and their practical quantum advantage over classical approaches is disputed.
- Universal quantum computers (UQCs) that can run canonical quantum algorithms still require much lower error rates and larger scale (many more qubits) than available hardware.
- Large‑scale, error‑corrected logical qubits demand very large overhead (estimates of thousands of physical qubits per logical qubit) making near‑term scalability uncertain.
- A substantial gap exists between quantum algorithms (theory) and realizable hardware; many algorithms assume ideal, noise‑free machines and therefore are currently impractical.
- No settled standard benchmark for quantum advantage in applied finance; metrics and comparisons against optimized classical methods are complex.
- Programming and software stacks for quantum hardware are immature; languages, toolchains and best practices are fragmented and evolving.
- Heterogeneity of qubit technologies (ions, superconductors, spins, photons, topological) creates uncertainty about which approach will scale and be cost‑effective.
- Quantum annealers (AQC variants) are specialized (optimization) and may not cover the range of finance algorithms needed without further theoretical development.
