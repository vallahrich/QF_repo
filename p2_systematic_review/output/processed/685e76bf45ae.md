## Abstract summary
This preprint studies standard QAOA and ADAPT-QAOA for solving QUBO-formulated feature-selection problems via simulation, using approximation ratio and time-to-solution as metrics. It finds ADAPT-QAOA notably outperforms standard QAOA on harder instances (higher α) while standard QAOA is adequate for simpler cases, and provides hardware scaling estimates showing superconducting devices offer shorter time-to-solution but trapped-ion devices yield lower error rates; topology, gate durations, and error mitigation challenges are discussed.
## Methodology
The authors evaluate the performance of the standard Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) and the adaptive ADAPT-QAOA variant on randomly generated QUBO instances formulated as feature-selection problems. QUBO matrices were mapped to Ising cost Hamiltonians via x_i -> (1 - Z_i)/2. Simulations were executed on the ideal Qiskit simulator with 10^4 shots per optimization iteration; circuit parameters were optimized iteratively layer-by-layer using the Powell optimizer (SciPy) with up to 1500 iterations per layer. Experiments used multiple problem sizes (primarily 6, 10 and 14 features; scaling experiments up to tens of features for hardware time estimates), two trade-off hardness parameters (alpha = 0.2 and 0.6), and 10 random-instance seeds per size. ADAPT-QAOA used an O(n^2)-sized mixer pool (global X/Y mixers, single-qubit X/Y, and two-qubit BC operators with B,C in {X,Y,Z}) and selected the mixer per layer by a maximum-gradient criterion (gradient evaluated with a small gamma0 = 0.01). Solution quality was measured by approximation ratio r_k = C_k / C_exact where C_exact was obtained via the classical Gurobi OptiMods QUBO solver. Time-to-solution and estimated total error probabilities were also reported. Additionally, the authors performed device-level resource and error estimations for ibm_brisbane (superconducting heavy-hex) and Quantinuum H2 (trapped-ion all-to-all) using reported calibration data (gate durations and error rates) and simple models for layer execution time and total error probability to compare topologies (heavy-hex, square lattice, all-to-all). Error mitigation methods were not applied in the presented hardware error estimates.

**Algorithms used:** QAOA, ADAPT-QAOA, Gurobi OptiMods (classical QUBO solver), Powell optimizer (SciPy)
**Frameworks:** Qiskit (ideal simulator), SciPy (optimizer), Gurobi

**Experimental setup:** Ideal Qiskit simulator with 10,000 shots per optimization iteration; iterative layer-wise circuit construction up to 30 layers; Powell optimizer with max 1500 iterations per layer. Hardware-level estimates used calibration data from IBM Quantum (ibm_brisbane) and Quantinuum (H2) including single- and two-qubit gate durations and error rates; topologies considered: heavy-hex (ibm_brisbane), square-lattice (theoretical), and all-to-all (theoretical and Quantinuum H2).

## Findings
- [supported] ADAPT-QAOA outperforms standard QAOA in approximation ratio on harder feature-selection QUBO instances (trade-off parameter alpha = 0.6) in the authors' simulator experiments.
- [supported] For easier feature-selection problems (alpha = 0.2) the approximation ratios of standard QAOA and ADAPT-QAOA are similar in the authors' simulations.
- [supported] ADAPT-QAOA often requires longer wall-clock time than standard QAOA for larger problem sizes because the adaptive mixer-selection step (mixer pool ~ O(n^2)) adds computational overhead.
- [supported] In the reported experiments (n = 14, alpha = 0.6) 15 layers of ADAPT-QAOA outperformed 30 layers of standard QAOA in both approximation ratio and time-to-solution (simulator results).
- [supported] A classical commercial solver (Gurobi OptiMods) demonstrated better scaling in time-to-solution than the studied QAOA variants on the formulated feature-selection problems (authors' experiments).
- [supported] Device qubit topology materially affects estimated performance: higher connectivity (square lattice, all-to-all) reduces SWAP overhead and improves estimated time-to-solution and error compared to heavy-hex.
- [supported] Based on calibration data used by the authors, superconducting hardware (ibm_brisbane) shows much shorter gate durations and therefore lower estimated time-to-solution than the trapped-ion Quantinuum H2, while Quantinuum H2 shows substantially lower gate and measurement error rates.
- [supported] The authors used and report specific device calibration values: ibm_brisbane single-qubit gate duration 0.06 µs, two-qubit 0.66 µs, single-qubit error 3.3e-4, two-qubit error 1.2e-2, measurement error 3.7e-2; Quantinuum H2 single-qubit 63 µs, two-qubit 308 µs, single-qubit error 3.0e-5, two-qubit error 1.0e-3, measurement error 1.0e-3.
- [supported] Estimated total error probabilities (via the authors' model) for executing small QAOA circuits are lower on the Quantinuum H2 (given its lower gate/measurement error rates) than on the ibm_brisbane heavy-hex topology.
- [supported] Z gates can be treated as effectively zero-time and zero-error via software frame changes in the hardware models used (cited implementation detail).
- [speculative] ADAPT-QAOA's adaptive mixer selection is believed to reduce excitations to higher-excited states, improving accuracy and speed in general — stated as a motivating principle but not proven universally in this paper.
- [speculative] QAOA-based methods might become practically useful for financial feature-selection if hardware (connectivity, gate/measurement error) improves and if problem formulations are chosen that are challenging for classical solvers.

## Limitations
- Experiments use small problem sizes (6, 10, 14 features), limiting conclusions about scalability to larger, practically relevant QUBOs.
- Performance evaluation of algorithms (QAOA and ADAPT-QAOA) was performed on an ideal simulator (noise-free) for most comparisons, so results may not reflect behavior on noisy real hardware.
- Hardware resource and time-to-solution estimates rely on snapshots of device calibration data and simplified models (e.g., excluding state-preparation and measurement overheads), reducing realism of device-level projections.
- Error estimations were performed only for single-layer QAOA and did not include effects of multi-layer noise accumulation or of error mitigation techniques, so total error projections are incomplete.
- The study compares algorithms against a classical solver (Gurobi) on the same problem formulation and finds classical scaling superior, limiting claims of quantum advantage for the considered instances.
- Randomly generated QUBO matrices were used rather than real-world financial datasets, so representativeness for enterprise feature-selection use cases is limited.
- ADAPT-QAOA's mixer pool grows as O(n^2) and the mixer-selection step increases time-to-solution substantially for larger n, creating a practical overhead not fully mitigated here.
- Only two hardware platforms/families were considered (IBM superconducting and Quantinuum trapped-ion), and some ibm topologies considered were hypothetical (square lattice, all-to-all), limiting generality across hardware ecosystems. [inferred]
- Optimizations used a single classical optimizer (Powell) with fixed shots and iteration caps (104 shots, max 1500 iterations per layer); sensitivity to optimizer choice, shot count, and optimization budgets was not explored. [inferred]

## Open questions
- Which classes of QUBO/feature-selection instances (structure, size, noise sensitivity) can QAOA or ADAPT-QAOA realistically outperform classical solvers on in near-term devices?
- How can problem formulations be modified (e.g., penalty terms, embeddings) to make instances harder for classical solvers but still amenable to quantum heuristics?
- What is the practical trade-off between ADAPT-QAOA's improved approximation ratios and its additional classical overhead (mixer-selection cost) at scale?
- How do error mitigation techniques (zero-noise extrapolation, probabilistic error cancellation, etc.) change time-to-solution and effective error probability for multi-layer QAOA/ADAPT-QAOA on real devices?
- How sensitive are the algorithms' performances to choices of classical optimizers, shot budgets, and layer-increment strategies in realistic noisy settings?
- How do different hardware topologies and connectivity patterns (beyond heavy-hex, square, all-to-all) impact overall performance when including compilation, routing (SWAPs), and control overheads?
- Can trapped-ion devices' lower per-gate error but slower gates yield better end-to-end solution quality (accounting for decoherence and total execution time) than faster superconducting devices?
- What are efficient strategies to reduce ADAPT-QAOA mixer-pool complexity or to accelerate mixer selection without sacrificing solution quality?
- How does QAOA/ADAPT-QAOA performance scale as problem size increases beyond the small instances studied, particularly when classical solvers become infeasible?
- What is the impact of realistic measurement and state-preparation overheads on time-to-solution and error accumulation in full-stack deployments?

**Future work:**
- Find and formulate problem instances that are harder for classical solvers but still tractable for quantum algorithms.
- Incorporate penalty terms into QUBO objective functions (e.g., to encode constraints) and study their effect on algorithm performance.
- Explore more efficient quantum algorithms and alternative QAOA variants, and study their susceptibility to various gate and qubit errors.
- Investigate hardware improvements: increase qubit connectivity and reduce single-/two-qubit gate and measurement error rates.
- Study the inclusion and impact of error mitigation techniques on algorithm fidelity and time-to-solution in realistic device scenarios.
