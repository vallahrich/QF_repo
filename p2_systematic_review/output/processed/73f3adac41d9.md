## Abstract summary
This preprint presents a QUBO formulation of portfolio optimization that incorporates expected return, budget, transaction costs, market impact, and ESG scores, using binary encoding for integer holdings. It evaluates D-Wave hybrid quantum-classical solvers on synthetic datasets, showing scalability to portfolios with hundreds of assets with low QPU time but substantial classical pre/post-processing and accuracy limitations due to current hardware.
## Methodology
The study formulates portfolio optimization as a Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO) problem by defining a composite Hamiltonian H that combines risk (pairwise correlations), expected returns, a quadratic budget penalty, transaction costs, market impact, and an ESG reward term. Integer asset holdings n_i are discretized using binary encoding n_i = sum_s 2^s x_{i,s} with N_p ≈ 14 precision bits, yielding N = N_a * N_p binary variables. Synthetic financial datasets (uniformly distributed values; ESG in [0,1]; prices ~0–500 with bias toward lower values; single-value expected returns μ_i and correlations σ_ij) were generated (pool of 2,000 assets) and saved to CSV. Initial holdings n_i,0 were randomized within a stated range. QUBO instances were constructed from the discretized Hamiltonian using parameter settings γ=1, ρ=6, δ=2, ε=2, ω=6 and total-investment K=10,000. The resulting binary quadratic models were solved using D-Wave’s hybrid quantum-classical solvers (BQM and CQM) accessed via the D-Wave Leap platform; small instances were compared against D-Wave’s exact solver. Experiments explored single-period and multi-period (10-period) formulations, varied N_a from small sizes up to 630 (logical variables example: 630 assets × 14 bits = 8,820 binary variables), and measured wall-clock run time, QPU access time, run-time variability, solution energies, and portfolio returns. Timing experiments were repeated three times for run-time statistics; energy/solution-distribution experiments used 100 repeats for histograms. Results were presented as average run time, standard deviations, QPU access times, energy-frequency histograms, and comparisons to the exact solver for small instances.

**Algorithms used:** Quantum annealing (adiabatic quantum computing), QUBO formulation (binary-encoded integer programming), Hybrid quantum-classical solvers: D-Wave BQM (Binary Quadratic Model), Hybrid quantum-classical solvers: D-Wave CQM (Constrained Quadratic Model)
**Frameworks:** D-Wave Leap (cloud platform / service), D-Wave hybrid solvers (BQM, CQM), D-Wave exact solver (for small instances)

**Experimental setup:** QUBO problems built from the discretized Hamiltonian were submitted to D-Wave’s Leap platform using D-Wave hybrid solvers (BQM and CQM) and D-Wave simulators/solvers; small instances were solved with D-Wave's exact solver for baseline comparison. Experiments included single-period and 10-period (multi-period) settings, N_p ≈ 14 bits of precision per asset, K=10,000 total investment units, parameter values γ=1, ρ=6, δ=2, ε=2, ω=6. Runs were repeated (3× for timing; 100× for energy histograms). Reported measurements include average wall-clock run time, run-time standard deviation, QPU access time, solution energy distributions, and portfolio returns.

**Dataset:** Synthetic stock dataset generated uniformly (pool of 2,000 synthetic assets). For each asset: ESG score in [0,1], price in ~0–500 (biased to lower values), expected return μ_i (single-value), and expected correlations σ_ij (single-value). Generated datasets saved as CSV. Experiment instances sample random subsets of assets from the 2,000-asset pool.

- [supported] Most of the computational workload in the tested hybrid workflow was classical pre- and post-processing (data preparation, embedding/compilation, submission, postprocessing), often dominating total runtime.
- [supported] In the author’s experiments, the BQM (binary quadratic model) hybrid solver ran faster and produced solutions closer to an exact solver (on small instances) than the CQM (constrained quadratic model) solver.
- [supported] The CQM solver exhibited higher runtime variability and larger total runtimes (reported as up to ~5x slower than BQM for 600-asset portfolios in these experiments).
- [supported] Solution energies for a 100-asset instance exhibited wide variability for both hybrid solvers (energy histogram spanning roughly -420 to +980 in the reported experiment), indicating difficulty in consistently finding global optima.

- [supported] The experiments highlighted practical hardware/compilation limitations: beyond ~630 assets the QUBO formulation could not be compiled onto available hardware in these experiments.
- [speculative] The paper claims feasibility of optimizing portfolios comprising hundreds of assets with reduced computational time compared to classical methods; however, no direct classical-runtime baseline or head-to-head benchmark was presented in the study.
- [speculative] The work suggests that incorporating ESG metrics into quantum-enabled portfolio optimization is a promising direction; the paper provides a model that includes ESG but does not demonstrate financial/ESG trade-offs on real market data.

**Results summary:** The author presents a QUBO-based formulation for portfolio optimization that integrates return, risk (pairwise correlations), budget, transaction cost, market impact and ESG terms, discretized via binary encoding (Np ≈ 14). Using synthetic data, D-Wave hybrid solvers (BQM and CQM) were run on instances up to 630 assets (≈8,820 binary variables). Empirical results show that QPU time is minimal (milliseconds per run; 10.24 s total reported), while classical pre- and post-processing dominates runtime. In these experiments BQM outperformed CQM in runtime and was closer to an exact solver on small instances; CQM showed higher runtimes and variability. The study also reports wide variability in solution energies and notes compilation/hardware limits beyond ~630 assets. The paper argues potential for ESG-aware quantum portfolio optimization but does not provide direct comparisons to classical solver runtimes or tests on real market datasets.

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** speculative

The paper suggests potential advantages of quantum annealing (e.g., small QPU times and scalability to hundreds of assets). However, it does not present direct, controlled comparisons to classical solvers on the same instances (wall-clock end-to-end comparisons), and reports substantial classical overhead and solution-quality limitations. Therefore a practical quantum advantage is not demonstrated and remains speculative based on the provided evidence.
