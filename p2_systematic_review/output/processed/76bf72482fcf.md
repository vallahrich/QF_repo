## Abstract summary
This review compares classical (ensemble and deep learning) and quantum (QSVM, VQC) machine learning approaches for financial fraud detection using peer-reviewed studies and real transactional data. It finds classical ensemble and deep models remain the most scalable and robust in practice, quantum methods are currently limited by hardware, noise, and feature dimensionality, and hybrid classical–quantum architectures are the most promising near-term direction.
## Methodology
This paper is a literature-based comparative study. The authors surveyed peer-reviewed studies and real transactional datasets (rather than synthetic-only experiments) to compare classical, quantum and hybrid machine learning approaches for financial fraud detection. The methodological approach synthesises reported empirical findings from the literature: it summarizes common data characteristics (large transaction volumes, high dimensionality, extreme class imbalance), typical preprocessing steps (feature normalization, noise reduction, redundant feature removal, dimensionality reduction such as PCA), and imbalance-handling techniques (undersampling, cost-sensitive learning, SMOTE, GAN-based oversampling referenced in related work). The review then groups learning approaches into classical pipelines (logistic regression, SVM, ensemble methods such as Random Forests and Gradient Boosting / XGBoost, and deep neural networks including feedforward, convolutional and recurrent architectures) and quantum/hybrid pipelines (Quantum SVMs using quantum kernels, Variational Quantum Classifiers trained with hybrid classical-quantum optimisation loops, and hybrid designs that apply classical preprocessing/PCA prior to quantum encoding). Evaluation practices reported in the literature (cross-validation, grid or randomized hyperparameter search) and fraud-oriented metrics (recall, precision, F1-score, AUC) are summarised to enable comparative assessment. The paper emphasises constraints reported across studies (limited qubit counts, noise and decoherence on NISQ devices, use of simulators, need for dimensionality reduction for quantum models) and concludes by synthesising performance trends and practical recommendations (hybrid classical-quantum approaches as the most feasible near-term path).

**Algorithms used:** Logistic Regression, Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest, Gradient Boosting Machines (GBM), XGBoost, Deep Neural Networks (feedforward, CNN, RNN), Quantum Support Vector Machine (QSVM), Variational Quantum Classifier (VQC), Principal Component Analysis (PCA), SMOTE (synthetic oversampling), Cost-sensitive learning, Cost-sensitive decision trees, Generative Adversarial Networks (for oversampling) (referenced)

**Dataset:** Anonymized credit card transactional datasets drawn from real financial systems (high volume, high dimensionality, extreme class imbalance with fraud typically <<1% of transactions). The Kaggle credit card fraud dataset is specifically cited as an example used in related studies.

## Findings
- [supported] Classical ensemble methods (Random Forests, Gradient Boosting/XGBoost) consistently show high predictive performance (high AUC and recall) and are scalable and robust for large-scale fraud detection.
- [supported] Deep learning models (feedforward, CNN, RNN) can reach comparable recall and F1-scores to ensemble methods but typically require more computational resources and reduce model interpretability.
- [supported] Handling class imbalance (undersampling, cost-sensitive learning, SMOTE) is essential in fraud detection and improves minority-class detection (recall).
- [supported] Quantum machine learning approaches discussed include Quantum Support Vector Machines (QSVMs) with quantum kernels and Variational Quantum Classifiers (VQCs); small-scale studies and simulations report competitive results on reduced-dimension datasets.
- [supported] QSVM performance is sensitive to input feature dimensionality; applying dimensionality reduction (e.g., PCA) before quantum encoding can improve QSVM results.
- [supported] Current quantum hardware limitations (noise, decoherence, limited qubit counts) and frequent reliance on simulators/NISQ devices restrict the scalability and practical deployment of QML for real-world financial fraud detection.
- [supported] Hybrid classical-quantum architectures (classical preprocessing/feature selection + quantum encoding/classifier) are identified as the most practical near-term integration path for QML in fraud detection pipelines.
- [supported] Fraud-oriented evaluation metrics (recall, F1-score, AUC) are emphasized as more appropriate than raw accuracy in highly imbalanced transaction datasets.
- [supported] Preprocessing requirements for quantum pipelines (strong dimensionality reduction and feature selection) are more stringent than for classical pipelines due to qubit/resource constraints.
- [speculative] Quantum machine learning may provide richer feature representations and potential learning advantages (via superposition/entanglement) that could translate into improved separability for some datasets, but practical advantage in deployed financial fraud systems is not demonstrated.
- [speculative] Short-term practical benefits of quantum techniques for fraud detection will likely come from hybrid systems rather than fully quantum solutions.

**Results summary:** The review concludes that classical machine learning—particularly ensemble methods and, to a similar extent, deep learning—remains the most mature, scalable and empirically validated approach for large-scale financial fraud detection. Quantum machine learning techniques (QSVMs, VQCs) show theoretical promise and have produced competitive results in low-dimensional or purpose-designed datasets (often in simulation or on small quantum hardware), but are currently constrained by hardware noise, limited qubits, and scalability issues. Hybrid classical-quantum architectures (classical preprocessing and feature reduction followed by quantum encoding/classification) are presented as the most practical near-term strategy to explore quantum benefits while retaining system robustness.

## Contradictions
- The paper reports that classical ensemble methods (Random Forest, XGBoost) consistently achieve higher and more robust predictive performance on large-scale fraud detection than QML in practice, contradicting optimistic claims in some QML literature of near-term superiority.
- The review highlights that scalability claims for quantum approaches are contradicted by current hardware limitations (qubit counts, noise) and the frequent reliance on simulators, undermining assertions that QML methods can scale to real transactional workloads in the near term.
