## Abstract summary
The paper integrates quantum probability models into social interaction experiments to examine how superposition, measurement-induced collapse, and interference influence individual and group decision-making. It finds that quantum approaches can better capture cognitive uncertainty, preference reversals, and context-dependent order effects than classical models, and discusses potential applications for survey design and policy simulation while noting practical hardware, interdisciplinary, and ethical challenges.
## Methodology
The paper is theoretical and methodological: it proposes representing individual cognitive states as state vectors in a complex Hilbert space (using Dirac bra-ket notation), modeling survey questions and decision probes as measurement (projection) operators that induce state collapse, and using quantum probability (probability amplitudes, interference terms and non‑commutativity) to describe order effects, preference reversals and contextual dependencies. Experimental designs are sketched rather than executed: (a) controlled within-subject survey experiments that vary measurement order to test non-commutativity and interference; (b) weak‑measurement inspired questionnaires to capture intermediate (partially collapsed) opinion states rather than forcing binary responses; (c) multiple-round sequential choice tasks to observe collapse dynamics and superposition effects; and (d) multi-agent quantum-game setups that introduce entanglement-like correlations among participants to study group decision interference. Data analysis is proposed as a model-comparison exercise in which quantum probability models (with interference terms and projection operators) are fitted to empirical response distributions and compared against classical probability/Bayesian and Markov alternatives to evaluate fit and explanatory power. The paper also outlines potential use of quantum algorithms and quantum‑enhanced simulation (QAOA, VQE, QFT, Grover, QML and QRL) or hybrid quantum-classical pipelines to scale simulations and optimization for policy-simulation tasks, while noting practical limits of current hardware and recommending quantum simulators and hybrid approaches for development and validation.

**Algorithms used:** QAOA, VQE, QFT, Grover, QML, QRL

## Findings

- [supported] Question/measurement order effects in surveys are empirically robust and can be modeled by non-commutative (quantum) measurement theory.
- [speculative] Human cognitive states can be meaningfully represented as quantum-like superpositions that collapse upon measurement, offering a framework for modeling indecisiveness and dynamically formed preferences.
- [supported] Interference terms in quantum probability provide a principled mechanism to explain enhancement or suppression of choice probabilities (constructive/destructive interference) observed in some decision tasks.
- [speculative] Weak measurement concepts can be applied to survey design to capture intermediate opinion states without forcing full collapse to binary answers.
- [speculative] Quantum game-theoretic constructs (superposition, entanglement) can produce strategic outcomes different from classical game theory and may enable cooperative strategies not achievable classically.
- [speculative] Quantum algorithms (QAOA, VQE, QFT, Grover) and quantum machine learning methods could potentially improve large-scale social simulation, optimization of policy interventions, and pattern extraction from high-dimensional social data.
- [supported] Current quantum hardware limitations (decoherence, noise, limited qubit counts/stability) constrain the scalability and practical deployment of quantum social experiments and quantum-enhanced social data analysis.

- [speculative] Quantum-inspired models could reveal non-classical correlations (entanglement-like effects) in group decision-making that classical aggregation fails to capture.
- [disputed] Strong claims that human mental states can violate Bell inequalities or exhibit literal quantum entanglement analogous to physical systems are controversial and not broadly accepted.
- [speculative] Ethical concerns and governance risks arise from quantum-driven social simulations and measurement manipulations (e.g., influence on cognition, potential misuse by governments/corporations), requiring new oversight and legal frameworks.
- [supported] There is a substantive interdisciplinary gap: widespread application requires cross-training and collaboration between quantum physicists, computer scientists, and social scientists.

**Results summary:** This theoretical paper argues that quantum probability and measurement concepts offer a promising alternative framework for modeling cognitive uncertainty, order effects, interference, and context dependence in social interaction experiments. It synthesizes prior empirical findings (notably on order effects and some decision anomalies) showing improved fit of quantum models, proposes experimental designs to test superposition, interference and measurement-order effects, and outlines potential applications of quantum algorithms and Quantum AI for large-scale social simulation and policy optimization. At the same time, the paper acknowledges hardware limitations, the need for extensive empirical validation, interdisciplinary barriers, and ethical/governance risks associated with quantum-enabled social research.

## Open questions
- Do human cognition and social decision-making genuinely follow quantum-like principles, or are quantum models chiefly convenient mathematical analogies?
- How can quantum effects (superposition, interference, entanglement) be empirically validated and measured robustly in real-world social and field settings?
- What experimental designs and statistical tests can reliably distinguish quantum-probability predictions from classical-contextual or bounded-rationality explanations?
- How can quantum social experiments be scaled to large, heterogeneous populations and complex social systems?
- To what extent can current and near-term quantum hardware support the computational needs of quantum social-science analyses?
- What is the role and operational interpretation of entanglement/non-local correlations in group decision-making and multi-agent social interactions?
- How should researchers design ethically responsible measurement procedures given measurement-induced changes in participants’ cognitive states?
- What legal, regulatory, and governance frameworks are needed to prevent misuse of quantum social-simulation tools by public or private actors?
- How can quantum computing be integrated with AI (QML, QRL) in a way that is interpretable, fair, and robust for social applications?
- What post-quantum cryptographic and data-governance mechanisms are required to protect sensitive behavioral and social data processed with quantum resources?
