## Abstract summary
This preprint introduces Arline Benchmarks, an open-source platform for automated cross-benchmarking and profiling of quantum compilers with a focus on NISQ applications. The tool evaluates compilers (Qiskit, Cirq, Pytket, PyZX) across metrics such as gate counts, depth, hardware-weighted cost, and run time, and supports composing compiler-specific optimization subroutines into composite pipelines to achieve improved circuit compression across various random and structured circuits and hardware architectures.
## Methodology
The authors developed Arline Benchmarks, an open-source Python platform (with an Arline Quantum backbone) to perform automated, cross-compiler benchmarking of quantum compilation and optimization frameworks targeted at NISQ-era applications. Experiments were configured through jsonnet files that specify target circuits (OpenQASM files or randomly generated circuits), hardware models (native gate sets, qubit count and connectivity) and compilation pipelines (ordered sequences of vendor-provided subroutines wrapped as Strategies). They integrated multiple compilers/frameworks (Qiskit, Cirq, PyZX, Pytket) and orchestration of composite pipelines composed from their subroutines. The platform measures and logs metrics at each compilation stage (gate counts by gate class, circuit depth, two-qubit depth, runtime, classical fidelity for small circuits, and a phenomenological circuit cost function combining gate fidelities and a depth penalty K). Benchmarks included: KAK-optimal two-qubit and three-qubit synthesis tests, extensive random-circuit experiments (varying two-qubit density and gate set), structured algorithmic circuits (UCCSD VQE ansatz, Trotterized TFIM dynamics, Grover, and option-pricing via amplitude estimation), routing/mapping tests on k-regular random graphs and real device coupling graphs, and a brute-force search to build composite optimization pipelines from a restricted set of subroutines. Results were aggregated, compared to theoretical bounds (e.g., KAK lower bounds for 2- and 3-qubit circuits), and exported as CSV/LaTeX/PDF reports with input/output QASM for reproducibility and debugging.

**Algorithms used:** VQE (UCCSD ansatz), Trotter decomposition (quantum dynamics / TFIM), Grover search, Amplitude Estimation (option pricing / European call/put), KAK (Cartan) decomposition / two-qubit synthesis, Phase-polynomial (Pauli gadget) optimizations, Random circuit generation / Haar-sampled single-qubit rotations
**Frameworks:** Arline Benchmarks / Arline Quantum (platform), Qiskit, Cirq, PyZX, Pytket (tket), QCEC (integration for equivalence checking), QFAST (used for three-qubit synthesis verification; cited integration planned: VOQC, Quilc, ProjectQ, Staq)

**Experimental setup:** Benchmarks used a mixture of synthetic circuits and structured algorithmic circuits. Random circuits were generated with specified gate sets and two-qubit gate densities (e.g. p(CX)=0.1 or 0.9), uniform sampling of control/target qubits, single-qubit continuous angles sampled from Haar or uniform distributions. Some tests assumed all-to-all connectivity to isolate optimization effects; routing/mapping tests used random k-regular graphs (N=16) and real-device coupling maps. Hardware models included Mock IBM All2All (all-to-all), IBM Rueschlikon (16 qubit), IBM Falcon (27 qubit), Rigetti Aspen (16 qubit), Google Sycamore (53 qubit), IonQ (32 qubit). For cost-function evaluation, vendor-reported average single-qubit and two-qubit fidelities and a depth penalty parameter K were used (values provided in the paper). Final outputs were rebased to hardware-native gate sets for fair comparison. Reports (CSV/LaTeX/PDF) and QASM of intermediate stages were produced.

**Dataset:** No external financial market dataset was used. The 'financial' use-case consisted of quantum circuits that implement option pricing via the amplitude estimation algorithm for a European call/put option (circuit instances synthesized according to references). All experiments used synthetic circuits (random circuits) or algorithmic circuit instances (UCCSD, Trotter, Grover, amplitude-estimation option-pricing circuits) rather than empirical financial datasets.

### Reproducibility
Code and example circuits are available in the Arline Benchmarks repository (cited in the paper). The package can be installed via pip (pip install arline-benchmarks). Benchmark experiments are configured via jsonnet configuration files; outputs include CSV, LaTeX and PDF reports and QASM of intermediate and final circuits. Some subroutines require rebasing to specific gate sets; reproducibility requires matching gate-set rebases and the vendor/library versions used (Qiskit, Cirq, PyZX, Pytket). The paper provides example hardware parameter values (gate fidelities and depth penalty K) and the repository contains input QASM files referenced. ## Findings
- [supported] Arline Benchmarks is an open-source Python platform that automates benchmarking of quantum compilers (Qiskit, Cirq, PyZX, Pytket) and can generate PDF/CSV/LaTeX reports with metrics and intermediate circuits.
- [supported] The platform collects per-stage metrics (single-/two-qubit gate counts, depths, a phenomenological circuit cost function, runtimes) and can rebase circuits to hardware-native gate sets for fair comparisons.
- [supported] For two-qubit random circuits drawn from [CX, U3], Qiskit, Pytket and Cirq achieve the KAK-optimal compression (gc(CX)=3, gc(U3)=8) while PyZX did not reach that optimum in the tested workflow.
- [supported] For three-qubit generic random unitaries the theoretical lower bound on CNOT count is ≈14; Pytket produced the best compression among tested compilers but did not reach that theoretical lower bound in experiments.
- [supported] Routing/mapping overhead (CX count and CX depth) decreases with increasing node degree of the hardware graph; ring (k=2) gives worst-case overhead and fully connected (k=N-1) gives no overhead in tests.
- [supported] On random k-regular graphs and tested real-device connectivities, Qiskit Transpile (SABRE) and Pytket DefaultMapping show similar routing overhead performance, while Cirq GreedyRouting produced larger overheads in these experiments.
- [supported] Compression performance strongly depends on circuit class and gate set: (a) for low CX-density random circuits most gains are in single-qubit gates, (b) for high CX-density random circuits CX reduction is modest (~10–20%), (c) for phase-polynomial circuits ([CX, Rz]) PyZX (ZX-based) achieves large CX reductions at the cost of increasing single-qubit gates.
- [supported] For structured algorithmic circuits: PytketChem (a domain-specific Pytket pipeline) and PyZX perform well on UCCSD (VQE) circuits; PytketChem outperforms others (notably in CX depth reduction) on Trotterized dynamics (TFIM) circuits by drastically changing gate layout.
- [supported] Composite (cross-compiler) pipelines—combining subroutines from multiple frameworks and ordering them appropriately—can yield better compression than any single-framework pipeline in the tested search space. A brute-force search over small sets of subroutines found improved pipelines for tested circuits.
- [supported] Individual subroutines rank differently by circuit class: e.g., FullReduce (PyZX) and PytketChem are top for UCCSD; RemoveRedundancies, Peephole, Transpile rank high for certain finance option-pricing circuits; some Cirq routines (e.g., MergeInteractions) sometimes increased single-qubit gates and cost.
- [supported] Pytket (C++ backend) had the best runtime performance in trials; Qiskit, PyZX and Cirq (Python implementations) showed comparable but slower runtimes.
- [speculative] The paper proposes a phenomenological circuit cost function (minus log product of gate fidelities with a depth penalty K) as a scalar NISQ performance metric informed by vendor fidelities; authors note it is simplistic and suggest future refinements.
- [speculative] The authors claim that more advanced, possibly ML-based, hardware-specific cost models could replace the simple phenomenological cost in future work.
- [speculative] The paper asserts that circuit equivalence checking is QMA-complete in general and highlights practical/verifiable approaches (state-vector comparison for small N, DAG-based QCEC, and VOQC integration planned).
- [speculative] The authors suggest that further improvements are possible by expanding supported compilers (Quilc, ProjectQ, Staq, VOQC), adding fault-tolerant metrics (T-count), and enlarging circuit datasets.

**Results summary:** The preprint introduces Arline Benchmarks, a modular open-source platform to automate cross-compiler benchmarking for NISQ-focused compilation tasks. Using the tool the authors empirically compare Qiskit, Pytket, Cirq and PyZX on random and structured circuits across multiple hardware gate sets and connectivities. Key empirical outcomes include: KAK-optimal two-qubit synthesis is achieved by Qiskit/Pytket/Cirq but not by PyZX in the tested pipelines; Pytket yields the best three-qubit compression among tested compilers though still short of theoretical lower bounds; routing overhead correlates inversely with connectivity (k), with Qiskit and Pytket showing similar, superior routing performance relative to Cirq in these tests; compression success is highly circuit-class dependent (e.g., PyZX excels on phase-polynomial circuits); domain-specific passes (PytketChem) strongly benefit Trotterized dynamics and UCCSD circuits; and composite cross-compiler pipelines found via brute-force search can outperform single-vendor pipelines. The authors also report that Pytket had the best runtime performance and propose a pragmatic circuit cost metric based on gate fidelities and depth penalty for NISQ assessment.

## Limitations
- The circuit cost model is simplistic: it assumes factorization of gate fidelities, uses a phenomenological depth penalty K, and omits readout errors, scheduling details, crosstalk and non‑Markovian noise.
- The depth penalty parameter K is hard to estimate from first principles for a given device and was chosen phenomenologically.
- Equivalence checking is computationally hard (QMA-complete); the direct statevector/probability fidelity check only scales to small N (N < 15) and is not feasible for large/unstructured circuits.
- Verification tools (e.g., QCEC) may still fail or be impractical for unstructured random circuits with continuous gates.
- PyZX has limited input gate-set support and does not natively support routing; its optimizer sometimes increases gate counts and fails to reach optimal compression for some benchmarks.
- Many experiments assume all-to-all connectivity to isolate optimization from routing; this reduces realism for connectivity-constrained hardware.
- Composite pipeline search was performed by brute force over a small set of subroutines (S=5) and limited maximum depth (MaxD), because the search space grows factorially/exponentially — so the composite-pipeline results are limited by search scope.
- Benchmarks focus on NISQ-era metrics (single- and two-qubit gate counts, depth, a phenomenological cost) and do not address fault-tolerant-era metrics (e.g., T-gate count/depth) in depth.
- Some compilation pipelines rebase/optimize in intermediate (non-native) gate sets and then translate to hardware-native gates (e.g., IonQ, Rigetti), which can introduce large single-qubit overheads that the current pipelines are not designed to avoid.
- The hardware fidelity parameters used (Table I) are taken from vendor publications / mock devices and are phenomenological; results depend on these choices.
- [inferred] The benchmarking suite currently lacks some widely used compilers (e.g., Quilc, ProjectQ, Staq, VOQC) limiting comparability across all major tools.
- [inferred] The approach does not yet provide predictive tools to determine, a priori, which optimization pipeline will perform best for a given circuit instance.
- [inferred] Parametric (symbolic) compilation for variational algorithms was not benchmarked here, though it is relevant for low-latency VQE/QAOA workflows.
## Open questions
- How can the depth-penalty parameter K be determined quantitatively from device-specific physical parameters (gate times, T1/T2, scheduling) rather than chosen phenomenologically?
- How to construct more realistic, hardware-aware cost models that incorporate scheduling, parallelism, cross-talk, readout errors, and non‑Markovian noise?
- How can circuit equivalence checking be scaled to larger, unstructured circuits with continuous single-qubit gates in a practical way?
- Can one develop methods to predict, before compilation, the potential for compression for an arbitrary circuit and the most effective optimization passes for it?
- What algorithmic or heuristic strategies can make composite-pipeline search (ordering and selection of passes across compilers) scalable beyond small brute-force searches?
- How to best perform optimizations directly in hardware-native gate sets to avoid rebase-induced overheads (e.g., for IonQ and Rigetti) and what pipeline designs enable that?
- What are the appropriate benchmarking methodologies and metrics for the near‑fault‑tolerant / fault‑tolerant regime (e.g., T-count and T-depth combined with residual noise effects)?
- How representative are random-circuit benchmarks of real algorithmic workloads, and how should benchmark suites be designed to reflect practical financial-service quantum applications?
- How sensitive are the benchmarking outcomes to the choice of vendor-reported fidelities and noise parameters, and can benchmarks be standardized to be robust to such variability?
- How to integrate parametric compilation benchmarking (for VQE/QAOA) and measure latency benefits/overheads in iterative optimization loops?

**Future work:**
- Extend integration to additional frameworks: Quilc, ProjectQ, Staq, and VOQC.
- Add Verified Optimizer for Quantum Circuits (VOQC) module with built-in equivalence checking.
- Expand the database of target circuits / quantum algorithms (more benchmarks).
- Introduce additional metrics such as T-gate count, quantum volume for compressed circuits, and other fault-tolerant relevant measures.
- Include other test types such as unitary synthesis and quantum state preparation benchmarks.
- Develop more realistic circuit cost functions incorporating scheduling, measurement/readout errors, cross-talk, and non‑Markovian noise; possibly replace handcrafted cost functions with learned (neural‑network) approximators.
- Introduce benchmarking of parametric (symbolic) compilation for variational algorithms (VQE/QAOA) in future releases.
- Improve equivalence checking workflows and integrate scalable tools (e.g., QCEC, VOQC) into the pipeline for larger circuits.
- Explore and automate construction of composite pipelines (cross-compiler pass sequencing) with more efficient search strategies than brute force.
## Key ideas
- #idea:near-term-feasibility — Arline Benchmarks is an open-source automated platform targeted at NISQ-era compiler evaluation, demonstrating that composite compiler pipelines can reduce gate counts and depth across random and structured circuits (VQE, Trotter dynamics, Grover, and amplitude-estimation option-pricing circuits).
- #idea:hybrid-approach — The platform composes vendor-specific classical compilation/optimization subroutines into composite pipelines (brute-force search over ordered subroutines) and integrates classical verification and fidelity-based cost metrics to guide compilation choices.
- #limitation:simulation-only — All experiments are performed via compilation and classical simulation/analysis (exported QASM, fidelity proxies) with no executions on real QPUs reported.
- #limitation:no-empirical-validation — Validation is limited to classical fidelity comparisons for small circuits (N<15) and equivalence checks (QCEC); there is no hardware-run empirical benchmarking to confirm runtime/noise behavior.
- #limitation:qubit-count — Finance-relevant amplitude-estimation circuits were tested at small sizes (2–11 qubits) and mapping tests used up to N=16; these sizes are far below production-scale financial Monte Carlo usage, limiting direct applicability to real-world derivative pricing workloads.
- #limitation:noise — Cost evaluation relies on a phenomenological circuit cost combining vendor-reported average fidelities and a depth penalty K rather than detailed noise simulation or error-mitigation analysis, so conclusions about compiled-circuit performance under realistic noise are limited.
