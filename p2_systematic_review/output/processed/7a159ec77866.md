## Limitations
- Quantum hardware is difficult and expensive to manufacture and needs ultra-cold, specialized environments.
- Qubit coherence times are limited (microseconds–milliseconds for solid-state; seconds–minutes for trapped ions), constraining circuit depth and algorithm runtime.
- Qubit connectivity is often limited (e.g., superconducting qubits limited to nearest neighbours), complicating algorithm mapping.
- Physical qubits are not perfectly identical; device variability makes scaling and calibration harder.
- Gate fidelity is imperfect and errors accumulate quickly, limiting useful circuit depth without error correction.
- Qubit count alone is an inadequate metric of computational power — coherence, fidelity, connectivity and other system-level properties matter.
- Logical (error-corrected) qubits have not yet been realised in hardware at scale.
- Only a small number of current quantum devices can address real-world, industrial-scale applications; commercial advantage timelines are uncertain.
- Quantum computing will not accelerate ordinary desktop applications (e.g., Word, Excel); benefits are for specific complex problems.
- Current fraud-detection systems have high false-positive rates; quantum approaches are proposed but not yet validated in production.
