## Abstract summary
The paper proposes a challenging yet solvable benchmark for large-scale multi-period portfolio optimization under realistic market frictions, formulating the problem as a QUBO so it is compatible with both classical and quantum approaches. The authors run experiments on real S&P500 data (up to 499 assets) comparing digital BQP solvers (e.g., Gurobi) and GPU QUBO heuristics (ABS2/qoqo) alongside quantum-ready/quantum platforms (D-Wave, IBM Qiskit, InfinityQ), and report that while current quantum hardware cannot yet surpass classical methods, the QUBO benchmark provides a fair, transparent basis to measure future quantum advantage.
## Methodology
The study formulates a realistic multi-period discrete portfolio optimization problem (mean-variance objective) with transaction costs, short selling, integer/block constraints and path-dependent cash limits, and casts it as a Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO) and equivalent Binary Quadratic Program (BQP). The QUBO/BQP objective includes a time-indexed risk term q * sum_{t} x_t^T (P_t Σ_t P_t) x_t, a profit term based on price differences, transaction costs proportional to absolute position changes, short-selling costs, terminal liquidation costs, interest on unspent cash, and quadratic penalty terms enforcing capital and asset-count constraints via binary-encoded slack variables. Two empirical experiments are run: a medium-scale problem (n=200 assets, T=10 days, k=3 blocks per asset, B=60, C=10 resulting in 12,100 binary variables) and a large-scale problem (n=499, T=15, same k/B/C resulting in 45,060 binaries). The risk-aversion parameter q is swept over eight values (0, 1e-6, 1e-5, 5e-5, 1e-4, 5e-4, 1e-3, 1e-2). Digital solvers (Gurobi 11.0, CPU-based Branch-and-Bound with relaxation-based lower bounds) are used as precise baselines; GPU-based heuristic QUBO solver ABS2 (integrated in qoqo) runs on an NVIDIA A100 GPU to produce rapid heuristic solutions; quantum/quantum-ready platforms explored include IBM Qiskit (VQE/QAOA style variational circuits), D-Wave Advantage quantum annealer (including hybrid workflows for larger problems), and InfinityQ’s quantum-inspired Ising solver. Performance is evaluated using computational metrics (objective value, optimality gap to BQP relaxation lower bound, time-to-solution) and economic metrics (portfolio volatility, profits, Sharpe ratio, transaction costs, and trading trajectories). Time limits were set per-solver (Gurobi: 10,000 s; ABS2: 3,600 s) and results compared across q values to produce Pareto frontiers and convergence profiles.

**Algorithms used:** Branch-and-Bound (via Gurobi), Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO) heuristics (ABS2 / qoqo), Adaptive Bulk Search (ABS2), Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE), Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) (discussed/implemented via Qiskit), Adiabatic Quantum Annealing (D-Wave), Quantum-inspired Ising solver (InfinityQ)
**Frameworks:** Gurobi 11.0, qoqo (ABS2 integration), NVIDIA CUDA / GPU (A100) for ABS2, IBM Qiskit, D-Wave (Advantage / Hybrid), InfinityQ (Ising machine)

**Experimental setup:** Two realistic datasets from S&P 500 stocks were prepared: a 200-stock, 10-day scenario (Oct 2–13, 2023) and a 499-stock, 15-day scenario (Apr 29–May 17, 2024). BQP instances were solved with Gurobi (CPU workstation) with a 10,000 s time limit to obtain feasible solutions and relaxation-based lower bounds. QUBO instances were solved with ABS2 (qoqo) on an NVIDIA A100 GPU with a 3,600 s time limit. Additional quantum/quantum-ready experiments used IBM Qiskit (VQE/QAOA on IBM superconducting backends), D-Wave Advantage annealer (Pegasus topology, with hybrid decomposition for larger instances), and InfinityQ’s Ising-machine service. Key optimization parameters: transaction cost δ=0.1%, daily risk-free rate ρc=0.01%, short-sale loan rate ρs=0.0025%, capital C=10 units, B=60 assets, k=3 blocks per asset. The risk-aversion parameter q was varied across eight values. Evaluation metrics included objective values, optimality gaps vs the BQP relaxation lower bound, time-to-first-best-solution (TTS), Sharpe ratio, transaction costs, and Pareto frontiers.

**Dataset:** Real historical price data for S&P 500 constituent stocks. Two experimental selections were used: top 200 market-cap stocks from S&P 500 for Oct 2–13, 2023 (n=200, T=10) and 499 S&P 500 stocks for Apr 29–May 17, 2024 (n=499, T=15). The model uses per-asset daily prices p_{i,t} and time-indexed covariance matrices σ_{ij,t}. No public code or explicit public data repository is referenced in the paper.

## Findings
- [supported] The authors formulate a multi-period portfolio optimization with transaction costs, short-selling, integer block selection and cash constraints as a QUBO/BQP problem and implement it.
- [supported] The authors constructed benchmark instances up to n=499 assets, T=15 timesteps (45,060 binary variables in the QUBO representation) and a medium instance n=200, T=10 (12,100 variables).
- [supported] Classical BQP solver (Gurobi 11.0) provides provable bounds (lower/upper) and can solve these instances given sufficient time (10,000s time limit used).
- [supported] GPU-based heuristic QUBO solver (qoqo/ABS2) often finds good feasible solutions much faster than Gurobi in early-stage runtime; in many tested settings ABS2 reached competitive objective values quickly.
- [supported] For harder, balanced risk-profit settings (intermediate q), Gurobi can achieve better final objective values with longer runtime, while ABS2 attains high-quality solutions more quickly but sometimes with larger final gaps.
- [supported] In extreme risk-aversion or profit-only regimes (q at extremes) the optimization becomes easier; both solvers rapidly converge to the intuitive solutions (e.g., cash-only when q large).
- [supported] Quantum platforms tested (D-Wave annealer and InfinityQ quantum-inspired solver and IBM Qiskit experiments) produced objectively worse optimization results and lower economic performance (Sharpe ratios) than the best classical/digital solvers in these experiments.
- [supported] Empirically, objective improvements for both ABS2 and Gurobi largely plateau after a few hundred seconds in these instances, with only marginal gains afterward under the tested settings.
- [speculative] Any QUBO can be mapped to an Ising Hamiltonian and is therefore ‘‘quantum-ready’’ for variational/annealing quantum algorithms (QAOA, VQE, QA).
- [speculative] QAOA (in theory) can produce high probability of good or optimal solutions when run with sufficient depth/steps; current NISQ hardware, however, cannot practically realize the required depth for the studied large instances.
- [speculative] The provided QUBO benchmark will serve as a transparent and comparable baseline to measure future quantum (or hybrid) advantages as hardware/software improve.

**Results summary:** The paper presents a QUBO/BQP formulation of a realistic multi-period portfolio optimization problem (transaction costs, short-sales, integer blocks, cash constraints) and releases medium (n=200, T=10; 12,100 vars) and large (n=499, T=15; 45,060 vars) benchmark instances. Empirical experiments show that classical exact/branch-and-bound BQP solving (Gurobi) yields provable bounds and strong final solutions given long runtimes, while a GPU-based QUBO heuristic (qoqo/ABS2) typically attains high-quality feasible solutions faster in early runtime. For balanced risk-profit settings, Gurobi can surpass ABS2 when allowed much longer solve times. Current quantum hardware and quantum-inspired solvers tested (D-Wave, InfinityQ, IBM Qiskit runs) underperformed the best digital solvers by objective and economic metrics (Sharpe ratios). The authors argue the QUBO benchmark is ‘‘quantum-ready’’ and useful to measure future progress but do not demonstrate a practical quantum advantage on present devices.

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** not-applicable

The paper does not demonstrate a practical quantum advantage on current hardware. It reports that tested quantum and quantum-inspired solvers underperformed classical/digital methods on the presented large, realistic benchmarks. The authors present the QUBO formulation as 'quantum-ready' and argue the benchmark will help identify future quantum advantages as hardware and algorithms improve, but they make no empirical claim of existing quantum advantage.
## Limitations
- Quantum computing advantages remain unproven empirically; current experiments do not demonstrate a clear quantum advantage over classical methods (author-stated).
- Current quantum hardware and software cannot effectively solve the largest benchmark instances in this study (e.g., n=499, T=15); only digital solvers could solve the large-scale problem (author-stated).
- Quantum hardware suffers from robustness and noise issues and lacks full error correction, limiting practical performance of VQE/QAOA/other variational methods (author-stated).
- QUBO problem matrices tend to be dense and grow quadratically with the number of assets, creating severe scaling and embedding difficulties (author-stated).
- Proving global optimality for large-scale BQP/QUBO instances is computationally demanding; optimality gaps can be large and require long solver runtimes or cannot be closed within time limits (author-stated).
- [inferred] Benchmark and experiments rely on simplified and fixed market-model parameters (uniform transaction cost δ, fixed loan rate ρs, normalized capital units, etc.), which may limit the generality of conclusions for other market settings.
- [inferred] Some realistic market frictions are not modeled or are simplified (e.g., liquidity constraints, market impact, slippage, taxes, varying transaction costs), reducing economic realism of the benchmark.
- [inferred] The discrete/block binary representation of holdings (k blocks per asset) coarsely approximates real share counts and may limit the realism of allocation granularity and execution implications.
- [inferred] Quantum platforms (gate-based and annealers) are constrained by qubit connectivity, embedding overhead and the maximum size of fully-connected subproblems (e.g., D-Wave Pegasus limits), restricting direct applicability to large dense QUBOs.
- [inferred] Heuristic QUBO solvers (e.g., ABS2/qoqo) achieve good near-term performance but do not provide the same provable optimality guarantees (tight bounds) as classical BQP solvers like Gurobi.
- Solver progress often plateaus after ~600 seconds in these experiments; additional runtime yields marginal improvements for both QUBO and BQP approaches (author observation).
- [inferred] Experiments report in-sample optimization results; out-of-sample robustness, transaction execution feasibility, and economic viability under live trading conditions are not evaluated.
- [inferred] Operational and implementation aspects necessary for live trading (latency, order execution scheduling, regulatory constraints, borrowing limits beyond simple rates) are not modeled or tested.

## Key ideas
- #idea:near-term-feasibility — Empirical benchmark across Gurobi, GPU QUBO heuristics (ABS2), D-Wave, IBM VQE/QAOA and InfinityQ shows current quantum and quantum-ready platforms do not outperform classical solvers on realistic multi-period portfolio QUBOs (n=200 up to n=499, up to 45,060 binaries).
- #idea:hybrid-approach — Hybrid workflows (D-Wave hybrid decomposition, classical optimizers with variational circuits) and quantum-inspired Ising solvers provide viable routes to obtain feasible solutions for large instances, but they are used as complements rather than replacements for classical BQP solvers.
- #limitation:qubit-count — The full problem encoding (tens of thousands of binary variables) far exceeds current QPU capacity, necessitating decomposition/hybridization and preventing end-to-end quantum solutions.
- #limitation:noise — Results on IBM variational circuits and other QPUs were degraded by hardware noise and limitations of NISQ devices, contributing to inferior objective values and larger optimality gaps relative to classical baselines.
- #limitation:data-encoding — Binary slack-variable encoding and quadratic penalties inflate problem size (many auxiliary binaries), highlighting a costly data-encoding overhead for mapping realistic multi-period constraints to QUBO/Ising form.
- #idea:near-term-feasibility — The paper proposes the presented large-scale QUBO benchmark as a transparent standard to measure and track future quantum advantage, despite no current advantage being observed.
## Contradictions
- The paper provides empirical evidence that classical BQP solver (Gurobi) and GPU-based QUBO heuristics (ABS2 on A100) outperform current quantum platforms (IBM VQE/QAOA, D-Wave annealer, InfinityQ Ising) on both medium (n=200) and large (n=499) portfolio instances, contradicting claims that near-term quantum devices already deliver superior performance for practical portfolio optimization.
- Scaling results show that mapping realistic multi-period portfolio problems (up to ~45k binary variables) requires decomposition or hybrid classical-quantum approaches; this contradicts optimistic assertions that current annealers or variational circuits can directly scale to industrial-sized QUBOs without hybridization.
