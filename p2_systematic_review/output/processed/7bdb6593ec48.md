## Abstract summary
The paper introduces a bi-objective weighted portfolio optimization framework that integrates the Trend Ratio (TR) model with D-Wave's constrained quadratic model (CQM) hybrid quantum annealing solver. Using a scalarization-based non-dominated analysis across different weights for risk and return, and evaluating cross-market data from the DJIA and Nikkei, the method generates diverse high-quality portfolios and demonstrates competitive performance and runtime versus classical multi-objective evolutionary algorithms. The study also analyzes the scalarization step size (∆λ) to balance solution quality and computational cost.
## Methodology
The paper proposes a bi-objective weighted portfolio optimization framework that integrates the Trend Ratio (TR) performance model with quantum annealing via D-Wave's constrained quadratic model (CQM) hybrid solver. Portfolios are encoded with integer allocation variables xi ∈ {0,1,...,100} representing percentage of initial funds allocated to each asset, subject to the constraint sum_i xi ≤ 100. Share counts ωi are computed from xi by determining the number of shares purchasable per 1% of funds at the first-day price and scaling by xi to avoid rounding issues in the CQM integer formulation. The TR objective is decomposed into daily expected return (DER) and daily risk (DR) computed from portfolio value time series FS(j) over the experiment window; the solver minimizes a scalarized objective E(ω) = λ·DR − (1−λ)·DER. Bi-objective behavior is achieved via scalarization: multiple independent optimization runs are executed with λ stepped from 0 to 1 in increments Δλ (e.g. 0.1) to generate a diverse set of trade-off solutions; non-dominated solutions across runs are aggregated to form the estimated Pareto/front (combined NDS). Experimental comparisons are made against classical multi-objective evolutionary algorithms (SMS-EMOA, NSGA-II, MOEA/D) using standard settings to evaluate quality (Hypervolume, HV and HV rate) and convergence (IGD). The approach is evaluated on real financial data from WRDS covering DJIA (30 constituents), Nikkei (top 30 by market cap), and a combined cross-market set (60 assets) over 2022–2024 (36 monthly intervals).

**Algorithms used:** Quantum annealing (D-Wave Leap hybrid CQM solver), Scalarization (weighted-sum λ), Trend Ratio (TR) model, SMS-EMOA, NSGA-II, MOEA/D
**Frameworks:** D-Wave Leap hybrid CQM

**Experimental setup:** D-Wave Leap hybrid CQM solver executed targeting the D-Wave Advantage quantum system; each QA run given a 5 second time limit (solver minimum). Integer allocation variables xi ∈ [0,100], with total allocation ≤100. λ scalarization parameter varied in steps of Δλ (default experiments used Δλ = 0.1; additional tests used 0.2, 0.05, 0.025). Comparison MOEAs configured with population=100, generations=250, crossover probability=0.9, mutation probability=1/number_of_stocks; MOEA/D neighborhood size T=10. Evaluation metrics: Hypervolume (HV), HV rate (relative to combined NDS), and Inverted Generational Distance (IGD) using the combined NDS as reference. Data span: 2022–2024, monthly intervals (D=36).

**Dataset:** Historical equity price data from Wharton Research Data Services (WRDS). Three datasets: DJIA (all 30 constituents), NIKKEI (top 30 stocks by market capitalization), and a cross-market combined set of the two (60 assets). Time period: 2022–2024 (three years, 36 monthly intervals).
## Experiment details
### Input
Source: WRDS (Wharton Research Data Services). Size: 30 assets (DJIA), 30 assets (selected top-cap NIKKEI), and 60 assets (cross-market), with 36 monthly data points per asset (2022–2024). Preprocessing: selection of top 30 Nikkei stocks by market cap; price series aligned to experiment window; initial fund normalization used (fundsini); share counts computed from first-day prices with rounding handled by computing shares per 1% of funds then scaling by xi. No additional public preprocessing scripts or code repository are reported.

### Process
1) Formulate integer-weighted portfolio variables xi ∈ {0..100}, enforce sum_i xi ≤ 100. 2) Compute shares ωi from xi using 1%-based share computation and first-day prices. 3) Compute daily portfolio value FS(j) for each day j, derive DER (daily expected return) and trend line TL(j), compute DR as average squared deviation from TL scaled by α. 4) For a chosen scalarization parameter λ (λ ∈ [0,1] stepped by Δλ), form single-objective E(ω)=λ·DR − (1−λ)·DER. 5) Submit the CQM problem to D-Wave Leap hybrid CQM solver (Advantage system) with per-run time limit 5s; collect solutions. 6) Repeat for each λ value to produce a set of candidate solutions. 7) Aggregate non-dominated solutions across λ runs to form combined NDS. 8) Evaluate solution sets using HV, HV rate (reference point defined using max profit×1.01 and min risk×0.99) and IGD relative to the combined NDS. 9) Compare results against MOEAs (SMS-EMOA, NSGA-II, MOEA/D) run with specified EA parameters. Additional experiments varied Δλ (0.2, 0.1, 0.05, 0.025) to study trade-off between solution quality and runtime.

### Output
Outputs: sets of portfolio allocations (integer percentages xi and implied share counts ωi) per optimization run. Performance metrics reported per month and averaged over the 3-year period: Hypervolume (HV), HV rate (HV relative to combined NDS), Inverted Generational Distance (IGD) against combined NDS, number of non-dominated solutions (NDS#), and measured runtime (seconds). Baselines: SMS-EMOA, NSGA-II, MOEA/D with identical data and evaluation metrics. Additional analyses: market-level comparisons (DJIA vs NIKKEI vs cross-market) and sensitivity analysis over Δλ values.

## Findings

- [supported] In experiments on 2022–2024 data, a cross-market universe (DJIA + Nikkei top 30) produced better Pareto-front metrics (higher HV, lower IGD) than treating each market individually.
- [supported] The proposed method outperformed the tested MOEAs (SMS-EMOA, NSGA-II, MOEA/D) on cross-market experiments in terms of HV, IGD, number of non-dominated solutions, while having comparable runtime in the reported configurations.

**Results summary:** The authors present a bi-objective portfolio optimization framework combining the Trend Ratio (TR) performance model with D-Wave's constrained quadratic model (CQM) hybrid solver. By scalarizing the two objectives (daily risk and daily expected return) with a tunable weight λ and running multiple optimizations with varying λ (step Δλ), they generate a broad non-dominated solution set. Empirical tests on 2022–2024 data for DJIA, Nikkei, and a combined cross-market (60 assets) show that the cross-market yields improved Pareto-front quality and diversity. Compared to SMS-EMOA, NSGA-II, and MOEA/D in the cross-market setting, the proposed method achieved higher hypervolume, lower IGD, and found more low-risk solutions. Smaller Δλ increments improve solution quality at the cost of substantially increased runtime; Δλ = 0.1 was recommended as a practical balance in their experiments.

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** speculative

The paper demonstrates that a D-Wave Leap CQM hybrid solver can produce competitive and in some metrics superior Pareto-fronts versus classical MOEAs on the tested instances; however, the solver used is a hybrid quantum-classical system and the experiments do not isolate a pure quantum speedup or prove an unambiguous quantum advantage. The authors present evidence of practical competitiveness and potential rather than a rigorous demonstration of quantum advantage.
## Limitations
- Most current quantum annealing (QA) applications focus on single-objective formulations; applying QA to multi-objective portfolio optimization remains largely unexplored (author-stated).
- The multi-objective problem is solved via scalarization (weighted sum) requiring multiple runs with different weights; this approach may miss parts of the Pareto front and requires repeated solver invocations (author-stated).
- Use of D-Wave’s constrained quadratic model (CQM) hybrid solver implies dependence on hybrid classical-quantum infrastructure and solver-specific behavior (author-stated).
- Minimum solver runtime and resource constraints (e.g., 5 s per QA run) limit experiment design and may affect search thoroughness (author-stated).
- The true Pareto front is unavailable; evaluation relies on a combined NDS approximation constructed from all method outputs (author-stated).
- Dataset and scenario choices are limited: DJIA (30 stocks), top-30 Nikkei stocks, and a 60-asset cross-market; experiments cover a 2022–2024 period only, which restricts generalizability across broader markets and longer horizons (author-stated).
- The trend ratio (TR) model requires reformulation for CQM and uses parameters (e.g., α) that vary by experiment period; parameter selection may be ad hoc and affect results (author-stated).
- Allocation representation is integer-percentage (xi ∈ {0,1,…,100}) with a rounding workaround for share counts; this discretization and workaround may limit fidelity relative to continuous allocation models (author-stated).
- Smaller ∆λ (finer weight increments) improves NDS quality but at substantial computational cost; improvement in solution quality is not proportional to runtime growth, making the trade-off a practical limitation (author-stated).
- [inferred] Weighted-sum scalarization can fail to recover non-convex regions of the Pareto front, so some efficient trade-offs may be systematically unattainable with the chosen method.
- [inferred] Hybrid solver results may be sensitive to classical post-processing and hybrid orchestration; it is unclear how much of the performance gain is due to quantum annealing versus classical components.
- [inferred] The study omits practical trading considerations such as transaction costs, taxes, minimum lot sizes, liquidity constraints, and market impact, which could materially affect portfolio realizability.
- [inferred] Limiting Nikkei selection to top-30 by market cap may bias cross-market diversification results and may not reflect the entire index dynamics.

- [inferred] Use of a recent three-year window (2022–2024) may not capture diverse market regimes (e.g., crises, long bull/bear cycles); robustness across regimes remains untested.
- [inferred] Rounding and discrete share count workaround introduce modeling approximations whose impact on optimality and risk-return calculations is not fully quantified.
## Open questions
- How well does the proposed QA + CQM hybrid approach scale to larger universes (hundreds or thousands of assets) and longer historical periods?
- Can alternative multi-objective QA strategies (e.g., true multi-objective quantum formulations, Pareto-based quantum methods, or multi-objective hybrid heuristics) overcome scalarization limitations and recover non-convex Pareto regions?
- What is the relative contribution of quantum annealing versus the classical components in the hybrid solver to the observed performance gains?
- How sensitive are results to TR model hyperparameters (e.g., α) and to the TR model itself compared to conventional risk measures (variance, CVaR, drawdown-based metrics)?
- How to choose ∆λ adaptively (or design smarter weight-sweeping strategies) to maximize Pareto coverage while minimizing total runtime?
- How robust are the obtained non-dominated solution sets across different market regimes, out-of-sample periods, and other international exchanges beyond DJIA and Nikkei?
- How would incorporating realistic trading constraints (transaction costs, liquidity, minimum tradeable units, shorting, leverage) affect solution quality and solver performance?
- Is it possible to obtain or estimate the true Pareto front for benchmarking (rather than constructing a combined NDS from competing methods), and how would that affect metric-based comparisons?
- How does discretizing allocations into integer percentages (0–100) affect optimality vs using continuous allocation variables, and can CQM handle high-resolution continuous or mixed-integer formulations efficiently?
- What solver parameter tuning (e.g., run time, annealing schedule, hybrid solver settings) yields the best trade-off between solution quality and compute cost in practice?
- Can the method be adapted to incorporate dynamic or multi-period rebalancing strategies (beyond single-period allocation) within the QA/CQM framework?
