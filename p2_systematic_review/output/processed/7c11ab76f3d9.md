## Abstract summary
This preprint benchmarks two quantum algorithms—Quantum Imaginary-Time Evolution (QITE) and the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA)—for solving Markowitz portfolio optimization by mapping it to an Ising Hamiltonian and testing on digital quantum hardware and noisy simulators. The study finds that QAOA converges well in noiseless settings and scales to larger systems but is highly sensitive to realistic gate noise, while QITE is more robust to noise at the cost of substantially higher classical optimization effort.
## Methodology
The authors benchmark two quantum approaches for solving a digitized Markowitz portfolio optimization mapped to an Ising Hamiltonian. They (1) encode integer investment fractions using a binary expansion (w bits per asset) and derive a QUBO then Ising Hamiltonian (hi, Jij) from synthetic historical price data; (2) implement and compare QAOA and QITE using parameterized variational circuits composed of single-qubit U3 gates and two-qubit ECR/CX entangling gates (adjacent coupling); (3) for QAOA they minimize the Hamiltonian expectation (and optionally the shifted objective |Eg - E(s)|) using classical optimizers (primarily COBYLA, with L-BFGS-B tested), iterating parameters for hundreds-to-thousands of circuit evaluations; (4) for QITE they approximate the non-unitary imaginary-time propagator e^{-βH} by embedding it into a larger unitary using SVD/QR-based construction with an ancilla qubit and post-selection, and then train a variational circuit to approximate that unitary via overlap minimization (CQITE); (5) evaluate performance across noiseless simulation, controlled local noisy simulators (with tunable CX error rates), MPS simulations for larger (20/30 qubit) instances, and runs of QITE on IBM Quantum hardware (“Brisbane”). Metrics include energy deviation |Eg−E|, portfolio return error F_error, and measured output-state probability distributions. Noise models are calibrated to device error parameters and controlled CX/ECR error rates are swept to evaluate robustness.

**Algorithms used:** Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA), Quantum Imaginary-Time Evolution (QITE), COBYLA (classical optimizer), L-BFGS-B (classical optimizer)
**Frameworks:** Qiskit, IBM Quantum

**Experimental setup:** Simulations performed on: (a) noiseless local simulator (Qiskit), (b) local noisy simulator with controlled CX error rates (examples reported: 0.001, 0.007, 0.011), (c) IBM Quantum hardware (device: "Brisbane") for QITE runs (selected qubit chain indices [0..12], mean ECR error ≈ 0.007), and (d) an MPS simulator for 20- and 30-qubit noiseless experiments. Variational ansatz layers combine U3 single-qubit rotations and ECR (or CX in local sim) two-qubit gates; QAOA used shallow circuits (2 layers reported for 9-qubit), QITE required deeper circuits (4–8 layers for 9-qubit).

**Dataset:** Synthetic historical price data generated per-instance: for 9-qubit experiments m=3 assets, Nf=100 historical price points per asset, budget b=10. Price generator au,0 sampled uniformly from [b/10, b]; subsequent points au,l generated as au,l = (1+α) au,0 with α ~ Uniform[-0.25, 0.25]. Expected returns ru and covariance cu,v computed from these series and then rescaled into QUBO/Ising coefficients. 100 distinct Hamiltonian instances generated for the 9-qubit studies.
## Experiment details
### Input
Source: synthetically generated asset-price time series as described above. Size/examples: 9-qubit instances built from m=3 assets with w=3 bits per asset (n = m·w = 9 binary variables), Nf=100 price points, 100 problem instances generated. Parameters for portfolio objective: θ1=0.8, θ2=0.1, θ3=0.1, budget b=10, quantization fraction pw = 1/2^(w-1). Preprocessing: compute daily returns, expected returns ru (Eq. 2), covariance cu,v (Eq. 3), rescale to QUBO parameters qi and Qi,j and map xi -> (1+σz)/2 to obtain Ising hi, Ji,j.

### Process
Pipeline: (1) Generate synthetic price histories and compute ru, cu,v; (2) form QUBO and map to Ising Hamiltonian H = Σi hi σz_i + Σi<j Jij σz_i σz_j; (3) construct variational ansatz layers (U3 per qubit + ECR/CX entanglers) — QAOA uses shallow layers (p≈2 for 9-qubit), QITE uses deeper layers to approximate the embedding unitary; (4a) QAOA: initialize parameters, iteratively evaluate E(s)=⟨ψ(s)|H|ψ(s)⟩ on simulator/hardware and minimize cost (CQAOA = |Eg−E(s)| or ⟨H⟩) using COBYLA (L-BFGS-B tested) for O(600–1000) iterations for 9-qubit; (4b) QITE: build target non-unitary Unon = e^{-βH}, perform SVD/QR to embed into unitary U with ancilla, train variational V(s) to minimize overlap cost CQITE(s)=1−Tr[V†(s)Ucircuit]/2^n (convergence threshold CQITE<0.1), perform ancilla post-selection to obtain approximate imaginary-time evolved state; (5) measure energy, return F and output-state distributions; (6) repeat across noise levels using calibrated local noise models and on IBM hardware for QITE. Additional scaling tests run QAOA on MPS simulator for 20 and 30 qubits.

### Output
Reported outputs and metrics: (1) energy deviation |Eg − E(s)| during optimization; (2) minimum achieved energy deviation per instance; (3) portfolio return error F_error = F_ideal − F_circuit (F_ideal ≈ 0 for normalized formulation); (4) output-state probability distributions (bitstring frequencies) to identify optimal bitstrings; (5) histograms of F_error across 50–100 instances; (6) comparisons against random-state baseline and across noise levels. Baselines: noiseless QAOA simulations and random (uniform) states. Results include convergence traces, minimum achieved errors, and sensitivity to two-qubit gate error rates.

## Findings
- [supported] In noiseless digital simulations, QAOA converges well to the ground state of Ising-type Hamiltonians for Markowitz portfolio instances (examples: 9-qubit instances converged after ~600–1000 iterations).
- [supported] QAOA is highly sensitive to two-qubit gate noise: increasing CX/ECR error rates destabilize the optimization and substantially degrade final energies and portfolio returns.
- [supported] QITE exhibits greater robustness to realistic device noise than QAOA in the authors' experiments: QITE runs on IBM hardware (ECR error ≈0.007) produced identifiable optimal bitstrings and low return errors.
- [supported] QITE requires substantially more classical optimization/training effort (deeper variational circuits, more classical cost) but typically needs fewer distinct quantum-circuit executions than QAOA for the problems studied.
- [supported] For 9-qubit portfolio instances on IBM hardware, QITE produced portfolio return errors (F_Error) mostly in the range ~1–6 across 50 tested instances, and optimal bitstrings remained identifiable despite noise.
- [supported] Noisy QAOA simulations with modest CX error rates produced large portfolio return errors (examples reported: F_Error exceeding ~20) and probability distributions that did not reveal the optimal bitstring.
- [supported] Reference (completely unoptimized) random states yield much larger return errors (F_Error ≈38) than well-optimized noiseless QAOA or QITE hardware runs.
- [supported] In noiseless MPS simulations, QAOA was shown to scale to intermediate sizes (20 and 30 qubits) and still reach energies near the ground-state energy (reported |Eg − Ei| ≈5 for 20 qubits and ≈10 for 30 qubits under their MPS simulations).
- [speculative] QAOA offers better long-term scalability potential on larger quantum devices if hardware noise can be effectively mitigated (claim based on noiseless/limited-noise simulations and general arguments about measurement parallelism).
- [speculative] QITE is more noise-robust in current NISQ devices but limited in practical scalability because of heavy classical training costs and deeper ansatz requirements.
- [speculative] The trade-off between QAOA (better scalability, noise-sensitive) and QITE (more noise-robust, classical-costly) implies choice of algorithm should depend on device noise characteristics and available classical resources.

**Results summary:** This preprint benchmarks QAOA and QITE for mapping Markowitz portfolio optimization to Ising Hamiltonians under noiseless and noisy digital simulations and on IBM hardware. Empirically, noiseless QAOA converges well to ground states but degrades rapidly with modest two-qubit gate noise; noisy QAOA often fails to identify optimal bitstrings and yields large return errors. QITE, implemented via a variational approximation of non-unitary imaginary-time evolution and executed on IBM hardware (ECR error ≈0.007), demonstrated greater robustness: optimal bitstrings remained identifiable and return errors were modest (typically 1–6 for 9-qubit instances). QAOA shows promise for larger-scale problems in noiseless or well-mitigated settings (MPS simulations to 20–30 qubits), whereas QITE demands heavier classical optimization and deeper circuits, limiting its scalability despite improved noise tolerance in the tested regime.

**Performance claims:**
- QAOA noiseless convergence: ~600–1000 optimization iterations for 9-qubit instances to reach small |Eg − Ei| (minimum |Eg − Ei| reported < 2.5 for several instances).
- QAOA scaling (noiseless MPS): 20-qubit instances reached |Eg − Ei| ≈5; 30-qubit instances reached |Eg − Ei| ≈10 in their MPS simulations.
- QAOA sensitivity to CX error: even CX error rate = 0.001 caused significant degradation; at CX error ≈0.007 (comparable to IBM device) QAOA produced large return errors (examples > 20); at CX error ≈0.011 performance further deteriorated toward random-state levels.
- QITE on IBM hardware (ECR/CX mean error ≈0.007): produced return errors F_Error often < 6 and in some representative cases F_Error < 1, with optimal bitstrings still prominent in measured distributions.
- Random unoptimized reference states produced return errors F_Error ≈38 (used as baseline).
- Resource comparison claim: for same problem scale the authors estimate QAOA requires hundreds to thousands of circuit executions while QITE can be achieved with a single trained circuit evaluation (but with substantial classical training cost); example: a 9-qubit QAOA run required a few hundred iterations whereas the corresponding QITE circuit needed 4–8 variational layers and more classical optimization.

## Key ideas
- #idea:near-term-feasibility — QAOA converges in noiseless simulation and can be scaled via MPS to 20–30 qubits, but its performance degrades rapidly at realistic two-qubit gate error rates (examples: CX/ECR error ≈0.007), questioning NISQ applicability.
- #idea:near-term-feasibility — QITE displays greater robustness to realistic gate noise and was runnable on IBM 'Brisbane' hardware, but requires deeper circuits, ancilla embedding with post-selection, and substantially more classical optimization effort.
- #idea:hybrid-approach — Both approaches operate in a hybrid loop: classical preprocessing (returns/covariance → QUBO/Ising), binary encoding of allocations (w bits per asset), and classical optimizers (COBYLA, L-BFGS-B) tuning parameterised circuits.
- #idea:hybrid-approach — The explicit QUBO/Ising mapping and binary expansion encoding impose notable qubit/resource overheads and influence circuit depth and noise sensitivity.
## Contradictions
- QAOA's apparent scalability in noiseless simulations (20–30 qubits via MPS) does not translate to realistic noisy devices: at two-qubit error rates around 0.007 its portfolio-performance metrics collapse, contradicting optimistic claims that shallow QAOA is NISQ-ready for portfolio optimization.
- QITE's empirical robustness to noise is achieved by using deeper circuits, ancilla embedding and post-selection plus heavy classical overlap-minimisation; this contradicts any assertion that variational imaginary-time methods automatically provide practical near-term quantum advantage without substantial classical overhead.
- The paper's binary encoding/QUBO formulation increases qubit counts required for reasonable asset quantization, contradicting suggestions that small-qubit experiments directly demonstrate readiness for practical portfolio discretizations.
