## Abstract summary
The authors formulate mRNA secondary structure prediction as a quadratic binary optimization problem and solve it with a CVaR-based VQE on utility-scale gate-based quantum processors (IBM Eagle and Heron). They demonstrate runs for sequences up to 60 nucleotides (10–80 qubits) using a two-local ansatz and NFT classical optimizer, reporting that, with minimal error mitigation and fixed-depth circuits, hardware results match classical CPLEX solutions for minimum free energy structures.
## Methodology
The authors formulate RNA secondary-structure prediction with pseudoknots as a quadratic binary optimization over ‘‘quartet’’ variables (two consecutive base pairs). They convert the constrained quadratic program into a QUBO (adding penalty terms for crossing quartets) and use CPLEX as a classical baseline solver. For quantum experiments they map the QUBO to a problem Hamiltonian and solve it with a CVaR-modified Variational Quantum Eigensolver (CVaR-VQE). The variational ansatz is a hardware-efficient two-local circuit composed of single-qubit Y-rotations and native two-qubit entangling gates (ECR or CZ depending on device). The classical parameter optimizer is the NFT sequential minimal (three-evaluation) optimizer; CVaR (lower-α tail average) is used as the objective (α tuned between 0.1 and 0.25). They run noise-free MPS simulations (Qiskit MPS) for many random instances and then execute experiments on IBM utility-scale processors (ibm_brisbane, ibm_osaka, ibm_torino / Heron). Minimal error suppression/mitigation is applied (dynamical decoupling during idles, matrix-free measurement mitigation (M3) for readout), and circuits are transpiled with aggressive optimization. Performance is evaluated versus the CPLEX baseline using metrics including success probability (fraction of runs that sample the global optimum) and optimality gap (relative difference of best sampled energy vs CPLEX optimum). Multiple independent trials with random initial parameters are used to mitigate optimizer local minima.

**Algorithms used:** CVaR-VQE, Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE), NFT (Nakanishi-Fujii-Todo) optimizer, QUBO formulation / Ising Hamiltonian, CPLEX (classical integer/quadratic programming solver), Dynamical Decoupling (DD) for error suppression, Matrix-free Measurement Mitigation (M3)
**Frameworks:** Qiskit

**Experimental setup:** Simulations: Qiskit MPS simulator using a two-local ansatz (single-qubit Y-rotations + pairwise CZ/ECR layers), two ansatz layers for many sims, CVaR(α=0.1) objective in MPS runs. Hardware: IBM utility-scale processors (ibm_brisbane, ibm_osaka—ECR native two-qubit gate; ibm_torino Heron—CZ native gate). Qubit mappings chosen by per-layer coupling fidelity characterization; circuits transpiled at optimization level 3; dynamical decoupling applied during idles; M3 readout mitigation used. Problem sizes: simulations 10–40 logical qubits; hardware runs at 26, 40, 50 qubits and an 80-qubit run (parameter training done offline in simulator, executed on ibm_torino).

## Findings
- [supported] The authors formulated mRNA secondary structure prediction (including pseudoknot-capable models via quartet variables) as a QUBO and solved it with a CVaR-VQE variational hybrid algorithm on gate-based quantum processors.
- [supported] Noise-free MPS simulations of CVaR-VQE (two-local ansatz, p=2, CVaR α=0.1) for problems of 10–40 qubits produced high success probabilities (average ~0.4–1.0 across instances) and average optimality gaps under ~20%.
- [supported] Hardware experiments on IBM Eagle/Heron-family devices for 26-, 40-, and 50-qubit instances produced nontrivial results: success probabilities ≥ ~0.38 and average optimality gaps ≤ ~34% using minimal error mitigation (dynamical decoupling and matrix-free measurement mitigation) and fixed-depth circuits.
- [supported] An 80-qubit (42-nucleotide) run on ibm_torino (Heron) using circuit parameters optimized offline by MPS simulation sampled the optimal bitstring (matching the classical solver CPLEX) with measured probability ≈0.045.
- [supported] Classical solver CPLEX solved instances with up to 327 variables and ~38,946 constraints in this dataset, but worst-case running time (measured) grows rapidly (box-plot suggests exponential scaling) with sequence length.
- [supported] Using a CVaR objective (α < 1), NFT (sequential minimal) optimizer, and a hardware-efficient two-local ansatz produced robust convergence behavior that tolerated hardware noise in these experiments.
- [supported] The authors used low-overhead error suppression/mitigation (Dynamical Decoupling and M3 readout mitigation) and circuit transpilation optimizations (Qiskit level 3) to obtain reliable outputs on noisy hardware for the studied instances.
- [supported] The particular heavy-hex-layout circuits and shallow depths used remain classically simulatable (enabling offline parameter optimization via MPS), and the authors acknowledge this simulability.
- [speculative] The paper claims that utility-scale quantum processors enable exploration of relatively large problem sizes for RNA folding and motivates further development — a forward-looking claim about future practical impact.
- [speculative] The authors state ("to the best of our knowledge") that this is the first study to run RNA sequences up to 42 nucleotides on a gate-based quantum processor — a novelty claim that could be true but is presented as best-effort rather than proven exhaustive.
- [speculative] The paper notes that scalability beyond ~100 qubits and whether variational schemes remain practical at that scale remain open questions requiring further research.

**Results summary:** The authors demonstrate feasibility of formulating mRNA secondary structure prediction as a QUBO and solving it with CVaR-VQE on utility-scale, gate-based quantum hardware and simulators. Noise-free MPS simulations (10–40 qubits) yielded high success probabilities and low optimality gaps; hardware runs on IBM Eagle/Heron-family devices (26, 40, 50 qubits) produced the lowest-energy structures matching classical CPLEX results with moderate success probabilities (≥ ~0.38) and optimality gaps (≤ ~34%) using minimal error mitigation. An 80-qubit / 42-nucleotide instance run with offline-optimized parameters sampled the CPLEX-optimal bitstring with ~4.5% probability. The authors conclude that variational quantum algorithms can be executed end-to-end on current noisy hardware for these problem sizes, but the question of scalability beyond ~100 qubits remains open.

## Limitations
- Scalability to larger problem sizes (>100 qubits) remains an open question
- Performance declines as problem size increases (declining success probability and larger optimality gaps)
- Use of fixed-depth (shallow) circuits (constant p), which likely limits expressivity and solution quality
- Only minimal error mitigation was applied; more powerful schemes (e.g., ZNE, PEC) were not used
- The 80-qubit experiment used offline (classical) parameter optimization — the optimization step was not performed on-hardware for that instance
- MPS (matrix-product-state) simulations are limited by entanglement growth, restricting simulation to shallow-depth circuits
- Hardware experiments were limited in scope (e.g., typically one sequence per problem size on hardware), limiting statistical generality of hardware results
- Classical optimizers (e.g., NFT) can get stuck in local minima, requiring multiple independent trials to obtain good solutions
- The number of quantum circuit executions (Ncircuit) can be large (driven by 1/psucc, Niter, Nshots) and its scaling with problem size is uncertain
- [inferred] Heavy-hex lattice circuits at the employed depths are classically simulatable, so these experiments do not demonstrate a clear quantum advantage
- [inferred] The QUBO/formulation and experiments focused on a particular modeling choice (quartets, rewards/penalties); alternative formulations or more complex motifs (e.g., full treatment of pseudoknots or other biological constraints) were not fully explored and may be challenging
- [inferred] Limited exploration of ansatz design, optimizer choices, and objective variants beyond the specific two-local + NFT + CVaR(α) configuration used
## Open questions
- Can the variational quantum approach (CVaR-VQE with two-local ansatz and NFT) scale favorably to problem sizes substantially beyond those tested (e.g., >100 qubits)?
- Do the critical factors determining total circuit executions (1/psucc, Niter, Nshots) scale exponentially with the number of qubits, or can they be controlled algorithmically?
- To what extent will increasing ansatz depth (number of layers p) improve solution quality versus increasing noise/complexity in the hardware context?
- What is the optimal choice of the CVaR parameter α for different problem sizes and noise regimes, and can adaptive/advanced CVaR schemes yield systematic improvements?
- How much can more advanced error mitigation techniques (e.g., zero-noise extrapolation, probabilistic error cancellation) improve results for these problems when combined with CVaR objectives?
- Can improved mapping/qubit-selection and calibration strategies materially improve success probabilities on real devices for larger problems?
- Can quantum algorithms meaningfully outperform classical solvers on RNA secondary structure prediction in regimes beyond classical simulatability (i.e., demonstrate practical quantum advantage)?
- How well do the chosen QUBO formulation and the quartet-variable representation generalize to sequences with biologically important features such as pseudoknots or to much longer mRNA sequences?
- What are the trade-offs between doing full on-hardware parameter optimization versus offline/classical parameter optimization (re: scalability, fidelity, and practicality)?

**Future work:**
- Investigate increasing the number of layers in the ansatz to improve performance
- Optimize the choice of the CVaR parameter α (and possibly use adaptive α strategies)
- Incorporate and test other recent advancements in CVaR-based objectives
- Explore more advanced optimization techniques and algorithmic variants beyond NFT and two-local ansatz
- Study the interplay of CVaR objectives with noise and error mitigation (including examining bounds and robustness in noisy regimes)
- Examine improved error mitigation strategies and their impact on variational optimization performance
- Continue research into scaling behavior and feasibility for larger problem instances and different hardware topologies
