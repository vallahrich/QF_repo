## Abstract summary
This work extends Montanaro's quantum Monte Carlo methods to estimate expectations of heavy-tailed random variables that may have infinite variance but a finite (1+δ)-th moment (0<δ≤1). The authors give a quantum algorithm achieving ε-mean absolute error with sample complexity scaling as O(C^{1/2δ} ε^{-(1+δ)/2δ}), yielding a quadratic speedup over classical Monte Carlo in both ε and the moment parameter, and prove matching classical and quantum lower bounds to show near-optimality; they also analyze modeling assumptions and a hierarchy of available prior information.
## Methodology
This is a theoretical / algorithmic study that extends existing quantum Monte Carlo mean-estimation techniques to random variables with infinite variance but finite (1+δ)-moment (0 < δ ≤ 1). The authors work in an oracle/code model: a classical or quantum ‘code’ A produces samples v(A) and is promoted to a quantum operator following the Kothari–O'Donnell (KO23) construction (including the phase/mean-estimation operator e^{-2 i arctan v(A)}). The method is to (1) assume an a priori known upper bound C on E[|v(A)|^{1+δ}], (2) pick a truncation/cutoff y = (8 C / ε)^{1/δ} to split the distribution into truncated (light-tailed) and tail parts, (3) apply the KO23 / Montanaro-style quantum mean estimation to the truncated variable (using amplitude/phase estimation tools) to obtain an additive ε estimate, and (4) bound the tail contribution via Hölder and moment inequalities. The paper proves sample-complexity upper bounds O(C^{1/(2δ)} ε^{-(1+δ)/(2δ)}) (hidden constants depend on δ) and converts high-probability guarantees to mean-absolute-error (MAE) guarantees via clipping and a powering/median-of-repetitions technique. For lower bounds it reduces Grover-type search/classification problems to the mean-estimation task, establishing corresponding Ω(C^{1/δ} ε^{-(1+δ)/δ}) classical and Ω(C^{1/(2δ)} ε^{-(1+δ)/(2δ)}) quantum sample lower bounds. Theoretical tools used include the Marcinkiewicz–Zygmund generalized law of large numbers, Hölder's inequality, reductions to query complexity (Grover lower bounds), and an analysis of Pareto examples to illustrate parameter regimes. No empirical or hardware experiments are performed; all results are complexity proofs and constructive algorithm descriptions with proofs of correctness and optimality up to constants/log-factors.

**Algorithms used:** Montanaro quantum Monte Carlo (quantum mean estimation), Kothari–O'Donnell mean-estimation operator / algorithm (uses e^{-2 i arctan v(A)}), Quantum amplitude / phase estimation (as used in Montanaro/KO23), Grover's search (used in lower-bound reductions), Classical Marcinkiewicz–Zygmund generalized law of large numbers (analytical tool)

**Results summary:** This theoretical preprint extends quantum Monte Carlo mean estimation to random variables with infinite variance but finite (1+δ)-moment (0<δ≤1). By truncating the heavy tail at a threshold computed from a known upper bound C on E[|X|^{1+δ}] and invoking the Kothari–O'Donnell quantum mean-estimation routine on the truncated variable, the authors obtain an additive-ε estimator using O(C^{1/(2δ)} ε^{-(1+δ)/(2δ)}) quantum samples. They give matching lower bounds (classical Ω(C^{1/δ} ε^{-(1+δ)/δ}), quantum Ω(C^{1/(2δ)} ε^{-(1+δ)/(2δ)})), show how to obtain MAE guarantees with an extra logarithmic factor, discuss extensions to central moments, and study implications for common heavy-tailed families (e.g., Pareto). All claims are theoretical (analytical) and supported by proofs and reductions rather than empirical experiments.

**Performance claims:**
- Quantum upper bound (Theorem 1): sample complexity O(C^{1/(2δ)} ε^{-(1+δ)/(2δ)}) for additive-ε mean estimation when E[|X|^{1+δ}] ≤ C and 0<δ≤1.
- MAE guarantee (Theorem 4): an ε-MAE estimator can be produced with sample complexity O(log(C/ε) · C^{1/(2δ)} ε^{-(1+δ)/(2δ)}) (log factor hides δ-dependence).
- Classical lower bound (Theorem 6): any classical sampler needs Ω(C^{1/δ} ε^{-(1+δ)/δ}) samples (worst-case) to achieve additive-ε accuracy with constant success probability.
- Quantum lower bound (Theorem 6): any quantum sampler needs Ω(C^{1/(2δ)} ε^{-(1+δ)/(2δ)}) samples (worst-case) to achieve additive-ε accuracy with constant success probability.
- For the Pareto(α) case discussed, the paper derives specific sample-scaling exponents comparing the proposed algorithm, an 'ideal' interval-decomposition algorithm, and direct KO23 application with different truncations (Section 7.1).
## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** theoretical

The paper proves a theoretical quadratic speed-up in sample complexity over classical sampling for mean estimation of heavy-tailed variables with finite (1+δ)-moment: the quantum upper bound and the quantum lower bound match (up to constants/log factors), establishing the advantage analytically rather than empirically.
## Limitations
- The algorithm requires as input a known upper bound C on the (1+δ)-th moment C1+δ = E[|v(A)|1+δ]; Theorem 1 cannot hold with any finite bound on sample size if C is not provided.
- The sample complexity and the algorithm parameters depend explicitly on δ (0 < δ ≤ 1); hidden factors in the O(·) bounds themselves also depend on δ.
- The method relies on truncation of the random variable (choice of a cutoff y) — handling genuinely variable-time or truly unbounded classical code A requires care and/or truncation.
- The model assumes access to the 'code' for the sampler and to specific quantum primitives (Synthesizer and controlled-X, controlled-X†). The results depend on that access model and may not directly apply when only black-box sampling (without code) is available.
- The algorithm is not a new primitive but rather an analysis and parameter-setting wrapper around existing quantum Monte Carlo (notably KO23); performance therefore inherits limitations of those black-box methods.
- Guarantees are primarily for additive-error (ǫ) MAE and high-probability bounds; converting between forms of guarantees incurs additional logarithmic factors (e.g., powering/median-of-repeats to boost success probability and the MAE bound).
- Lower- and upper-bound results are asymptotic and tight up to constants and logarithmic factors; the paper does not give exact constant factors or an empirical assessment of constant/log factors that matter in practice.
- Performance can be suboptimal if alternative prior moment information is available (e.g., knowing the second moment can lead to smaller sample complexity than relying only on C1+δ).
- [inferred] The approach assumes correct specification of the given moment bound C; robustness to misspecification or estimation error in C is not analyzed.
- [inferred] Practical implementation overheads (quantum circuit depth, error, required precision of arithmetic, costs of constructing the Synthesizer and controlled unitaries) are not treated.
- [inferred] The results assume the availability of a quantum-adapted version of the code A and its inverse in a particular restricted form; transforming arbitrary classical or randomized samplers into this quantum-accessible form may be nontrivial or incur hidden costs.
- [inferred] Theoretical treatment focuses on single-dimensional mean estimation; extensions to multivariate expectations or more complex financial risk measures are not provided.
## Open questions
- Can one design a δ-independent algorithm (i.e., whose parameters/sample complexity do not require knowing δ) that matches the lower bounds across δ, or otherwise adaptively attains near-optimal complexity for unknown δ?
- Is it possible to remove the requirement that an upper bound C on the (1+δ)-th moment be known a priori, and still achieve comparable quantum speedups (perhaps by estimating C adaptively)?
- How should different pieces of prior information (bounds on different moments, knowledge of tail probabilities q_i or a cutoff y, or a function C(δ)) be optimally aggregated to minimize sample complexity?
- Can the prior-information paradigm be generalized beyond knowledge of a single moment (1+δ) to richer prior descriptions of the tail (e.g., direct knowledge of the q_i sequence described in the paper), and can practical algorithms be built that exploit such information?
- How to quantize and efficiently handle variable-time classical sampling procedures A (those whose runtime depends on the sample), in particular when the classical sampler represents extreme heavy tails?
- What is the robustness of the quantum estimator to mis-specification of C or δ in practice (how sensitive is sample complexity / accuracy to errors in the provided moment bound)?
- Can the hidden factors and δ-dependence in the asymptotic bounds be tightened or explicitly computed, and how do these constants affect practical performance for realistic problem sizes?
- How do implementation-level quantum costs (gate counts, coherence time, noise) affect the theoretical sample-complexity benefits when applied to concrete financial models with heavy tails such as Pareto-like returns?
- Can the approach be extended to other error metrics or objective functions important in finance (e.g., relative error, tail quantiles, expected shortfall) with comparable quantum advantages?
- Are there adaptive or online algorithms that can estimate or refine cutoff y or relevant tail probabilities during execution to improve efficiency compared with fixing y from the given moment bound?

**Future work:**

- Study procedures that remove or relax the need for an a priori upper bound C on the (1+δ)-th moment—e.g., by adaptively estimating moment bounds or using robust estimators.

- Extend the framework to handle variable-time classical samplers A in a principled way, including implementation strategies for their quantization.
