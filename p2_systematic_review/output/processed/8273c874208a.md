## Abstract summary
The paper introduces an alternative Quantum Accelerated Monte Carlo pipeline for derivative pricing that combines a new 'direct encoding' protocol—capable of loading payoff sign information into quantum states—with a modified Real Quantum Amplitude Estimation (mRQAE) algorithm that recovers signed amplitudes. This approach avoids splitting payoffs into positive and negative parts, is experimentally compared to standard QAMC on an emulator, and is shown to retain the expected quantum speedups while discussing practical implementation challenges and future improvements.
## Methodology
The authors propose and experimentally evaluate an alternative Quantum Accelerated Monte Carlo (QAMC) pipeline for option pricing that combines a new state preparation/encoding method ("direct encoding") with a sign-capable amplitude estimation routine (the modified Real Quantum Amplitude Estimation, mRQAE). Experiments are performed in a simulated quantum environment: the underlying asset terminal distribution is sampled exactly under a Black–Scholes model (closed-form sampling of ST) and discretized into a finite grid; two encodings are compared (the conventional square-root encoding that loads sqrt(payoff) into an ancilla amplitude and the proposed direct encoding that loads the payoff amplitudes directly and applies U_S^• to recover an amplitude proportional to the signed expectation). The mRQAE is used to read the sign and magnitude of the expected payoff directly, avoiding the need to split payoffs into positive and negative parts as in previous approaches; the modified iterative QAE (mIQAE) is used as a baseline amplitude-estimation algorithm (and standard QAMC with square-root encoding and separation of positive/negative parts is used as a comparative baseline). Experiments measure absolute error between the QAMC estimate and a discretized (classical) expectation as a function of number of oracle calls (N_Q) and target precision epsilon; medians and 25/75 percentiles over repeated runs are reported. The implementation uses the Atos QLM / myQLM software stack with a custom Python library (NEASQC FinancialApplications).

**Algorithms used:** Quantum Accelerated Monte Carlo (QAMC), Direct encoding (proposed), Square-root encoding (standard), Modified Real Quantum Amplitude Estimation (mRQAE), Modified Iterative Quantum Amplitude Estimation (mIQAE), Real Quantum Amplitude Estimation (RQAE) (discussed), Grover amplitude amplification (used internally)
**Frameworks:** Atos QLM (QLM-1.9.1 / myQLM-1.9.9), Python (3.9.x), NEASQC: FinancialApplications (authors' Python library on GitHub)

**Experimental setup:** All experiments were run on classical HPC nodes (quantum emulator/simulator) hosted at the Galicia Supercomputing Center (CESGA) on the Finisterrae III infrastructure. Software: QLM / myQLM and the authors' NEASQC FinancialApplications code. No physical quantum processor (QPU) was used; experiments are emulator-based.

**Dataset:** Synthetic financial data: simulated terminal asset-price distributions sampled exactly from the Black–Scholes model (risk-neutral measure) and discretized onto uniform grids. No external market dataset was used.
## Experiment details
### Input
Source: synthetic simulation from Black–Scholes closed-form sampling ST = S_t * exp((r - 0.5 sigma^2) (T - t) + sigma * Z * sqrt(T - t)). Sizes / discretization: typical experiments used 32 discretization points of ST in range [0.01, 5.0] (requiring 5 qubits) for single-time-step payoffs; cliquet option experiment used 32x32 grid = 1024 paths (requiring 10 qubits). Parameters: risk-free rate r (examples: 0.01 or 0.05), volatility sigma (examples: 0.5), initial S0 = 1.0, maturity T (1 year for many experiments; cliquet used reset at 1 year and maturity 2 years). Payoff types: vanilla call/put, digital call/put, linear payoff F(x)=x-K, and a path-dependent cliquet option with caps/floors (c,f, cg, fg). Strikes: experiments include K values such as 1.0 and an example with K = 1.5 (as reported in figures). Preprocessing: discrete grid construction of ST probability mass and mapping to computational basis amplitudes; construction of UBS (Black–Scholes sampler) and payoff oracle UF or U_sqrt_F depending on encoding.

### Process
Pipeline: (1) prepare discrete probability amplitude state |S> implementing the Black–Scholes discretized distribution (oracle US / UBS); (2) for square-root encoding: apply U_sqrt_F to load sqrt(payoff / norm) into an ancilla amplitude and measure P(|0>) of that ancilla (classical postprocessing rescales by normalization); for direct encoding: apply UF to load payoff amplitudes into an ancilla then apply U_S^† to bring the global amplitude proportional to the signed expectation into the |0> basis state; (3) run amplitude estimation: either mRQAE (on the direct-encoding oracle U_DE = U_S^† U_F U_S) to obtain signed amplitude or mIQAE (iterative QAE) on the square-root encoding (with splitting positive/negative parts when needed) as baseline; (4) vary number of oracle calls N_Q / target precision epsilon and collect estimates; (5) repeat experiments to produce medians and 25/75 percentiles and plot absolute error vs N_Q for different epsilon. Key parameters: discretization size (32 or 1024 paths), number of qubits (5 or 10), Black–Scholes params (r, sigma), target precision eps, and amplitude-estimation configuration (mRQAE uses shift b strategy, amplification policy parameter q, per-iteration shots Ni derived from Hoeffding bound; mIQAE uses its standard iterative settings). Iterations: amplitude-estimation internal iterations until desired epsilon reached; number of Grover amplifications per iteration chosen per algorithm policy (mRQAE details described in Appendix B).

### Output
Outputs: estimated expected payoff values (quantum estimates) and corresponding absolute error relative to the discretized classical expectation. Primary metrics: absolute error vs number of oracle calls N_Q, reported medians and 25th/75th percentiles across runs; comparisons (baselines): standard square-root encoding + mIQAE (with positive/negative splitting for negative-payoff cases) versus proposed direct encoding + mRQAE (no splitting needed). Plots and numeric results show convergence behavior and N_Q scaling for multiple payoff types including cases where the expected value can be negative.

## Findings
- [supported] The paper introduces a 'direct encoding' that loads (non-squared) payoff values into amplitudes and preserves information about the sign of the expected payoff; this encoding was implemented and validated on a quantum emulator.
- [supported] The paper introduces a modified real quantum amplitude estimation (mRQAE) algorithm able to recover the sign of the target amplitude, and demonstrates empirically (on an emulator) that mRQAE is competitive with state-of-the-art iterative AE (mIQAE) for the tested option-pricing examples.
- [speculative] The authors present theoretical complexity bounds for mRQAE and claim it is an asymptotically optimal variant of RQAE, with a provable upper bound of the form N_Q = O(1/ϵ · log(1/γ)) (more precisely N_Q < C1(q) · 1/ϵ · log(C2(q)/γ) for constants C1,C2 depending on an amplification policy parameter q).
- [supported] Combining direct encoding with mRQAE yields a QAMC pipeline that avoids splitting payoffs into positive/negative parts and, on the emulator, recovers correct option prices (including negative expectations) without the separate-decomposition workaround required by the standard square-root encoding.
- [supported] Empirical comparisons on a quantum emulator across multiple payoff types (vanilla call/put, digital options, a linear payoff example, and a cliquet path-dependent option) show that the direct-encoding + mRQAE pipeline produces error-versus-oracle-call behaviour competitive with the standard square-root encoding plus mIQAE (measured under the simulator and the imposed simplifications).
- [speculative] The paper argues that, in theory, QAMC provides a quadratic asymptotic improvement in sampling error over classical Monte Carlo (CMC) (QAMC: O(1/N_Q) vs CMC: O(1/sqrt(N_C))); the authors rely on known amplitude-estimation results and extend them in their analysis.
- [supported] The authors clearly state practical limitations: a realistic, end-to-end implementation requires many logical qubits and deep Grover-like circuits which are infeasible on current NISQ hardware; experiments therefore used substantial simplifications (exact Black–Scholes one-step sampling and coarse discretizations) and were run on simulators.
- [speculative] The authors propose future research directions (efficient approximate state preparation, alternative encodings to reduce truncation/normalization error, and exploration/optimization of mRQAE parameters) as plausible ways to make the pipeline practical on hardware — these are forward-looking suggestions, not empirically demonstrated here.

**Results summary:** The paper proposes an alternative QAMC pipeline combining a 'direct encoding' (which loads payoffs directly into amplitudes and preserves sign information) with a modified real quantum amplitude estimation algorithm (mRQAE) that can read amplitude sign. On a quantum emulator (with simplifying assumptions: exact Black–Scholes one-step sampling, small discretizations), the authors show the direct-encoding + mRQAE approach recovers correct option prices including negative expectations and is competitive with the established square-root encoding + mIQAE approach in terms of error versus number of oracle calls. They provide theoretical analysis for mRQAE (complexity upper bounds) and discuss practical hardware limitations and future work required to make a full end-to-end implementation feasible on real devices.

**Performance claims:**
- CMC statistical error scales as O(1/sqrt(N_C)) (classical Monte Carlo standard result).
- QAMC sampling error scales as O(1/N_Q) (amplitude-estimation theoretical result; exploited by the pipeline).
- mRQAE theoretical upper bound (paper): N_Q < C1(q) · (1/ϵ) · log(C2(q)/γ) for constants C1(q), C2(q) depending on amplification policy parameter q (given in Appendix B).
- mIQAE referenced empirical bound in literature (Table 2): N_mIQAE < 123 · (1/ϵ) · log(6/γ) (cited from [20] / literature table).
- Example discretizations used in emulator experiments: single-step Black–Scholes encoded with 32 discretization points (range 0.01–5.0) ⇒ 5 qubits; cliquet example used 32×32 (1024) paths ⇒ 10 qubits.
- Experiments show comparable absolute error versus oracle calls between direct encoding+mRQAE and square-root+mIQAE across tested payoffs (no single-number speedup factor reported; performance shown in plots as medians and quartiles).
## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** theoretical

The paper demonstrates (theoretically and in simulator experiments under simplifying assumptions) that the QAMC paradigm retains its asymptotic quadratic advantage in sampling error (O(1/N_Q) vs classical O(1/sqrt(N))). The authors validate the direct-encoding + mRQAE pipeline on emulators and provide complexity bounds for mRQAE, but they do not demonstrate a practical advantage on current quantum hardware; they explicitly note that end-to-end implementation is infeasible today due to qubit count and circuit depth constraints, so the advantage remains theoretical/ simulator-validated rather than experimentally realized on real devices.
## Limitations
- Implementation of the oracle US (state preparation for the path distribution) requires an excessively large number of qubits (author-stated).
- Depths required by Grover-like routines (amplitude amplification) are not feasible under current decoherence times (author-stated).
- Total number of gates when combining oracle US and Grover-like algorithms demands gate error rates beyond current technology (author-stated).
- Experiments rely on several simplifying assumptions (exact one-step simulation via Black–Scholes solution, use of a UBS oracle to encode the target distribution) rather than a fully general end-to-end QAMC pipeline (author-stated).
- Direct and square-root encodings require knowledge of a normalization bound for the payoff (or truncation), which introduces truncation/normalization error (author-stated).
- The mRQAE parameter choices presented are not unique; optimal parameter selection (e.g., shot allocation, amplification policy q) is unresolved (author-stated).

## Open questions
- How can states that approximately encode complex target probability distributions be prepared efficiently with dramatically fewer qubits and gates (practical, high-fidelity state initialization)?
- What alternative encodings can avoid or reduce the need for payoff normalization/truncation and thereby reduce truncation error?
- What are the optimal choices/tuning strategies for mRQAE parameters (amplification policy q, per-iteration shots Ni, per-iteration confidence γi) to minimize total cost (shots, oracle calls, depth) in practical regimes?
- How will the proposed direct encoding + mRQAE pipeline perform on real, noisy quantum hardware (NISQ devices) compared with emulator results?
- How to extend the pipeline to realistic multi-step SDE discretizations and path-dependent payoffs without prohibitive resource requirements?
- What are concrete resource estimates (logical qubits, physical qubits with error correction, total gate counts, depths) for end-to-end implementations achieving useful precisions in finance use cases?
- How to efficiently implement shifted oracles Ab (required by mRQAE) for a broad class of payoffs and models in practice?
- How sensitive is the combined error (discretization + Euler/EM + quantum estimation + truncation) to choices in discretization and encoding, and how to optimally balance these sources of error?
- Can further algorithmic improvements (beyond mRQAE) reduce circuit depth or shot complexity while retaining sign recovery and asymptotic optimality?
- How does the approach scale with dimension (multi-asset derivatives) in terms of qubit count and circuit complexity, and are there practical dimension-reduction strategies?

**Future work:**
- Develop new methods to efficiently initialize quantum states that approximately encode target probability distributions with far fewer qubits and gates (state-preparation research).
- Investigate alternative payoff encodings to reduce or eliminate the need for payoff normalization/truncation and mitigate associated errors.
- Explore different parameter settings and optimizations for mRQAE (e.g., shot allocation, amplification policy q) to improve practical efficiency (reduce total shots and oracle calls).
- Extend and test the pipeline beyond the Black–Scholes exact-simulation setting toward multi-step discretizations and more realistic SDE models.
- Study implementation strategies and resource estimates for end-to-end QAMC on realistic hardware (including error correction overheads) to assess practical feasibility.
