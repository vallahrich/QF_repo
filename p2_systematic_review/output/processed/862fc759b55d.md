## Abstract summary
The paper introduces a 'residual sampling' strategy for Variational Quantum Monte Carlo that modifies the sampling weight to remove problematic singularities and reestablish the Central Limit Theorem in its strongest form for estimates of the total energy and the residual variance. The author provides theoretical analysis of the statistical properties, derives confidence intervals, and demonstrates the approach numerically (all-electron carbon atom), arguing the method generalizes to other operators and improves control over random error compared with standard sampling.
## Methodology
The paper develops and analyses a modified sampling strategy for Variational Monte Carlo (VMC) called "residual sampling". Analytically, the author formulates expectation values as quotients of weighted sums over sampled local field variables (local energy EL and related quantities), introduces a weight function w(E) = ε^2/((E - E0)^2 + ε^2), and proves existence of required co-moments so that the bivariate (and trivariate where needed) Central Limit Theorem (CLT) applies. Theoretical derivations include bounding asymptotic tails, demonstration that residual sampling removes problematic power-law tails caused by nodal-surface singularities, and use of Fieller's theorem to obtain confidence intervals for quotients of correlated random variables. Numerically, the author generates a multiconfigurational trial wavefunction (48 determinants from MCHF via ATSP2K), augments it with an 83-parameter Jastrow factor and a 130-parameter backflow transformation (219 total parameters), optimises it via variance minimisation, and runs VMC Metropolis sampling using both standard (P ∝ ψ^2) and residual sampling (P ∝ ψ^2 / w(EL)). Numerical analyses include large-sample experiments (up to 10^6 local-energy samples), block-averaging, estimation of bivariate means and covariance matrices, construction of Fieller confidence wedges, kernel density estimation (top-hat kernel) of resulting estimator distributions, and comparison of statistical error scaling and outlier behaviour between sampling schemes. Key reported outputs are total energy and residual variance estimates with confidence intervals and demonstrated scaling improvements (e.g., r^{-1/2} for residual sampling vs r^{-1/3} for standard sampling for some quantities).

**Algorithms used:** Variational Monte Carlo (VMC), Metropolis algorithm (for sampling), Multi-Configuration Hartree-Fock (MCHF) for trial wavefunction generation, Jastrow factor and backflow transformation (wavefunction correlation models), Variance minimisation (wavefunction optimisation), Fieller's theorem (for confidence intervals of ratios), Kernel density estimation (top-hat kernel)
**Frameworks:** ATSP2K (for MCHF wavefunction generation), CASINO (referenced as QMC code; user guide cited)

## Findings
- [supported] The commonly used 'standard sampling' implementation of continuum variational quantum Monte Carlo (VMC) can produce uncontrolled random errors: the Central Limit Theorem (CLT) is only weakly valid for total energy estimates and fails for residual variance estimates, leading to leptokurtotic power-law tails and outliers.
- [supported] A new 'residual sampling' strategy using weights w(E) = ε^2 / ((E − E0)^2 + ε^2) is proposed; it bounds sampled quantities (removes singular behaviour at the nodal surface) and reintroduces the CLT in its strongest form for total energy and residual variance estimates.
- [supported] For total-energy estimates under residual sampling, the numerator and denominator form a bivariate random variable; the bivariate CLT holds, Fieller's theorem can be used to obtain valid confidence intervals, and the statistical width scales as r^(-1/2).
- [supported] For residual-variance estimates residual sampling likewise restores the (bivariate) CLT and yields controlled confidence intervals; in contrast standard sampling lacks a valid CLT for the residual variance.
- [supported] Numerical experiments on an all-electron carbon atom (multi-determinant trial wavefunction with Jastrow and backflow) demonstrate the expected statistical improvements of residual sampling and validate the theoretical analysis.
- [supported] Residual sampling meaningfully reduces statistical error for practical examples: for the presented carbon-atom example the residual-sampling total-energy confidence interval at optimal parameters is ≈75% of the standard-sampling interval (i.e. fewer samples needed), and residual sampling can substantially reduce errors for variance/kinetic-energy estimates.
- [supported] For kinetic-energy estimation, using Green's theorem to reformulate the estimator combined with residual sampling removes the dominant singularities; empirical results for the carbon atom show residual-sampling kinetic energy EKE = 37.894(17) a.u. vs standard-sampling 37.879(48) a.u., and the authors report standard sampling needs ≈8× as many samples to reach the same accuracy in this test.
- [supported] Residual sampling samples the region near the nodal surface (unlike standard sampling) which is important for controlling errors relevant to subsequent Diffusion Monte Carlo (DMC) fixed-node biases.
- [supported] Residual sampling entails additional computational cost and complexity because the local energy must be evaluated at candidate points to generate the sampling weights; this is a trade-off between computational overhead and improved statistical control.
- [supported] A general analysis shows that the asymptotic power-law behaviour of the standard seed distribution P_ψ^2 limits which moments can be robustly estimated; residual sampling (with appropriate decay exponent p) sets an upper bound on reliably estimable moments and prevents persistent power-law tails when conditions are met.
- [speculative] The authors suggest residual sampling (or similar controlled-weight samplers) could reduce the number of samples needed for wavefunction optimization and avoid ad hoc stabilisation schemes in optimisation—leading to more reliable optimisation near nodal surfaces and potentially reducing DMC fixed-node errors.
- [speculative] The paper proposes that the residual-sampling idea can be generalized to other operators and estimators by choosing weights as functions of the corresponding local-field variable to ensure bounded summands and restore strong CLT behaviour.

**Results summary:** This preprint identifies a statistical deficiency in the common 'standard sampling' approach to variational quantum Monte Carlo: singularities in sampled local quantities (notably at the nodal surface) cause heavy tails and the breakdown (partial or full) of the Central Limit Theorem for key estimates such as total energy and residual variance. The author introduces 'residual sampling', a weighted sampling strategy w(E) = ε^2/((E−E0)^2+ε^2), and demonstrates both analytically and with VMC calculations on an all-electron carbon atom that this approach bounds sampled values, re-establishes the bivariate CLT for quotients of sample means, and yields controlled confidence intervals for total energy, residual variance, and kinetic energy estimates. Residual sampling reduces statistical error for the presented examples (modest improvement for total energy, substantial improvement for variance/kinetic energy), at the cost of extra computational work to evaluate local energies for sampling weights. The method is presented as generalisable to other operators and potentially useful for more robust wavefunction optimisation and control of fixed-node DMC errors.

**Performance claims:**
- [supported] For the carbon-atom example, residual sampling yields a total-energy confidence interval ≈75% of the standard-sampling interval (i.e., roughly 0.75× the error) at optimal (E0, ε).
- [supported] Residual sampling 'requires approximately half as many samples as standard sampling' to reach a given accuracy for the total-energy estimate in the demonstrated example (statement in text; borne out by examples).
- [supported] For residual-variance estimates with r = 10^3, the authors report that standard sampling error is 1–2 orders of magnitude larger than residual sampling for their carbon-atom example, and that the ratio of errors grows with sample size approximately as r^(1/6).
- [supported] Kinetic energy example (carbon atom): residual sampling (with a Green's-theorem estimator) gives EKE = 37.894(17) a.u.; standard sampling gives EKE = 37.879(48) a.u.; authors state standard sampling requires ≈8× as many samples to achieve the same accuracy in this test.

## Limitations
- Residual sampling increases computational expense because the local energy must be evaluated to generate sample points with the required distribution.
- Interpretation of random error is more complicated: estimates must be considered as a quotient of two correlated random variables rather than a single random variable.
- Residual sampling requires choosing parameters (E0, ε); performance depends on these and they may need tuning (though the paper finds the method resilient to modest inaccuracies).
- Residual sampling only removes nodal-surface (type 3) singularities; type 1 and type 2 singularities (electron-nucleus and electron-electron cusps) remain and can still limit error behaviour for some estimates.
- The analysis is limited to expectation values that can be expressed in terms of the local energy field variable; applicability to other forms may require reformulation.
- Numerical validation is limited: tests are presented for a single example system (an isolated all-electron carbon atom) and may not represent performance in larger or more complex systems.
- Theoretical quotient-distribution results indicate the limiting distribution may lack a mean/variance (power-law tails) in the asymptotic limit; the paper argues finite-sample effects remove these tails in practice, but this remains a potential risk in other parameter regimes or systems.
- The analysis does not incorporate the uncertainty in estimates of Etot (used as EG) when computing residual variance confidence intervals; the random variation in EG is not included in the error analysis.
- [inferred] Residual sampling could have unfavorable scaling with system size (N) because per-step evaluations and weight computations may be more costly for many-electron systems.
- [inferred] Choosing and optimising (E0, ε) for arbitrary systems introduces extra implementation and computational overhead and may be nontrivial for practitioners.
- [inferred] The approach is developed and analysed for VMC; direct applicability to other QMC variants (e.g., Diffusion Monte Carlo) is suggested but not demonstrated.
## Open questions
- What are optimal strategies (algorithms) for choosing or adapting the residual-sampling parameters (E0, ε) automatically in general calculations?
- How well does residual sampling perform on a wider range of systems (larger atoms, molecules, solids, heavy elements) and wavefunction forms beyond the single tested carbon atom example?
- To what extent do the theoretical asymptotic pathologies (e.g., quotient distributions without mean/variance) manifest in other systems, parameter regimes, or with different trial wavefunctions?
- How can residual sampling be combined effectively with zero-variance, zero-bias corrections (Assaraf–Caffarel) to transfer or remove remaining singularities for difficult estimators?
- How does residual sampling affect downstream methods that depend on VMC outputs, e.g., fixed-node error in Diffusion Monte Carlo, when the trial wavefunction is optimised near the nodal surface?
- What are the practical trade-offs between increased per-sample cost and reduced number of required samples across different observables and systems?
- Can the residual sampling idea be generalized and implemented efficiently for a broader set of operators and for QMC variants beyond VMC?
- [inferred] What is the computational scaling (cost vs. system size) of residual sampling in practice, and are there techniques to mitigate any unfavorable scaling?
- [inferred] How sensitive are confidence intervals and error estimates to using estimated Etot (with its own uncertainty) as EG in residual-variance calculations, especially in automated optimisation workflows?

**Future work:**
- Generalise the residual sampling approach to expectation values of other operators and to other variants of the Quantum Monte Carlo method.
- Apply residual sampling to trial-wavefunction optimisation to reduce required sample numbers and to preferentially optimise the region near the nodal surface.
- Design and investigate alternative sampling strategies / estimator combinations to ensure CLT validity for specific physical quantities of interest.
- Investigate using zero-variance, zero-bias corrections together with residual sampling to handle or transfer remaining singularities.
- Optimise the sampling parameters (E0, ε), including exploring parameter choices that minimise conﬁdence intervals or otherwise improve practical performance.
- Use residual sampling to help control the fixed-node error in Diffusion Monte Carlo by improving trial wavefunction accuracy near nodal surfaces.
- Construct and analyse more general sampling strategies where weights are functions of other local quantities (Gn) to bound summands and ensure existence of co-moments for challenging estimators.
- [inferred] Validate and benchmark residual sampling on a broader set of systems and observables to characterise its practical benefits and costs across realistic computational workloads.
