## Abstract summary
This survey reviews implementations of quantum machine learning (QML) on real near-term quantum hardware, focusing on supervised and unsupervised techniques applied to real-world domains such as high-energy physics, healthcare, and finance. It analyzes practical limitations (data encoding, ansatz design, error mitigation, gradient methods), compares QML performance to classical approaches, and discusses current bottlenecks with suggested directions to improve applicability on noisy intermediate-scale quantum devices.
## Methodology
This work is a systematic survey of Quantum Machine Learning (QML) studies that implement supervised and unsupervised techniques on real (and in some cases simulators of) quantum hardware, with emphasis on applications relevant to real-world domains (High Energy Physics, Healthcare, Finance). The authors collected papers (via arXiv queries and literature search) using search terms such as "quantum machine learning", sampled approximately 1,000 papers from arXiv 'quant-ph', and filtered for works that ran models on quantum processing units (QPU) or realistic hardware-aware simulators. They grouped the selected papers by application domain and by method (e.g., VQC, quantum kernels, QGANs, qPCA, tensor-network approaches), and extracted method-level metadata into summary tables (qubit counts, hardware type, whether training was on QPU or simulator, and method used). The survey analyzes encoding techniques, ansatz choices, optimization strategies (optimizers, gradient rules, natural gradient variants), error mitigation approaches, and practical limitations (e.g., data-loading, decoherence, shot noise, long runtimes). The authors compared reported QML performances against classical baselines where available, discussed bottlenecks for real-hardware deployment, and proposed directions for future research. Quantum chemistry was intentionally excluded; the focus was on gate-based architectures.

**Algorithms used:** Variational Quantum Circuits (VQC) / Parameterized Quantum Circuits (PQC), Quantum Kernel Estimation / Quantum Support Vector Machine (QSVM), Quantum Generative Adversarial Networks (QGAN), Quantum Principal Component Analysis (qPCA), Quantum Tensor Networks (QTN), Quantum Neural Networks (QNN), Quantum Reservoir Computing (QRC), Quantum Federated Learning (QFL), Quantum Autoencoder (QAE), Quantum orthogonal neural networks, Swap-test classifier
**Frameworks:** IBM Quantum / Qiskit (IBM cloud backends referenced in surveyed works), PennyLane (used in some simulator experiments), scikit-learn (classical baselines and preprocessing referenced)

**Experimental setup:** Surveyed experiments were performed on gate-based quantum hardware (primarily superconducting QPUs and some ion-trap devices) and hardware-aware simulators. In the finance-related works summarized, reported QPU experiments ranged from small qubit counts (3–4 qubits) up to ~20 qubits; some studies used simulators for deeper or larger experiments. Typical practical details noted across the surveyed literature include use of shallow, hardware-efficient ansatzes or short-depth feature maps to match device coherence limits, use of many measurement shots for kernel estimation (examples up to 8192 shots in related domains), and long wall-clock runtimes for training on cloud QPUs (examples of multi-day/100+ hour runs reported in the survey).

**Dataset:** Across finance-focused studies surveyed, datasets varied: historical interest-rate forward rates (used for qPCA experiments on small correlation matrices), foreign exchange (FX) market time-series (used in quantum reservoir computing experiments), fraud-detection style datasets (used in feature-selection experiments), and synthetic or small proprietary financial datasets for benchmarking QGANs and classifiers. Many studies used small sample sizes or reduced-dimensional inputs (PCA preprocessing) to match available qubit counts.

**Results summary:** This survey compiles and analyzes recent supervised and unsupervised QML implementations on real near-term quantum hardware across domains (high-energy physics, healthcare, finance). It identifies two dominant paradigms (quantum kernel methods and variational quantum circuits), describes commonly used encoding strategies (basis, amplitude, angle, data re-uploading, Hamiltonian encoding), and summarizes empirical demonstrations on NISQ devices reporting modest, task-dependent performance (e.g., AUCs ≈0.78–0.83 for small HEP datasets, high-fidelity QGAN state replication). The work emphasizes practical bottlenecks—noise, finite coherence, data-loading costs, long wall-clock runtimes, measurement shot overhead, ansatz/trainability issues (barren plateaus)—and surveys mitigation strategies (error mitigation, ensemble learning, specialized optimizers). It concludes that a general quantum advantage for ML on classical data remains unproven; progress depends on improved encodings, benchmarks, hardware, and algorithmic advances.

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** speculative

The survey states that while theoretical and small constrained examples predict potential quantum advantages, empirical evidence of a broadly applicable quantum advantage in ML on classical datasets is lacking. Demonstrations on current hardware show task- and dataset-specific promise (small-scale or quantum-data tasks), but no general, reproducible quantum speedup or superior generalization has been established; therefore claims of advantage remain speculative and conditional on encoding, data type (quantum vs classical), and future hardware/algorithmic advances.
## Limitations
- Hardware noise and short qubit coherence times (crosstalk, decoherence, gate errors, SPAM, shot noise) limiting circuit depth and fidelity
- Insufficient qubit count and gate fidelity to enable quantum error correction in near-term devices
- Long end-to-end run times on real QPUs (including initialization, queuing, execution, measurement); example: 200 hours for 500 training iterations on 100 events
- High measurement (shot) requirements and trade-offs with readout errors; increasing shots can be counterproductive
- Data-loading bottleneck: many useful encodings (e.g., amplitude encoding) require deep or exponentially large circuits; lack of efficient, practical qRAM
- Quantum kernel methods scale poorly with large datasets (quadratic quantum cost with training dataset size) and incur expensive classical post-processing
- Finite sampling (shot) noise prevents current heuristic QML algorithms from provably solving classically hard learning problems
- Error mitigation and some error-correction approaches introduce significant additional resource overhead; some mitigation techniques scale unfavorably (exponential) with qubit number
- Barren plateaus and flat optimization landscapes (vanishing gradient variance) for many variational ansätze, aggravated by excessive entanglement, global observables, cost choice, and noise
- Ansatz selection, parameter initialization, and architecture choice critically affect trainability and are not yet standardized
- Limited access to QPUs and device variability (calibration drift, lack of transparency about calibration/compilation timings) hinder reproducibility and repeatable experiments
- Lack of standardized quantum datasets and benchmarks tailored to QML; current use of classical benchmarks may not expose quantum advantage
- Hyperparameter reporting and transparency are often insufficient; missing open-source reference implementations impair reproducibility and may lead to positive reporting bias
- Optimization and training overheads: classical optimizers require many repeated circuit evaluations; gradient estimation methods face scalability limits (parameter-shift, shot noise)
- Scalability limits for large problems: trainability, prediction error, and resource scaling are not well characterized for real-world (large) datasets
- Security and privacy: QML currently lacks mature privacy-preserving methods (e.g., differentially-private QML); classifiers can be vulnerable to adversarial examples
- Explainability: QML models lack established methods for interpretability/explainability suitable for high-stakes domains
- Overfitting and generalization challenges on small datasets; quantum models (e.g., kernels, QSVMs) can be sensitive to sample size and noise
- Inefficiency of many encodings when mapped to native device gate sets and connectivity (SWAP overheads, increased gate counts)
- Limited evidence of practical advantage over classical methods for real-world datasets—possible that careful classical baselines can match quantum approaches
## Open questions
- Can QML reliably demonstrate a clear, reproducible quantum advantage over classical algorithms for practical financial (and other real-world) tasks?
- To what extent are reported QML performance gains due to hyperparameter tuning, benchmark selection, or implementation choices versus fundamental quantum structure?
- Which data types and problem instances (classical vs quantum data, structure of features) are likely to yield genuine quantum advantage?
- How can we design encoding/feature maps that are both hardware-efficient and classically hard-to-simulate yet practical for real datasets?
- What are scalable, hardware-aware ansatz design and parameter-initialization strategies that avoid barren plateaus while remaining expressive?
- How can gradient estimation / training methods for parameterized quantum circuits be made as scalable and efficient as classical backpropagation?
- How much overhead will error mitigation and error correction impose in practice, and will that overhead negate expected quantum speedups for finance workloads?
- What standardized benchmarks and quantum datasets should the community adopt to fairly evaluate QML approaches?
- How can privacy-preserving techniques (e.g., differential privacy) be adapted or developed for QML, especially for sensitive financial data?
- What are appropriate methods for explainability in QML (XQML) that provide human-understandable insights into quantum model decisions for regulated domains?
- How can federated learning and distributed quantum training be effectively implemented given limited, heterogeneous QPU access?
- Which error-mitigation, ensemble, or hybrid classical-quantum approaches best trade off practicality and performance for near-term devices?
- How do device-level factors (connectivity, calibration variability, provider differences) impact cross-device reproducibility and algorithm selection?
- What are the realistic scaling limits (in terms of qubits, depth, shots) for QML approaches to handle enterprise-scale financial datasets?

**Future work:**
- Establish standardized benchmarks and open, easily preparable quantum datasets for fair QML evaluation
- Develop and study efficient quantum data-preparation and encoding techniques (including practical qRAM or qRAM alternatives) tailored to real-world datasets
- Research efficient error mitigation and scalable quantum error-correction methods; investigate quantum autoencoders and other QML-based error-correction strategies
- Design ansatz-selection frameworks, parameter-initialization strategies, and hardware-aware circuit architectures that mitigate barren plateaus and improve trainability
- Explore new training and optimization methods (including quantum natural gradient, QN-SPSA, and alternatives to parameter-shift) that reduce shot complexity and improve convergence
- Investigate privacy-preserving QML (e.g., differential privacy adaptations), and assess robustness to adversarial attacks
- Develop explainable quantum machine learning (XQML) methods to render QML decisions interpretable for high-stakes domains
- Encourage better reporting practices: open-source reference implementations, detailed hyperparameter documentation, and standardized evaluation protocols to improve reproducibility
- Study federated quantum learning, distributed training, and quantum boosting methods to scale QML across limited-capacity devices
- Identify practical, classically-hard problem instances and community consensus tasks where quantum methods are expected to outperform classical approaches
- Investigate ensemble-learning, bootstrap aggregating, and boosting as error-mitigation and generalization-enhancement techniques for near-term QML
- Characterize end-to-end system performance (including classical-quantum interaction latency) and develop benchmarks like CLOPS further to capture practical throughput
- Analyze trainability and generalization (theory and empirical) for large-scale, realistic datasets, including financial applications and sample-complexity studies

## Contradictions
- The paper contradicts optimistic claims of broad quantum superiority: although some small studies report comparable or slightly improved metrics (AUCs ~0.78–0.83) on limited datasets, the survey concludes no general, empirically established quantum advantage in ML for classical datasets.
- The paper challenges scalability claims: quantum kernel methods and certain encoding strategies face quadratic or worse scaling in training-set/kernel computation and data-loading costs, contradicting assertions that near-term approaches scale readily to real-world financial problem sizes.
