## Abstract summary
The paper addresses the bottleneck of extracting classical function values from quantum states that encode functions in their amplitudes. It proposes combining orthogonal function expansion with matrix product state (MPS) tensor-network approximation and presents a quantum circuit plus an alternating optimization (using Hadamard tests and SVD) to learn an MPS representation of the expansion coefficients. A numerical experiment approximating a multi-asset option price demonstrates the method's feasibility for producing a classically evaluable approximation while reducing degrees of freedom.
## Methodology
The paper proposes a method to extract a multivariate function encoded in the amplitudes of a quantum state by combining orthogonal function expansion with a tensor-network (matrix product state, MPS) approximation of the expansion coefficients, and by representing that MPS as a variational quantum circuit. Conceptually, the approach encodes an MPS of orthogonal-function expansion coefficients into a quantum circuit (VMPS), applies oracles that prepare orthogonal-function basis states (V_i^OF) to produce a state that amplitude-encodes the approximated function, and then optimizes the variational blocks of the circuit by alternatingly updating each block to maximize the fidelity with the target function-encoding state. The update for each block is carried out by forming a real matrix F_i (partial overlap), performing an SVD F_i = X D Y, and setting the block to V_i = X Y. In the numerical validation the authors perform a classical simulation: they compute the target expansion coefficients a_l via Monte Carlo pricing on a grid (discrete orthogonality with cosine basis), build a target vector |a>, and then classically optimize the VMPS circuit parameters by maximizing F' = <a|~a>, using the same alternating SVD update procedure. Tensor contractions to evaluate the approximate function are performed using the ITensor library. The numerical experiment focuses on pricing a 5-asset "worst-of" put under the Black–Scholes model; Monte Carlo is used both to compute grid values for coefficient estimation and to generate random sample points for evaluation. The paper also explores variant circuit topologies (sliding-block circuit V'_MPS) and studies the relationship between approximation error and degrees of freedom.

**Algorithms used:** Matrix Product State (MPS) / Tensor Train, Orthogonal function expansion (tensorized cosine basis), Variational quantum circuit optimization with alternating updates (block-wise SVD update), Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) for block updates, Hadamard test (described for quantum estimation of overlaps; replaced by direct classical overlaps in simulations), Monte Carlo integration (for option pricing and target state construction)
**Frameworks:** ITensor (tensor calculations), TF Quant Finance (Monte Carlo option pricing)

**Experimental setup:** All numerical experiments were classical simulations. The authors computed Black–Scholes derivative prices via Monte Carlo (10^5 sample paths) on a tensor-product grid (ngr = D per dimension) to obtain grid function values, computed discrete orthogonal expansion coefficients, and then performed classical optimization of the variational MPS circuit parameters (alternating block-wise SVD updates) using ITensor for tensor contractions. No quantum hardware was used.

**Dataset:** Synthetic financial data: prices of a five-asset worst-of put option under the Black–Scholes model. Option prices on a D-degree tensor grid were computed via Monte Carlo simulation (1e5 paths) and used to form discrete orthogonal expansion coefficients. Additional random samples (1e4) drawn from the BS model were used for evaluation.

## Findings
- [speculative] Directly reading out function values f(x) from an amplitude-encoded quantum state |f> is exponentially costly in the number of grid points Ngr (and hence in dimension d) because amplitudes scale like 1/sqrt(Ngr), so amplitude estimation on each basis state generally destroys the exponential quantum speedup.
- [speculative] Coefficients of an orthogonal-function expansion of f can be obtained from inner products ⟨P_l|f⟩ (where |P_l⟩ encodes the basis function values) and these inner products do not suffer from the same exponential amplitude suppression as computational-basis amplitudes, making orthogonal expansion a promising route to read out f.
- [speculative] A naive orthogonal expansion in d dimensions requires D^d coefficients and is therefore exponential in d; combining orthogonal expansion with a tensor-network approximation (MPS) for the coefficient tensor can drastically reduce degrees of freedom under suitable conditions.
- [speculative] One can construct a quantum circuit (VMPS + V^OF blocks) that encodes an MPS representation of expansion coefficients into amplitudes, and then encode the orthogonal basis function values so the overall circuit amplitude-encodes an MPS–orthogonal expansion approximation of f.
- [speculative] The blockwise alternating optimization procedure (estimating local reduced operators via Hadamard tests, SVD, update V_i = X Y) adapted from prior work can be used to optimize the VMPS circuit to maximize fidelity ⟨f|f_approx⟩.
- [supported] A classical numerical experiment (simulating the procedure with classical precomputation of orthogonal coefficients) on a 5-dimensional 'worst-of' put option showed that an MPS-based approximation (d=5, D=r=16, bond dimension r=16) produced approximations close to Monte Carlo reference values on test points.
- [supported] In that numerical example the MPS-based approximation used 12,544 degrees of freedom versus 1,048,576 full expansion coefficients (≈two orders of magnitude reduction) while maintaining comparable approximation accuracy.
- [supported] In experiments the maximum absolute difference between the MPS-based approximation and Monte Carlo reference on test points was ≈0.5086 (option price units) and the maximum difference between the full cosine expansion (D=16) and Monte Carlo was ≈0.5102; the max difference between MPS and full cosine expansion was ≈0.3164 on random test points (reported in paper Table 1).
- [supported] The authors observed an empirical power-law relationship between approximation error and degrees of freedom for their circuit variants in the numerical tests.
- [supported] The method (as implemented in the paper) was tested in a hybrid/simulated setting in which actual quantum oracles V_i^OF and O_f were not queried on quantum hardware; instead the coefficient tensor a_l was precomputed classically (ngr = D used in the experiment).
- [speculative] The construction of the basis-state preparation oracles V_i^OF is in principle possible (authors sketch a construction using Grover–Rudolph style state preparation and controlled blocks), but practical costs and complexities are not fully quantified in the paper.
- [supported] The paper explicitly states and demonstrates that it does not provide rigorous overall query-complexity bounds (for O_f and V_i^OF) or a proof that the alternating quantum-circuit optimization always converges; these remain open and are left for future work.
- [speculative] The authors claim the approach is compatible with both fault-tolerant quantum computers and NISQ devices and could be combined with quantum algorithms that output amplitude-encoded solutions (e.g., quantum PDE solvers) to produce classical approximations efficiently; however no demonstration of preserved asymptotic quantum speedup is provided.

**Results summary:** The paper proposes a general method to read out a function encoded in amplitudes of a quantum state by combining orthogonal-function expansion and tensor-network (MPS) compression of the expansion coefficients. A quantum circuit design (VMPS plus basis state blocks) and an alternating local optimization procedure (Hadamard-test-based estimation of local reduced operators followed by SVD updates) are presented to produce an MPS-based classical approximation of the function. The authors validate feasibility with a classical numerical experiment on a 5-asset 'worst-of' put option: an MPS representation (d=5, D=r=16) reduced parameter count from 1,048,576 to 12,544 while achieving comparable accuracy to the full cosine expansion and Monte Carlo references (maximum errors reported ≈0.5 in option-price units). They observe an empirical power-law decay of error with degrees of freedom. Theoretical complexity bounds and guarantees of convergence are not provided and are left as future work.

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** speculative

The authors motivate the work by noting that naive amplitude readout destroys potential exponential speedups (amplitudes scale with 1/sqrt(Ngr)), and they propose a method intended to avoid exponential readout cost by estimating inner products with orthogonal basis states and compressing coefficients with an MPS encoded via a quantum circuit. However, the paper does not provide rigorous query complexity bounds or proofs that the method preserves asymptotic quantum speedup; only a classical simulated feasibility study (with classical precomputation of coefficients) is presented, so any claim that the approach preserves quantum advantage remains speculative.

## Open questions
- What is the total query complexity (number of calls to O_f and V_i^OF) to produce an approximating function within a target error ϵ, as a function of d, D, r and desired accuracy?
- Under what conditions on the target function f (smoothness, decay, separability, etc.) does an MPS approximation with modest bond dimension r exist such that the proposed method is efficient?
- Can theoretical convergence guarantees be established for the alternating optimization of the circuit unitaries V_i (e.g., rates, local vs global optima, dependence on initialization)?
- How can the normalization factor C be estimated efficiently (quantum or classical) in general settings, and what is the cost?
- How do estimation/shot noise and hardware noise (NISQ-era errors) affect the fidelity estimation, optimization stability, and final approximation quality?
- How does the method perform and scale for much larger d (≫5) and for other classes of target functions beyond the tested finance example?
- What are the practical resource costs and circuit constructions for implementing V_i^OF and O_f on realistic quantum hardware (gate counts, depth, ancilla requirements)?
- What is the measurement/sample complexity of the Hadamard-test-based estimation of Fi required to reach useful precision, and can it be reduced?
- How sensitive is the method to the choice of orthogonal basis and grid (e.g., when discrete orthogonality is only approximate)?
- Can the power-law scaling between DOF and approximation error observed numerically be theoretically justified or predicted for broader classes of high-dimensional functions?
