## Abstract summary
The paper defines a reusable methodology for backtesting quantum and classical portfolio optimization algorithms under equivalent conditions and uses it to compare four quantum and three classical methods. Based on 10,000 experiments with IBEX35 data, the authors find quantum algorithms can match or slightly outperform classical ones and show better scalability trends; they also analyze VQE (including a CVaR variant) on simulators and IBM hardware and discuss practical challenges of running such algorithms on current >100 qubit devices.
## Methodology
The authors define a reusable backtesting methodology to compare classical and quantum algorithms for portfolio optimization under equivalent conditions. They implemented three classical (SMA, Markowitz mean-variance optimization (MVO), Sharpe-ratio optimization (SRO)) and four quantum algorithms (VQE, CVaR-VQE, QAOA, Grover Adaptive Search (GAS)) in Python using Qiskit and standard Python finance libraries. For each experiment they (1) select a random subset of assets from an IBEX35 universe, (2) obtain adjusted closing prices (Yahoo! Finance via bt), (3) compute expected returns and covariance matrix, (4) formulate a combinatorial optimization problem (binary selection x with budget constraint 1^T x = B) and transform it to a QUBO/Ising Hamiltonian, (5) run each algorithm (quantum methods use variational ansatz circuits or Grover-based search; classical methods use standard solvers), (6) obtain a candidate portfolio (equal-weight on chosen assets) and (7) backtest monthly rebalanced returns over the historical period. They execute large-scale statistical comparison: 10,000 independent backtesting runs with random subsets (five-asset experiments emphasized) to collect distributional performance metrics, and also run scalability experiments (2–100 assets) to compare per-iteration execution times. Quantum large-scale backtests were executed on simulators; selected illustrative VQE/CVaR-VQE runs were executed on IBM real devices (5-, 27-, 127-qubit machines) with careful qubit mapping and low-depth ansatz design to fit device connectivity and coherence limits. Performance metrics collected include realized total return distributions, mean/std, frequency of best-performing algorithm, and execution-time scaling per iteration.

**Algorithms used:** SMA, MVO, SRO, VQE, CVaR-VQE, QAOA, GAS (Grover Adaptive Search), NumPyMinimumEigensolver (classical exact reference)
**Frameworks:** Qiskit, bt (Python library), ffn (Python), cvxopt

**Experimental setup:** Backtesting pipeline implemented in Python/Qiskit. Large-scale backtesting (10,000 runs) performed using a quantum simulator (ibmq_qasm_simulator) for quantum algorithms and classical CPUs for classical algorithms. Selected validation runs executed on IBM Quantum real backends (ibmq_athens 5-qubit, ibm_cairo 27-qubit Falcon r5.11, ibm_washington 127-qubit Eagle r1 and other 127-qubit backends such as ibm_brisbane, ibm_cusco, ibm_nazca). Sessions were used to reduce queuing overhead where possible; reported execution times exclude queue wait. Ansätze were designed to fit device connectivity (linear entanglement path for 19 qubits on 27-qubit device, circular entanglement for 28 qubits on 127-qubit device).

**Dataset:** Historical adjusted closing prices for IBEX35 constituents obtained from Yahoo! Finance via the bt Python library. Backtesting period: price history from 2016 through 2020 used for monthly-rebalanced backtests (2016 used for historical calculations; investing from 2017). Example short-run tests used other windows (e.g., 2022-10-01 to 2022-12-01) for small-device demonstrations. Random subsets of the IBEX35 universe (typically 5 assets per experiment for the 10,000-run suite; other experiments used 2–100 random assets to measure runtime scaling).

## Findings
- [supported] The authors developed and published a reusable methodology to backtest and compare classical and quantum portfolio optimization algorithms and executed a large-scale experimental campaign (10,000 backtesting runs) under equivalent conditions.
- [supported] In the 10,000-experiment backtesting suite (five-random-asset experiments from IBEX35), quantum algorithms (notably VQE variants and QAOA) matched or slightly outperformed classical algorithms on average; after filtering for runs with higher inter-algorithm variance, QAOA and CVaR-VQE achieved the best strategy more often than classical baselines.
- [supported] Measured per-iteration execution times on IBM real quantum devices showed a milder growth with the number of qubits (smaller slope for newer devices) compared to classical methods, whose per-step time exhibited an exponential growth with problem size in their experiments.
- [supported] VQE with CVaR objective (CVaR-VQE) converged quickly in the hardware example reported (convergence reported after ~10 iterations) and produced a portfolio close to the classical exact (NumPyMinimumEigensolver) solution in the small-case example.
- [supported] Practical use on current IBM hardware requires careful mapping and Ansätze design: choosing higher-quality qubits and low-depth linear/circular entanglement paths (examples: 19-qubit linear path on a 27-qubit Falcon device and a 28-qubit circular path on a 127-qubit Eagle device) to avoid extra SWAPs and to keep execution within qubit coherence times.
- [supported] QAOA circuits are deeper than VQE circuits for equivalent problem encodings, making QAOA more vulnerable to noise on current NISQ hardware.
- [supported] Measurement/shot overhead and queueing dominate end-to-end quantum job time; reported examples include per-shot circuit durations and full measurement times (e.g., ~1125 μs per shot estimate for a transpiled 19-qubit VQE layer, ~35.3 s for 100,000 shots on one 27-qubit mapping; ~4352 μs per shot estimate and ~48.6 s for 100,000 shots on a 28-qubit mapping).
- [supported] Classical mean-variance optimization (MVO) and Sharpe-ratio optimization (SRO) implementations showed rapidly increasing runtime with asset count; SRO encountered feasibility problems above ~90 assets in their experiments.
- [speculative] The authors claim that quantum algorithms can handle very large numbers of assets in reasonable time on future larger (less noisy / fault-tolerant) quantum hardware.
- [speculative] Grover Adaptive Search (GAS) inherits a theoretical quadratic speedup but is impractical on current NISQ devices due to circuit depth and is therefore considered a candidate for future fault-tolerant quantum computers.
- [speculative] The paper asserts ('to the best of our knowledge') that this is the first systematic backtesting comparison of classical and quantum portfolio optimization algorithms; this is presented as an original-contribution claim by the authors.
- [speculative] The authors indicate that with improved models, error mitigation, and larger hardware, quantum algorithms could provide a practical advantage for realistic portfolio problems (including transaction costs and integer constraints).

**Results summary:** The paper presents a reproducible backtesting methodology and an extensive empirical comparison (10,000 simulations) of three classical and four quantum portfolio optimization algorithms using IBEX35 historical data. Empirically, quantum variational approaches (VQE and its CVaR variant) and QAOA performed competitively with classical methods and, after filtering for high-variance problem instances, were more often the best-performing strategy. The authors measured execution-time scaling on IBM hardware and observed that per-iteration quantum execution times displayed a gentler growth with problem size than the classical algorithms' per-step times (which grew exponentially). Practical experiments on 27- and 127-qubit IBM devices highlighted hardware constraints (noise, connectivity, mapping, circuit depth) and showed that low-depth VQE-CVaR can converge rapidly in small instances and produce results close to exact classical solvers. The paper concludes the observed quantum competitiveness is promising but emphasizes that a true, general quantum advantage remains conditional on future improvements in quantum hardware and error mitigation.

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** speculative

The paper reports empirical evidence that quantum algorithms (VQE variants and QAOA) can be competitive with classical algorithms on small-to-moderate instances and that per-iteration quantum execution times scale more favorably in their hardware measurements. However, they do not demonstrate a clear, general quantum advantage on realistic problem sizes; instead they argue potential for advantage on future larger, less noisy (or fault-tolerant) quantum hardware. Thus the claim of practical quantum advantage is presented as conditional and speculative rather than demonstrated.
## Limitations
- Quantum hardware limitations: noise, decoherence, limited coherence times, and nonuniform qubit/link error rates that constrain circuit depth and fidelity (author-stated).
- Queueing and execution overheads on cloud-access quantum devices (long queue times, communication/validation overhead) that make iterative hybrid algorithms (e.g., VQE) impractical to run at scale on real backends; therefore most backtests were performed on simulators (author-stated).
- Use of simulators for the bulk of backtesting; real-device experiments were limited to illustrative/validation runs and small instances (author-stated).
- Simplified problem modelling: equal-weight portfolios and a binary selection formulation with a penalty relaxation for the budget constraint; transaction costs, market impact, and integer lot constraints were not modelled (author-stated).
- Heuristic nature and stochasticity of the algorithms (both classical and quantum) produce high variance in outcomes across instances; therefore results are noisy and it is hard to declare a definitive winner (author-stated).
- Limited conclusions about practical quantum advantage: observed slight or occasional outperformance by quantum methods, but results are not uniformly conclusive and depend on filtering and instance choice (author-stated).
- Ansatz and mapping constraints imposed by specific hardware topologies (finding low-depth Ansätze and good linear/circular qubit paths) limit experiment sizes on real devices (author-stated).
- Penalty-term formulation for the 1Tx = B constraint can yield solutions violating the budget unless penalty is tuned — the chosen approach may affect comparability (author-stated).

- [inferred] Backtesting limited to one market index (IBEX35) and a particular historical period limits external validity across other markets, asset classes, and market regimes.

## Key ideas
- #idea:quantum-advantage — In a large empirical backtest suite (10,000 randomized runs on IBEX35 data) quantum algorithms (VQE, CVaR-VQE, QAOA, GAS on simulators) can match or slightly outperform classical baselines (SMA, MVO, SRO) and show favorable per-iteration scaling trends in simulator experiments.
- #idea:hybrid-approach — Practical implementations use hybrid workflows: variational circuits evaluated on QPU/simulator with classical COBYLA optimizer and classical pre/postprocessing (return/covariance estimation, penalty conversion to QUBO).
- #idea:near-term-feasibility — The authors demonstrate selected runs on IBM real devices (5-, 27-, 127-qubit backends) using low-depth ansatz and topology-aware mapping, arguing NISQ-era feasibility for illustrative instances.
- #limitation:simulation-only — Large-scale statistical comparisons (10,000 runs, scalability sweeps up to 100 assets) were performed on simulators; only illustrative subset runs were executed on real hardware.
- #limitation:noise — Deeper circuits (e.g., QAOA) are noted as more noise-sensitive; hardware runs required low-depth ansatz and careful qubit mapping, indicating noise-limited performance on current devices.
- #limitation:qubit-count — Practical constraints (connectivity, coherence) forced low-depth/topology-matched ansatz and limited the size of device-based experiments despite access to >100 qubit backends.
- #limitation:data-encoding — The approach relies on QUBO/Ising formulations and Pauli-term decompositions of portfolio objectives (risk/return), which incurs overhead in Hamiltonian construction and measurement aggregation (e.g., CVaR evaluation).
- #idea:quantum-advantage — The paper introduces a reusable backtesting methodology that enforces equivalent conditions between classical and quantum methods, providing a fair empirical basis to claim modest advantage in some settings.
- #idea:hybrid-approach — CVaR-VQE variant: replacing mean-aggregation with CVaR in the variational objective to target tail-risk-aware portfolios; classical computation is used for return/covariance estimation and postselection of measured bitstrings.
## Contradictions
- The paper claims better scalability trends for quantum algorithms (based on simulator timing sweeps) but most large-scale results are simulator-only and real-device experiments are limited by qubit connectivity, coherence and noise — a tension between simulated scalability and practical hardware limitations (contradiction:scalability).
