## Abstract summary
This preprint presents a quantum algorithm for Monte Carlo pricing of financial derivatives, showing how to prepare risk-neutral probability distributions in superposition, implement payoff functions as quantum circuits, and extract option prices via quantum measurements. By applying amplitude estimation the authors demonstrate a quadratic speedup in the number of samples needed to achieve a given estimation error, and illustrate the approach on European and Asian option pricing with numerical evidence.
## Methodology
This work is primarily theoretical and algorithmic. The authors develop a quantum algorithmic framework to price financial derivatives (European and Asian options) by mapping the Monte Carlo expectation problem onto a quantum amplitude-estimation/phase-estimation routine. Methodological components include: (1) modelling underlying asset dynamics with the Black-Scholes-Merton (BSM) risk-neutral framework; (2) discretizing relevant continuous probability distributions (Gaussian/Brownian motion) on n qubits using the Grover–Rudolph state-preparation scheme to prepare superpositions encoding sampling probabilities; (3) implementing payoff functions as reversible quantum arithmetic circuits (encoding payoffs, stock-price update steps, averages for Asian options); (4) encoding the payoff-valued random variable into an ancilla qubit via controlled rotations so that the probability amplitude of the ancilla equals the expectation to be estimated; (5) applying amplitude-amplification/estimation and quantum phase estimation (using the QFT and controlled powers of the constructed rotation/reflector unitary Q = US) to estimate the desired expectation with an asymptotically quadratic improvement in dependence on estimation error (1/epsilon quantum vs 1/epsilon^2 classical); and (6) validating the claimed scaling advantage by classical numerical simulations in which single-qubit phase rotations eiθσz/2 (with θ derived from the analytic BSM price) are used to emulate phase-estimation behavior and measure error scaling versus classical Monte Carlo. The methodology also provides complexity/resource analyses (queries to the reflection/unitary, qubit counts for discretization) and error decomposition (discretization error and amplitude-estimation error).

**Algorithms used:** Amplitude estimation (Brassard et al.), Amplitude amplification, Quantum phase estimation (QPE), Quantum Fourier Transform (QFT), Grover–Rudolph state preparation (log-concave distributions), Controlled rotations encoding real-valued payoffs, Reversible quantum arithmetic (add, multiply, exponentiate, comparisons)
**Frameworks:** Strawberry Fields (mentioned as possible simulator), ProjectQ (mentioned as possible simulator)

**Experimental setup:** No physical quantum hardware was used. Numerical experiments were classical simulations that emulate the phase-estimation subroutine by applying single-qubit rotations U_z = exp(-i θ/2 σ_z) with predetermined θ derived from the analytic Black–Scholes–Merton price. The simulation compared estimation error scaling of this single-qubit phase-estimation emulation (quantum case) against standard classical Monte Carlo sampling. A confidence target >99.5% was enforced by using D ≈ 24 independent phase-estimation repetitions (median trick). Example parameters used in simulations: S0 = 100, K = 50, r = 0.05, σ = 0.2, T = 1, D = 24. Number of Monte Carlo steps k was varied (figures show up to on the order of 1e7).

- [speculative] Amplitude estimation yields a quadratic improvement in sample complexity for estimating expectation values: classical Monte Carlo requires O(1/ϵ^2) samples while the quantum procedure requires O(1/ϵ) (up to polylogarithmic factors and dependence on a variance bound).

- [speculative] The state-preparation of log-concave distributions (e.g. Gaussian/Brownian increments) can be accomplished using the Grover–Rudolph procedure in O(n) steps for a 2^n discretization (section V and Appendix E).

- [speculative] The approach extends to path-dependent derivatives such as arithmetic Asian options by preparing product states of Brownian increments and computing averages coherently (section VI).
- [speculative] The algorithm assumes access to an efficiently preparable martingale (risk-neutral) measure and eﬃcient, low-depth reversible circuits for payoﬀs; these are listed as assumptions and are necessary for the stated speedups.
- [speculative] The paper argues potential practical impact: quadratic speedups could materially accelerate large Monte Carlo workloads used in XVA and other risk computations at financial institutions, enabling faster or more frequent risk evaluation (discussion).

**Results summary:** The paper proposes a concrete quantum Monte Carlo pricing pipeline: prepare a discretized risk-neutral distribution in superposition, compute payoﬀs into ancilla amplitudes via reversible quantum circuits, and apply amplitude estimation (via phase estimation / amplitude amplification) to extract expected payoﬀs. Theoretically, amplitude estimation reduces the sample complexity from O(1/ϵ^2) to O(1/ϵ) (up to polylog factors and dependence on a variance bound λ). The authors detail procedures for European and Asian options, give resource-scaling formulae, and provide classical numerical simulations (single-qubit phase-estimation proxy) that empirically exhibit near-quadratic error scaling consistent with the theoretical speedup. The proposal relies on key assumptions: efficient state preparation of the martingale distribution and efficient reversible payoﬀ circuits.

**Performance claims:**
- Quantum sample complexity for mean estimation: O(1/ϵ) (additive error ϵ) up to polylogarithmic factors (Theorem 2/3).
- Classical Monte Carlo sample complexity: O(1/ϵ^2) (Chebyshev bound, Eq. (15)).
- Resource bound: number of applications of the core unitary U scales as Õ(λ/ϵ) where λ^2 bounds the payoﬀ variance (Eq. (44)).
- Amplitude estimation success probability per run lower bound: 8/π^2 ≈ 0.81, with boosting by taking the median of O(log(1/(1−confidence))) runs (Appendix F).
- Numerical example: European call with S0=$100, K=$50, r=0.05, σ=0.2, T=1 has analytical price Π ≈ $10.5; classical fitted error exponent ζ_C = −0.5, quantum fitted exponent ζ_Q = −0.982 (simulation, Fig. 3).

- State-preparation complexity for log-concave distributions (Grover–Rudolph): O(n) gates to prepare a 2^n-point discretized distribution (section V and Appendix E).
## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** theoretical

The paper proves (and cites established theorems) that amplitude estimation yields a quadratic improvement in asymptotic sample complexity for estimating expectation values (O(1/ϵ) vs O(1/ϵ^2)), and demonstrates this scaling in classical numerical simulations of a phase-estimation proxy. The advantage is therefore theoretical/mathematical and supported by simulation evidence in an idealized model; it is not demonstrated on physical quantum hardware and requires assumptions (efficient state preparation, implementable controlled unitaries), so practical end-to-end advantage depends on those implementation costs.
