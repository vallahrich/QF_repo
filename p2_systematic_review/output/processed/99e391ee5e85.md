## Abstract summary
The paper adapts the GCS-Q (Graph-based Coalition Structure Generation) algorithm to cluster signed, weighted asset correlation graphs directly, formulating each partitioning step as a QUBO solved on a D-Wave quantum annealer. Evaluated on synthetic and real-world (Yahoo Finance) data, the method dynamically infers the number of clusters and outperforms classical baselines (SPONGE, k-Medoids) in clustering quality while preserving signed correlation structure.
## Methodology
The authors represent assets as nodes in a signed, weighted undirected graph where edge weights are pairwise Pearson correlations (ρij ∈ [-1,1]) computed from rolling returns. They apply the Graph-based Coalition Structure Generation algorithm (GCS-Q) to directly cluster this signed graph without transforming correlations into distances. GCS-Q begins with all nodes in one coalition and iteratively partitions subgraphs by computing minimum cuts that maximize intra-cluster total edge weight (equivalently minimize weight across the cut). Each minimum-cut step is formulated as a QUBO and solved on a quantum annealer (D-Wave Advantage_system5.4). The partitioning is applied recursively to the resulting subgraphs until a stopping condition is met (stop dividing a subgraph if no cut value is lower than the sum of all edges in that subgraph), thereby inferring the number of clusters k automatically. The study benchmarks GCS-Q against classical baselines (SPONGE, SPONGEsym, and k-Medoids/PAM), using synthetic structurally-balanced signed graphs and real-world asset return data (hourly prices from Yahoo Finance via yfinance). Evaluation metrics include Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) on synthetic data (ground truth clusters known) and a structural-balance Penalty metric on real data. The implementation uses Python 3.12, with QUBOs submitted via the D-Wave Ocean SDK to the remote D-Wave annealer and remaining computation run on a local Intel Core i7-12800H workstation with 64 GB RAM.

**Algorithms used:** GCS-Q (Graph-based Coalition Structure Generation), QUBO (Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization), Quantum annealing (D-Wave Advantage), SPONGE (spectral method for signed graphs), SPONGEsym, k-Medoids (PAM), Eigengap heuristic (for estimating k), Minimum cut (formulated as QUBO), Pearson correlation (for adjacency weights)
**Frameworks:** Python 3.12, dwave-ocean-sdk, yfinance (data retrieval)

**Experimental setup:** QUBO subproblems sent to D-Wave Advantage_system5.4 (5614 physical qubits, 40,050 couplers, Pegasus topology) via the dwave-ocean-sdk cloud APIs. Local computations performed on a workstation with 12th Gen Intel Core i7-12800H CPU and 64 GB RAM. Per-QUBO solve times were capped at 1 second; practical end-to-end runs include cloud latency and queue wait times.

**Dataset:** Synthetic: structurally balanced signed graphs with intra-cluster edges sampled from [0.1,1.0], inter-cluster edges from [-1.0,-0.1], non-uniform cluster sizes via a Dirichlet allocation, node counts varied (10 to 100), clusters varied (2 to 10), 33 random seeds. Real-world: hourly closing prices for 50 assets from diverse sectors obtained via yfinance; rolling log returns computed and Pearson correlation matrices used as signed adjacency.
## Experiment details
### Input
Synthetic graphs: generated with parameters described above (node counts 10..100, k=2..10, 33 seeds), continuous edge weights (intra [0.1,1.0], inter [-1.0,-0.1]), non-uniform cluster sizes via Dirichlet; no additional noise. Real-world: hourly closing prices from Yahoo Finance (50 assets), compute rolling log returns, compute Pearson correlation matrix used directly as signed adjacency matrix for clustering.

### Process
1) Compute Pearson correlation matrix from asset returns to obtain signed weighted graph. 2) Initialize GCS-Q with the full graph. 3) For each subgraph, formulate a minimum-cut partitioning as a QUBO instance (binary variables indicate side of cut). 4) Solve QUBO on D-Wave Advantage (annealer) via dwave-ocean-sdk; retrieve cut (St, S̄t). 5) Recursively apply steps 3–4 to induced subgraphs, removing cut edges, until stopping criterion (no cut whose value is lower than the sum of all edges in that subgraph). 6) Output partition Π; compute evaluation metrics. Baselines: run SPONGE/SPONGEsym (k estimated via eigengap) and k-Medoids (PAM) with required k inputs. Repeat across seeds/rolling windows. Parameters noted: per-QUBO solve cap = 1s; graph sizes varied; embedding limited practical QUBO size (~≤175 nodes) due to hardware topology.

### Output
Primary outputs: cluster partitions Π for each experiment. Evaluation metrics: Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) for synthetic experiments (ground-truth known), and Penalty metric (sum of absolute negative intra-cluster weights plus positive inter-cluster weights) for real-world data. Baselines compared: SPONGE, SPONGEsym, k-Medoids/PAM. Reported outcomes: GCS-Q achieved higher ARI on synthetic data and lower Penalty on real-world Yahoo Finance experiments, with results averaged across seeds and time windows.

**Results summary:** The paper adapts the GCS-Q graph coalition-structure algorithm to asset clustering on signed correlation graphs, formulates each partition step as a QUBO, and solves those QUBOs on a D-Wave quantum annealer. In both synthetic experiments (continuous-weight, non-uniform-size clusters) and a Yahoo Finance 50-asset dataset, GCS-Q produced higher clustering quality (measured by ARI on synthetic data and a structural-balance penalty on real data) than SPONGE/SPONGEsym and k-Medoids (PAM). The method determines the number of clusters automatically and preserves signed relationships without lossy transformations. Practical limitations due to hardware topology limit problem size (~175 nodes), and real-run times were dominated by cloud-access latency despite short per-QUBO anneal times.

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** disputed

The authors claim a quantum advantage in solution quality based on better clustering metrics vs selected classical baselines on their datasets, but they do not benchmark against state-of-the-art classical combinatorial optimizers (e.g., Gurobi/CPLEX) nor exhaustively rule out classical heuristics matching or exceeding solution quality. The claim relies on limited comparisons and is not demonstrated under standard, rigorous criteria for quantum advantage, so it should be considered disputed.

## Open questions
- How do clusters produced by GCS-Q perform in downstream financial tasks (portfolio construction, allocation, statistical arbitrage) measured by economic metrics such as Sharpe Ratio and realized returns?
- How robust is GCS-Q to realistic noise, non-stationarity, and time-varying correlations in market data?
- How does GCS-Q scale (solution quality and runtime) beyond the current ~175-node hardware limit, and what are practical limits on n and k?
- What is the impact of alternative similarity measures (e.g., rank correlations, cointegration, non-linear metrics) on clustering quality with GCS-Q?
- How much do hardware topology and connectivity (Pegasus vs Zephyr or other topologies) and embedding strategies affect solution quality and required resources?
- How does GCS-Q compare to state-of-the-art classical optimizers (Gurobi, CPLEX) and advanced heuristics in both solution quality and wall-clock runtime on the same problems?
- Can gate-model approaches (QAOA or other variational algorithms) or qubit-efficient encodings offer practical advantages over annealing for this problem?
- What are the effects of annealer noise, chain strength selection, and QUBO parameter tuning on robustness and reproducibility of results?
- How stable are the inferred clusters over time and across market regimes, and what is the appropriate rebalancing cadence for trading applications?
- What are the operational costs, latency, and reliability trade-offs of deploying a quantum-enhanced clustering pipeline in production trading systems?
