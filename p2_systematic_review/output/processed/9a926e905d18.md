## Abstract summary
This paper studies applying the Harrow–Hassidim–Lloyd (HHL) quantum algorithm to portfolio optimization by formulating the task as a quadratic program with equality constraints and mapping it to a linear system. The authors implement and test the approach with Qiskit on IBM’s platform, showing that HHL can produce approximate solutions that closely match exact classical solutions and demonstrating the potential for quantum speedup on such financial optimization problems. This paper surveys the state and challenges of unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) forensics and outlines key technical approaches for acquiring and analyzing evidence from UAV systems. It describes UAV system components (airframe, power, controllers, sensors, wireless modules and ground systems), outlines forensic methods for mobile apps, onboard memory and ground-station software, and demonstrates practical extraction and interpretation of flight logs from a DJI Mavic 2 Pro. The work aims to provide guidance and a baseline for future UAV forensic investigations and tool development.
## Methodology
The authors reformulate a portfolio optimization (quadratic program with equality constraints) as a linear system A x = b via Lagrange multipliers. They apply the HHL quantum linear-systems algorithm (phase-estimation → controlled rotation → uncompute → measurement/postselection) to solve the linear system on a quantum circuit. A small toy instance (three assets) is embedded into an enlarged (8×8) Hermitian matrix so it can be encoded on 3 data qubits (thus requiring a total circuit of 9 qubits in their example). They implement the HHL circuit using Qiskit on IBM’s quantum computing platform: prepare |b⟩, run QPE to estimate eigenphases, perform ancilla controlled rotations proportional to inverse eigenvalues, inverse-QPE (uncompute), then measure an ancilla qubit and postselect on outcome |1⟩ to obtain an output quantum state whose amplitudes encode the solution x. They extract component ratios from the resulting state and compare them to the exact classical solution to assess accuracy. The paper proposes a Quantum Hidden Markov Model (QHMM) approach for stock prediction by extending classical HMMs to the quantum (density matrix / Kraus operator) formalism. Observations (daily returns) are discretized into six classes based on pre-defined up/down range bins. The QHMM likelihood for a sequence y1..yT is formed using Kraus operators K_{y}, with probabilities p(y_t | rho_{t-1}) = tr(K_{y_t} rho_{t-1} K_{y_t}^†). Learning is cast as maximizing the sequence log-likelihood under the constraint that the stacked Kraus operator matrix K satisfy K^† K = I (Stiefel manifold constraint). The constrained optimization is handled via a projected/Manifold gradient-descent type update derived for the Stiefel manifold: k <- k - tau * U * (I + tau^2 V^† U)^{-1} V^† k (with U and V constructed from current gradient and K). A DA (data-assessment) score is computed to evaluate model quality and used to stop/adjust training; pseudo-code and update rules are given. For prediction the trained Kraus operators are used to compute J_n = - ln tr( K_n rho K_n^† ) for candidate n and pick the minimal J_n as the predicted class. Experiments were run on real stock data (CSI300 constituents, e.g. 600519, 601318, 000901) using daily prices from Jun 2019 to Oct 2019: returns were discretized to 6 classes (three up, three down buckets) and models were trained and evaluated for 1-, 3- and 5-day ahead predictions. Model learning and inference were implemented in MATLAB; density matrices were generated and Kraus operators estimated by the iterative procedure until DA convergence. A simple trading simulation using the 1-week predictions showed an empirical profit example (≈6.7% gain) for a pick-and-trade rule.

**Frameworks:** Qiskit, MATLAB

Controlled rotations and inverse phase-estimation used, with ancilla measurement and postselection on |1>.

Covariance/correlation matrix (3×3) provided in paper. The problem was embedded into an 8×8 Hermitian matrix and corresponding 8-D vector b for quantum encoding.

- [supported] The paper proposes a novel SIR epidemic model that incorporates two kinds of local information feedback: information from Infected neighbors (I) and from Recovered neighbors (R), which modulate a susceptible node's infection rate.
- [supported] For the model, a cavity-theory-based analytic expression for the epidemic threshold is derived; this analysis predicts the threshold depends on the vigilance parameter 'a' (response to I-information) but not on the recovery-feedback parameter 'b'.
- [supported] Simulation experiments (scale-free networks with <k>=6 and <k^2>=117) validate the cavity-theory prediction: the threshold increases with the vigilance parameter a and is effectively independent of b in the tested regimes.
- [supported] The paper demonstrates that a standard mean-field derivation gives a threshold expression that depends on both a and b and therefore can be misleading for this model; empirical results show mean-field predictions can be inaccurate here.
- [supported] The simulations reveal that nodes of intermediate degree are the most susceptible (largest final infection/recovery density), i.e., intermediate-degree nodes can show higher infection prevalence than low- or very-high-degree nodes in this model.
- [supported] The information signal originating from Infected neighbors (I) has a larger impact on epidemic dynamics (final prevalence, thresholds) than the information originating from Recovered neighbors (R) under the studied parameter ranges.
- [speculative] The authors argue (on conceptual grounds) that using local-information-driven behavioral responses can help control outbreaks by increasing 'a' (vigilance) — i.e., public-alerting strategies targeting awareness of infection may be more eﬀective than strategies that only disseminate recovery/precaution messages.
- [speculative] The work suggests that policy or intervention efforts focused on boosting public vigilance (the parameter a) might be prioritized over only distributing behavioral tips (parameter b) to suppress epidemic outbreaks, given their model results.

**Results summary:** The paper presents a small-scale demonstration applying the HHL quantum linear-systems algorithm to a 3-asset portfolio optimization problem (reduced to an equality-constrained QP and then linear system). Using a 9-qubit Qiskit implementation with phase estimation (2 bits), the authors recovered an approximate normalized solution vector whose component ratios closely matched the classical exact solution. They note amplitude/normalization discrepancies that would require more qubits and deeper circuits to reduce. The work is presented as a feasibility demonstration rather than a proof of quantum speedup. This study introduces an SIR-type model with two distinct local-information feedbacks: (i) vigilance prompted by locally observed infected neighbors (a), and (ii) precaution/advice spread from recovered neighbors (b). Using cavity-theory analysis the authors derive an epidemic-threshold condition that (for their approximations) depends on the vigilance parameter a but is insensitive to b; simulations on scale-free networks (N=10000, <k>=6,<k^2>=117) corroborate this prediction and show that mean-field theory can give qualitatively incorrect dependence on b. Numerical experiments further indicate that intermediate-degree nodes can be most vulnerable and that locally transmitted infection-awareness (I-feedback) has a stronger mitigating effect on outbreaks than the R-feedback in the tested regimes. The proposed model and analyses highlight the importance of modeling heterogeneous, behaviorally-mediated local information in epidemic dynamics.

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** speculative

The paper cites the standard theoretical claim that HHL can provide exponential speedup under certain assumptions, and suggests HHL could accelerate portfolio/optimization tasks. However, the authors only provide a small-scale feasibility experiment (no empirical timing or resource comparison to classical solvers) and do not demonstrate any practical quantum advantage for realistic problem sizes; therefore claims of advantage remain speculative in this work.
## Limitations
- The study is demonstrated only on a very small-scale (3-asset) toy portfolio — the implementation and results are therefore limited in scope and do not show behavior on realistic, larger portfolios (author-stated).
- The HHL-based solution yields an approximate quantum state whose amplitudes give only the relative ratios of solution components rather than the absolute classical coefficients; recovering exact classical solution amplitudes is not accomplished (author-stated).
- Achieving more accurate approximations requires increasing the number of qubits and circuit depth; doubling dimensions greatly increases gate count and resource requirements, so the approach does not yet scale practically (author-stated).
- The work demonstrates feasibility on simulated/limited quantum resources but does not show a scalable end-to-end advantage over classical methods for problem instances of real-world size (inferred).
- The paper does not address several practical HHL prerequisites and implementation challenges such as efficient quantum state preparation (QRAM or alternatives), condition-number dependence, and readout/tomography overhead to extract useful classical information from the quantum state (inferred).
- Noise, gate errors and decoherence on near-term quantum hardware (NISQ) are not treated; robustness of the approach under realistic device noise is not evaluated (inferred).
- The study reports amplitude discrepancies between exact and approximate solutions; the reasons for these discrepancies and the regimes in which they become prohibitive are not fully analyzed (author-stated/inferred).
- Limited empirical evaluation: experiments are reported on a small set of scenarios (e.g., CSI-300 time slices and a one-week trading simulation) and do not demonstrate robustness across diverse markets, time horizons, or economic regimes.
- No thorough baseline comparison: the paper does not present systematic comparisons versus standard classical methods (e.g., classical HMMs, ARIMA, LSTM, or other machine-learning models) or naïve baselines (e.g., buy-and-hold) to quantify relative gains.
- Short trading/backtest horizon and limited performance metrics: the only reported trading result (6.7% gain over one week) is short-term and presented without standard risk-adjusted metrics (e.g., Sharpe, drawdown) or transaction cost modeling.
- Approximation and numerical issues: the integer-polynomial encoding of real numbers (via LLL and scaling) introduces approximation error and numerical sensitivity that are not fully characterized theoretically or empirically.
- No realization on quantum hardware: the work formulates and uses a 'quantum hidden Markov' model but does not demonstrate deployment or acceleration on actual quantum devices — all results appear to be classical simulations/approximations.

- Scalability and computational cost: there is limited analysis of runtime and resource costs for larger universes of assets, longer sequences, or higher model complexity; scalability to production-sized problems is not demonstrated.
- Potential for overfitting / limited out-of-sample validation: the paper lacks a comprehensive out-of-sample cross-validation or walk-forward evaluation to rule out overfitting to particular time periods or assets.
- No treatment of market microstructure / trading frictions: the simulation ignores realistic transaction costs, market impact, latency and slippage which can materially change trading outcomes.
