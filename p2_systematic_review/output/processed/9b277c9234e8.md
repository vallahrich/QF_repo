## Abstract summary
The authors introduce a quantum algorithm based on variational quantum imaginary time evolution (VarQITE) to solve Feynman–Kac PDEs derived from multidimensional SDEs by exploiting the correspondence with a Wick-rotated Schrödinger equation. They describe discretization and Pauli-decomposition strategies, introduce a proxy re-scaling to approximately preserve ℓ1 (probability) normalization, and demonstrate close agreement with classical ODE solvers and Monte Carlo on a (2+1)-dimensional example using six and eight qubits, while discussing readout costs and algorithmic complexity.
## Methodology
The paper develops a variational quantum algorithm to solve expectations of stochastic differential equations (SDEs) by mapping them, via the Feynman–Kac formula and suitable variable transforms (including a gauge transform to remove first-order spatial derivatives and a Wick rotation to imaginary time), to a (possibly time-dependent) Schrödinger-type equation. The continuous PDE is discretized on a regular spatial grid (finite-difference stencils, periodic boundary conditions). The resulting discretized generator (matrix) is decomposed into a sum of implementable unitaries (tensor-products of Pauli operators) using constructions based on cyclic shift operators V±, diagonal projectors D(n), and truncated Taylor expansions of spatially varying coefficients. The quantum time evolution in imaginary time is approximated variationally (VarQITE) by a parameterized ansatz state (they use a RealAmplitudes-type circuit with layers of Ry rotations and CNOT entangling gates). McLachlan’s variational principle yields a linear system M ˙θ = V for the parameter dynamics; at each time step the elements of M and V are estimated via quantum circuits (Hadamard-test-style expectation value estimations, with QPE discussed as an alternative), the linear system is solved (Moore–Penrose inverse / least squares if ill-conditioned), and parameters are updated (forward Euler used in experiments; higher-order ODE integrators possible). Because probability-preserving PDEs (e.g., heat/Fokker–Planck) require ℓ1 normalization rather than quantum ℓ2 normalization, the authors embed the (discretized) probability vector as scaled amplitudes |˜ψ⟩ = α(t)|ψ⟩ and include α as an ansatz parameter; they propose an approximate ℓ1-enforcement via measuring the H⊗n overlap to estimate the ℓ1 proxy and rescale α. Observables / moments are extracted by constructing operator oracles Sf (via Taylor/piecewise approximations) and estimating expectation values of Sf using Hadamard tests or QPE. Numerical validation is performed by classical noiseless simulation (Python/NumPy) of the variational evolution on toy 2D anisotropic heat equations derived from correlated Brownian motions and comparing results against classical forward-Euler PDE solves, Monte Carlo, and an analytical kernel solution.

**Algorithms used:** Variational Quantum Imaginary Time Evolution (VarQITE), McLachlan variational principle, Quantum Imaginary Time Evolution (QITE), Hadamard test expectation estimation, Quantum Phase Estimation (discussed as alternative for readout), Finite-difference discretization (stencil method), Taylor expansion / truncated series for operator decomposition, Classical Monte Carlo (baseline), Forward Euler (classical PDE baseline)
**Frameworks:** Qiskit (RealAmplitudes ansatz referenced), Python, NumPy

**Experimental setup:** All numerical experiments were classical noiseless simulations implemented in Python using NumPy (no QPU or hardware noise was included). The authors simulate the variational circuit/state evolution (statevector-like simulation) for 6- and 8-qubit ansätze and compare to a classical forward-Euler finite-difference solver and Monte Carlo reference.

## Findings

- [supported] The authors numerically simulated the proposed VarQITE method on an illustrative (2+1)-dimensional anisotropic heat equation (correlated Brownian motions) and report strong agreement between VarQITE, classical forward-Euler PDE solution, and Monte Carlo estimates for 6- and 8-qubit encodings.
- [supported] For PDEs that should preserve probability (ℓ1 norm), introducing a proxy scaling parameter (α(t)) in the ansatz and an ℓ1-enforcement estimate allows the variational evolution to keep the solution approximately normalized, demonstrated in numerical experiments.
- [speculative] The paper analyses readout costs and claims an exponential speed-up is possible for state evolution (representation) but only a quadratic speed-up (compared with classical Monte Carlo) is achievable for extracting solution properties (moments) due to normalization and observable-loading costs.
- [speculative] Practical readout of expectations requires building an operator Sf that encodes the quantity of interest; constructing Sf may itself be exponentially costly in problem dimension, limiting end-to-end advantage in many cases.

**Results summary:** This preprint presents a variational quantum algorithm (VarQITE) for solving Feynman–Kac PDEs derived from SDEs by mapping them to Wick-rotated Schrödinger-type equations. The generator is decomposed into implementable Pauli-tensor unitaries via a construction using cyclic shifts, diagonal projectors and Taylor truncation. The authors provide complexity analyses for decomposition, parameter update circuitry, and readout, and they emphasize limitations stemming from ℓ1 vs ℓ2 normalization mismatch and construction costs for observable encodings. Numerical simulations (classical emulation) on a correlated two-dimensional Brownian example show close agreement between VarQITE, forward Euler PDE solutions and Monte Carlo for 6- and 8-qubit encodings. They further propose a proxy-scaling parameter and an ℓ1-enforcement procedure to maintain approximate probability normalization during imaginary-time variational evolution. The paper concludes with a discussion of algorithmic bottlenecks and potential financial applications, noting that end-to-end quantum advantage is constrained by readout and state-preparation costs.

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** theoretical

The authors argue theoretically (and support with classical simulations) that the variational imaginary-time approach can give an exponential compression for representing the discretized PDE solution (state evolution), but that extraction of meaningful observables (moments/payoffs) is limited to at best quadratic speed-ups due to normalization factors and the cost of constructing observable-encoding operators. No hardware demonstration of end-to-end advantage is provided; the advantage remains theoretical and conditional on observable-loading and readout costs.
## Limitations
- Ansatz expressivity limits accuracy: the chosen variational ansatz may not represent the target PDE solution adequately (authors note mismatch for small ansätze and need to increase layers/qubits).
- Barren-plateau risk: highly expressive (approximate t-design) ansätze can produce vanishing gradients and training difficulties (authors mention trade-off between expressivity and barren plateaus).
- Readout bottleneck: extracting properties of the final solution (e.g., expectations/moments) is costly — full state reconstruction (tomography) is infeasible and constructing measurement operators Sf can be hard or exponentially expensive.
- Limited quantum advantage for readout: even if evolution is efficient, readout of observables typically yields at best a quadratic speed-up (QPE vs Hadamard test trade-offs), and normalization factors can amplify estimation error (authors analyze α and ∥f∥2 effects).
- ℓ1 vs ℓ2 mismatch: embedding probability distributions (ℓ1-normalized) into quantum states (ℓ2-normalized) requires a proxy/scale parameter α(t); proxy enforcement is only approximate and enforcing ℓ1-norm can increase runtime and reduce potential advantage.
- Operator decomposition cost: decomposition of the PDE generator into implementable unitaries (Taylor expansion, projector-based construction) generates many Pauli terms; cost grows (polynomial in n per truncation order but can explode with dimension and Taylor order).
- Curse of dimensionality in measurement/operators: number of unitaries and measurements to estimate expectations grows exponentially with spatial dimension D and/or required Taylor order L (authors derive d2Dn2D(L+1) scaling and note exponential growth).
- State preparation overhead: preparing nontrivial initial states (payoﬀs, PDFs) can be expensive; general state preparation/oracle construction can be prohibitive and may offset any quantum advantage.
- Ill-conditioning and numerical stability: the McLachlan-derived matrix M may be ill-conditioned requiring Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse or least-squares; choice of time step δτ is critical and forward Euler used in examples is simplistic.
- Non-Hermitian Hamiltonians: general Feynman–Kac mappings can yield non-Hermitian generators requiring transformations (Wick rotation, similarity transforms) or embedding into larger unitary dilations, which introduce complexity and probabilistic success rates.
- Simulations idealized: numerical experiments ignore finite-sampling noise and hardware noise (authors acknowledge omission), so real-device performance is uncertain.
- Scaling of classical initialization: classical initialization of ansatz parameters (minimization to match initial condition) scales poorly with qubit number and can become a bottleneck.
- Backward-in-time/readout impracticality for option pricing: when pricing (backward evolution), normalization and readout requirements can make the approach impractical for direct single-price readout (authors highlight normalization scaling ∼B√2^n).
- [inferred] Accumulation of approximation error over long time horizons: while algorithmic cost may favor large T, imaginary-time/variational approximation and ansatz errors could accumulate and reduce final accuracy.
- [inferred] Dependence on truncation choices: truncation order in Taylor expansions and grid discretization choices (∆x, L, d) create a trade-off between operator complexity and approximation error that is nontrivial to optimize in practice.
- [inferred] Overhead from ancilla and gate-count: practical implementation of cyclic shifts, controlled high-order projectors and multi-qubit controlled gates requires many ancillas and deep gate sequences (authors reference O(n^2) / O(n^m) constructions), which may be prohibitive on near-term hardware.
## Open questions

- How to construct measurement operators Sf (and multi-dimensional oracles) eﬃciently for high-dimensional f without exponential cost?
- Can the ℓ1-norm enforcement proxy be improved to enforce probability normalization more accurately without destroying runtime advantages?
- How to handle non-Hermitian generators systematically (when transformations to Hermitian form are not available) in variational imaginary-time schemes with acceptable overhead?
- What are the effects of realistic noise (sampling noise, hardware noise) on VarQITE for PDEs and on the accuracy of extracted moments?
- How to choose discretization parameters (grid size, Taylor order L, number of intervals d) and ansatz hyperparameters jointly to balance approximation error and operator complexity?
- Can symbolic or circuit-based differentiation (instead of numerical differentiation) stabilize parameter updates and improve performance for deeper ansätze?
- What are optimal strategies for dealing with ill-conditioned M matrices (regularization, adaptive solvers) that arise in McLachlan updates?
- How to extend the method to other PDE types (hyperbolic equations like wave/Klein–Gordon) requiring higher-order time derivatives and their appropriate variational principles?
- How to generalize to SDEs with jumps / Poisson processes and include those in a Feynman–Kac to quantum mapping?
- How to combine or hybridize the proposed variational PDE approach with amplitude-estimation-based Monte Carlo methods to leverage strengths of both?
- What is the optimal choice (Hadamard test vs QPE) and quantum-classical resource allocation for estimating the required observables given target accuracies and hardware constraints?
- How does the approach scale and perform for realistic multi-factor financial models such as stochastic volatility, stochastic interest rates, and American-style (optimal stopping) derivatives?
