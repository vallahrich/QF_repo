## Abstract summary
The authors introduce Ascending-CVaR, an evolving objective function for variational quantum algorithms that starts from a Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) objective with small tail fraction and gradually increases the tail to the full expectation value. They evaluate the approach on VQE and QAOA across Max-Cut, Number Partitioning and Portfolio Optimisation instances (up to 20 qubits) and report faster convergence, higher overlap with optimal solutions and markedly improved success rates on hard instances compared to fixed CVaR or standard expectation-value objectives.
## Methodology
The authors propose and evaluate an evolving objective function (Ascending-CVaR) for variational quantum optimisation. They map combinatorial optimisation problems (Max-Cut, Number Partitioning, Portfolio Optimisation) to Ising/QUBO Hamiltonians, implement parameterised quantum ansatzes (hardware-efficient ansatz for VQE and the standard QAOA ansatz), and optimise variational parameters with a classical optimiser in a hybrid quantum-classical loop. Objective evaluation uses measurement samples in the computational basis: energies are computed per basis outcome and the Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) objective is computed on the lower α fraction of samples; Ascending-CVaR varies α during optimisation (linear or sigmoid schedules) starting from a small α0 (0.01) and increasing toward 1. Experiments are performed in simulation (IBM Qiskit Aer, noiseless multi-shot) on synthetic problem instances of sizes up to 20 qubits/variables. Metrics reported include overlap with the optimal (ground) computational-basis solution (probability), success rate defined as achieving ≥10% overlap, approximation ratio and normalised optimiser iterations. Baselines are constant-CVaR with fixed α (0.1, 0.2, 0.5) and the standard expectation-value objective (α=1). The optimisation loop uses COBYLA, with random initial parameters and per-evaluation shot budgets K=1000 (scaled as K/α when CVaR truncation is used). The authors report per-instance statistics over many random instances (e.g. 100 Max-Cut, 300 Number Partitioning, 100 Portfolio instances) and compare Ascending-CVaR to fixed-α CVaR and expectation-value objectives in both VQE and QAOA settings.

**Algorithms used:** VQE (hardware-efficient ansatz), QAOA, Ascending-CVaR (evolving objective function), CVaR (constant α), Expectation-value objective, COBYLA (classical optimiser)
**Frameworks:** Qiskit (Aer simulator), NetworkX (for graph instance generation)

**Experimental setup:** Noiseless simulation using IBM Qiskit Aer. Circuits executed multi-shot K=1000 (scaled as K/α for CVaR evaluations). VQE used a hardware-efficient ansatz with Ry rotations and CZ entanglers (layers p, number of parameters µ = n(1+p)); reported VQE experiments typically used p=1. QAOA used depths p in [1..6] (2p parameters). Classical optimiser: COBYLA. Maximum optimizer iterations set to 66 × (number of parameters). Normalised optimiser iterations reported (objective evaluations divided by number of parameters).

**Dataset:** Synthetic datasets generated by the authors: Max-Cut unweighted graphs (regular and random non-regular) with 15–19 vertices generated via NetworkX; Number Partitioning instances of 17–20 integers sampled uniformly from sets N1={0..200}, N2={0..500}, N3={0..750} (and a smaller set M={0..50} for some QAOA tests); Portfolio optimisation instances with 16–20 assets where expected returns µ and covariance Σ were randomly generated (budget B sampled uniformly from {0..n}, risk parameter q varied).
## Experiment details
### Input
Source: synthetic/random instance generation. Sizes: Max-Cut: 100 random instances, 15–19 vertices; Number Partitioning: 300 instances of 17–20 integers from three value ranges (N1,N2,N3); Portfolio: 100 random portfolios with 16–20 assets. Preprocessing: all problems mapped to QUBO/Ising form via xi = (1 - zi)/2 and promoted to Pauli-σz Hamiltonians; portfolio budget constraint implemented as a quadratic penalty term. Instance generation used NetworkX for graphs and uniform sampling for numbers/portfolio parameters; no external/real financial datasets used.

### Process
Pipeline: (1) generate synthetic problem instance; (2) map cost to Ising Hamiltonian HC; (3) choose ansatz: hardware-efficient VQE (Ry rotations + CZ entanglers) or QAOA (alternating e^{-iγHC} and e^{-iβHB}); (4) initialise parameters θ randomly; (5) for each optimisation iteration: prepare state U(θ)|0>^n, run K shots (K=1000) on Qiskit Aer, measure in computational basis, compute energies for each outcome, sort energies, compute CVaRα as the average over smallest α fraction of samples; (6) for Ascending-CVaR, update α according to schedule (linear: α_{t+1} = α_t + λ with λ in [0.025,0.045]; or sigmoid: α_t = 1/(1+exp(5-λ t)) with λ in [0.3,0.4]); scale shot budget as K/α for accuracy when using α truncation; (7) update parameters with COBYLA to minimise current CVaRα (or fixed-α CVaR / expectation-value baseline) until stopping or max iterations (66 × #parameters); (8) report metrics (overlap with ground state, success rate, approximation ratio, normalised iterations). Experiments repeated across many random instances and statistics summarised.

### Output
Outputs: per-instance and aggregated metrics: overlap with optimal computational-basis solution (probability mass of ground-state(s)), success rate (fraction of runs achieving ≥10% overlap), approximation ratio, average overlap, average normalised optimiser iterations and circuit repetitions to reach threshold. Baselines: constant CVaR at α={0.1,0.2,0.5} and expectation-value (α=1). Reported comparisons show Ascending-CVaR often achieves higher overlap, higher success rates, and comparable or faster convergence (in normalised iterations) than baselines.

## Findings
- [supported] Introducing an evolving (time-dependent) objective function called Ascending-CVaR that starts from a small CVaR α and gradually increases α up to 1 during classical optimisation.
- [supported] In noiseless emulation (up to 20 qubits) Ascending-CVaR produces higher overlap with the optimal computational-basis solution than both fixed-α CVaR and the standard expectation-value objective across tested problems (Max-Cut, Number Partitioning, Portfolio Optimisation).
- [supported] Ascending-CVaR often achieves higher success rates (defined as reaching ≥10% overlap with the optimal solution) and in many cases reaches the success threshold in fewer normalized optimiser iterations than fixed-α CVaR or expectation-value objectives.
- [supported] For the three tested problems and ansatz choices, VQE with a hardware-efficient ansatz outperforms QAOA at the small/intermediate depths used in the experiments; Ascending-CVaR improves performance for both VQE and QAOA but the gains are larger and more practically relevant for VQE.
- [supported] Empirically identified reasonable hyperparameter ranges for the ascending schedule: linear-ascending factor λ approximately in [0.025, 0.045] and sigmoid-ascending factor λ approximately in [0.3, 0.4] (on the problem sizes studied).
- [supported] Numerical experiments show especially large relative gains on harder instances (e.g., some Number Partitioning instances and Portfolio instances) where fixed objectives frequently fail.
- [speculative] Theoretical propositions (discussed in the paper) that CVaRα with α ≤ κ share the same global minimum (ground state) if κ is the maximal attainable overlap, and that varying α changes local minima structure—motivation for an evolving α to help escape suboptimal minima.
- [speculative] Suggesting dynamic (evolving) objective functions are a promising general direction to improve variational quantum optimisation and may serve as heuristics to avoid suboptimal minima in other problems/ansatze.
- [supported] Demonstrated that choice of ascending schedule (linear vs sigmoid vs other tested shapes) matters empirically; linear and sigmoid schedules performed best on the tested instances.

**Results summary:** This preprint proposes Ascending-CVaR, an evolving objective for variational quantum optimisation that starts with a small CVaR tail and gradually increases the CVaR parameter α toward the full expectation value. In noiseless classical emulation (up to 20 qubits) on Max-Cut, Number Partitioning and Portfolio Optimisation instances, Ascending-CVaR outperformed fixed-α CVaR and the standard expectation-value objective in overlap with the optimal computational-basis solutions and in empirical success rates (defined as achieving ≥10% sampling probability of the optimal bitstring). The authors also report that VQE with a hardware-efficient ansatz benefited more (and achieved much higher overlaps) than QAOA at the shallow depths considered. They provide empirical guidance for schedule hyperparameters and argue theoretically that varying α can change local minima structure, helping the optimiser escape suboptimal minima.

## Limitations
- Measurement cost increases with small α: evaluating CVaRα requires scaling the number of circuit runs by 1/α (K → K/α), increasing total circuit repetitions and sampling cost.
- Need to tune ascending hyperparameters: the ascending factor (λ) and the choice of ascending function must be chosen per problem/instance; authors report ranges that worked empirically but no general rule.
- Limited problem sizes and noiseless simulation: experiments were done in a noiseless emulation environment (Qiskit Aer) up to 20 qubits, so performance on larger systems and on real noisy hardware is untested.
- Restricted circuit depths: QAOA evaluations focused on small depths (p up to 6) and VQE mainly used shallow (p=1) circuits; behaviour at larger depths or in deeper circuits is not explored.
- QAOA underperformance at small depth: QAOA with small p produced low-overlap states compared to VQE; the approach may be less effective when only low-depth QAOA is feasible.
- Lack of theoretical guidance: there is no theoretical analysis or guarantees for how to choose the ascending schedule (function or λ) or for why and when Ascending-CVaR will reliably avoid local minima.
- Dependence on ansatz and optimizer: results are shown mainly for hardware-efficient VQE ansatz and COBYLA optimiser; sensitivity to different ansatze or optimisation methods is not analysed in depth.
- Choice of success threshold is heuristic: the 10% overlap threshold used to characterise 'success' is arbitrary and may affect conclusions about comparative performance.
- Increased total runtime trade-offs: while the method sometimes converged in fewer iterations (normalised), passing through multiple α values may involve additional computation and measurement overhead in practice.
- [inferred] Scalability concerns: behaviour, resource costs, and hyperparameter tuning demands for much larger qubit counts (>>20 qubits) are unknown and may differ substantially.
- [inferred] Noise robustness untested: because only noiseless simulations were used, it is unknown how Ascending-CVaR performs under realistic device noise and sampling error.
- [inferred] Generality beyond tested problems: only three combinatorial problems (Max-Cut, Number Partitioning, Portfolio Optimisation) were evaluated; generalisation to other problem classes is unproven.
- [inferred] Adaptive/automatic schedule selection absent: the approach relies on a preset ascending schedule; no adaptive mechanism based on optimisation feedback was provided.
- [inferred] Possible interaction with barren plateaus: potential effects of dynamic objective functions on barren plateau phenomena were not analysed.
- [inferred] Measurement-shot practicalities: scaling K by 1/α may be impractical for very small α in real devices due to time and noise accumulation.
