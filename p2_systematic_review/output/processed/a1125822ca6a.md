## Abstract summary
This preprint introduces the Maximum Amplification Optimisation Algorithm (MAOA), a quantum algorithm for combinatorial optimisation on near-term devices that constructs states which maximally amplify optimal solutions subject to restricted circuit depth, avoiding the costly variational tuning of QAOA. It connects the approach to Grover search, proposes a restricted Grover adaptive search (RGAS) baseline, and presents simulations on vehicle routing and portfolio optimisation demonstrating that MAOA outperforms RGAS and classical random sampling, with speedup that scales linearly with achievable circuit depth.
## Methodology
The authors develop and analyse the Maximum Amplification Optimisation Algorithm (MAOA) within the Quantum Walk Optimisation Algorithm (QWOA) framework and relate its dynamics to truncated Grover search. They show analytically that using a binary marking function on a complete graph and repeatedly applying the phase shift γ = π and walk time t = π/N yields maximal amplitude amplification of marked (high-quality) solutions for restricted circuit depth (rotation count r). They then implement numerical experiments (simulations) to compare MAOA performance with the Grover Adaptive Search (GAS), a restricted-circuit variant (RGAS), and classical random sampling. Simulations use precomputed solution-quality distributions (synthetic data for a capacitated vehicle routing problem and real financial data for a portfolio optimisation problem, plus an idealised large normal distribution) and a probabilistic Grover-amplitude model: P(r, ρ) = sin((2r+1)arcsin(sqrt(ρ)))^2. The MAOA contains a threshold-finding procedure that navigates threshold-response curves by starting at r=1 (sampling median from a small random sample), iteratively finding peaks and doubling r until the user-specified r_f is reached, then performing an adaptive search to select a threshold that lies in the low-convergence regime (P(r, ρ) ≤ 1/40). Once the threshold is found the maximally amplified state is repeatedly prepared and measured. Optimization of variational parameters (where done for comparison/analysis) uses Nelder–Mead from multiple random initializations. Experiment outputs are success-probability-vs-computational-effort curves (10,000 Monte Carlo trials per experiment), amplification factors, and analytic expressions for expected speedup (lim_{μ→0} e_C/e_Q = 2r+1). Numerical resources included Pawsey Supercomputing Centre resources and in-house QWOA simulation software (unnamed).

**Algorithms used:** MAOA (Maximum Amplification Optimisation Algorithm), Grover adaptive search (GAS), Restricted Grover adaptive search (RGAS), QWOA / QAOA (discussed and used as conceptual baseline), Grover's algorithm (truncated / amplitude amplification), Nelder–Mead (classical optimizer for parameter tuning)
**Frameworks:** QWOA (Quantum Walk Optimisation Algorithm framework), Grover amplitude-amplification framework, QAOA (alternating-operator ansatz) — discussed as baseline

**Experimental setup:** Numerical simulations (no physical QPU). Solution-quality distributions are precomputed for each problem instance. Simulations model Grover-amplitude dynamics by using the closed-form probability P(r,ρ)=sin((2r+1)arcsin(√ρ))^2 and assume each Grover-phase application requires two calls to the objective (quality) function; cost per state preparation and measurement is taken as 2r+1. Monte Carlo ensembles of 10,000 simulation runs are used to produce success-probability vs computational-effort curves. Computational resources: Pawsey Supercomputing Centre and local/ in-house QWOA simulation software.

**Dataset:** Portfolio optimisation: daily adjusted close prices from 2019-01-01 to 2020-12-31 for 20 ASX stocks (ASX20 subset listed in paper). Vehicle routing: synthetically generated instance (randomly generated depot/location costs and demand vector). Also idealised standard normal distribution used to emulate arbitrarily large problems.

## Findings
- [supported] The paper introduces the Maximum Amplification Optimisation Algorithm (MAOA), which prepares quantum states that aim to maximally amplify optimal (or near-optimal) solutions subject to a user-specified restricted circuit depth, and then repeatedly samples that state.
- [supported] For a binary marking of solutions on the complete graph the QWOA/Grover-style alternating operators with parameters γ = π and t = π/N are equivalent to Grover rotations; repeated application gives the same amplitude amplification as r Grover rotations.
- [supported] In the low-convergence regime the marked-set measurement probability scales approximately as PLC(r,ρ) = ρ(2r + 1)^2, so the amplitude amplification factor is (2r + 1)^2 and the asymptotic speedup (effort ratio) over classical random sampling is ≈ 2r + 1.
- [supported] The MAOA is able to locate a threshold (marking function) that places the problem into the low-convergence regime by iteratively doubling the rotation count and doing peak-finding on threshold-response curves, avoiding high-dimensional variational parameter searches.
- [supported] The authors implemented a restricted-circuit variant of Grover Adaptive Search (RGAS) as a baseline; both MAOA and RGAS outperform naive classical random sampling in their simulations, and MAOA consistently outperforms RGAS at restricted depths.
- [supported] Numerical simulations on specific combinatorial instances (a 10-location capacitated vehicle routing problem, N ≈ 5.8941e7, and a 20-asset portfolio selection problem, N ≈ 6.17576e7) show the MAOA and RGAS produce success-probability vs effort curves that lie significantly above classical random sampling for the tested rotation counts.
- [supported] The authors numerically verify that the derived amplification law ((2r + 1)^2) and the speedup (2r + 1) are observed in their simulated thresholded/marked complete-graph models up to the tested r regimes.
- [speculative] The paper claims MAOA represents the upper limit of speedup available within the class of deterministic amplitude-amplification algorithms under restricted circuit depth (i.e., within algorithms built from alternating phase-shift and diffusion/mixing operators).
- [supported] The analysis and simulations indicate that optimising expectation value (the typical QAOA objective) can favour amplifying numerous suboptimal solutions rather than maximally amplifying the true optimal solutions under restricted depth, motivating MAOA's focus on direct amplification of optima.
- [speculative] The MAOA approach can be generalized to multi-dimensional thresholding (e.g., fixing risk then maximizing return) by sequential threshold finding; the method is claimed to be flexible for multi-criteria problems.

**Results summary:** The paper presents the MAOA, an algorithm designed for near-term (restricted-depth) quantum optimisation which replaces high-dimensional variational tuning with a binary-threshold + complete-graph strategy that is equivalent to truncated Grover rotations. Analytic expressions (and numerical simulations) show that in the low-convergence regime the marked-set probability scales as ρ(2r+1)^2 and implies an asymptotic effort-speedup factor ≈2r+1 over classical random sampling. Simulations on a vehicle routing instance (N≈5.9e7), a portfolio problem (N≈6.18e7), and modelled large normal-distribution instances show that MAOA outperforms a restricted Grover adaptive search (RGAS) and classical sampling under the tested rotation-count limits, and that the observed amplification matches the derived theoretical bound in the tested regimes.

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** theoretical

The paper demonstrates (through analytic derivations and classical simulations of idealised quantum operations) an algorithmic speedup over classical random sampling quantified as ≈(2r+1) in effort for restricted-depth amplitude-amplification protocols. This is a theoretical/simulation-level advantage (no hardware experiments), and the authors themselves limit the claim to the class of amplitude-amplification algorithms and to idealised models (complete-graph binary marking, Grover-equivalent operations).
## Limitations
- MAOA and analyses are restricted to the class of amplitude-amplification algorithms (alternating phase-shift and diffusion/mixing operators); the paper shows MAOA is optimal only within this class.
- The analysis and simulations do not account for the quantum walk / mixing operator implementation cost; the paper explicitly states that mixing/walk overheads are not included in the computational-effort comparisons.
- Simulations assume idealised Grover-like phase shifts and perfect implementation of the complete-graph quantum walk (e.g., parameters γ = π and t = π/N).
- The threshold-finding subroutine depends on statistical sampling assumptions (e.g., initial sampling to estimate a median and quantiles) and on navigating threshold-response curves; robustness to atypical or heavy-tailed quality distributions is not proven.
- The MAOA requires repeated calls to the objective (quality) function and treats objective-evaluation cost as the dominant cost metric; gate-level resource costs, ancilla usage, noise, decoherence and error-correction overheads are not modelled in the effort metric.
- The paper uses precomputed, exact quality distributions for the simulation studies (especially for RGAS evaluation); in practical, classically-intractable problems those distributions are unknown and must be estimated.
- GAS (and RGAS) comparisons assume a user-specified maximum rotation count derived from N; in practice the choice and randomness of rotation counts may be constrained differently on hardware.
- The MAOA’s threshold approach relies on being able to produce a binary marking function (thresholding) that meaningfully separates very small fractions of the best solutions; for instances where such thresholds do not exist or are extremely sparse, performance may degrade.
- [inferred] The paper does not model noise, gate errors, or limited qubit connectivity; the demonstrated speedups may be reduced or lost under realistic NISQ hardware constraints.
- [inferred] Efficient implementation of the complete-graph quantum walk (or Grover diffusion) for very large N on actual hardware may require significant resources or nontrivial compilation, which could negate some of the predicted speedup.
- [inferred] The threshold-finding algorithm assumes the final rotation count is a power of two and relies on heuristic parameters (e.g., 20 consecutive successes) — its sensitivity to those hyperparameters and failure modes is not fully characterised.
- [inferred] The method assumes that the objective function evaluation can be embedded into phase-oracle-like operations; constructing such oracles for complex finance payoffs may be expensive or infeasible.
- [inferred] Multi-dimensional optimisation is handled by sequential thresholding in the paper’s examples (fix risk then optimise return); scalability and generalisation to higher-dimensional, strongly coupled objectives is not demonstrated.
## Open questions
- How robust is the MAOA to realistic hardware noise, decoherence, finite gate fidelity, and restricted qubit connectivity on near-term devices?
- What is the full end-to-end cost (including mixing/walk implementation, gate counts, ancilla qubits, error mitigation/correction) of MAOA on realistic quantum hardware, and does the theoretical speedup survive when these costs are included?
- Can efficient, scalable circuits be constructed for the complete-graph quantum walk or equivalent Grover diﬀusion for very large N in practical quantum architectures?
- How well does the threshold-finding procedure perform when the true quality distribution deviates from the assumed/observed shapes (e.g., heavy tails, multimodality, strong skew) or when the distribution is only poorly sampled?
- Are there classes of optimisation instances (quality distributions, problem structures) for which MAOA performs poorly compared to variational or non-amplitude-amplification approaches?
- Can algorithms outside the amplitude-amplification framework (e.g., filtering variational quantum algorithms) provide better end-to-end speedup than MAOA when their variational overheads are included?
- How sensitive is MAOA to the choice of hyperparameters in the threshold-finding routine (sample sizes, consecutive-success thresholds, step sizes), and can these parameters be adapted theoretically?
- How to construct practical phase-oracles (quality-dependent phase shifts) for complex, real-world financial objective functions without prohibitive overhead?
- How does MAOA scale for truly large, multi-dimensional optimisation problems when thresholds must be found and tuned across several correlated objective dimensions?
- What are the failure modes and guarantees (if any) for MAOA when the low-convergence regime assumption (P(r,ρ) ≤ 1/40) does not hold or cannot be reliably achieved?

- #limitation:simulation-only — All results are from numerical simulation using analytic Grover-amplitude formulas (P(r,ρ)=sin((2r+1)arcsin(√ρ))^2) and Monte Carlo trials; no experiments on noisy QPUs or hardware emulators are reported.
- #limitation:no-empirical-validation — The paper provides no real-hardware runs or noise-inclusive simulations to validate performance under realistic device constraints.
- #limitation:noise — The cost and success-probability analyses assume ideal, noise-free amplitude amplification; hardware noise and error accumulation effects are not modelled.
- #limitation:data-encoding — The approach assumes objective-function oracle calls (counted as two calls per Grover phase) and relies on enumerated quality distributions; the practical cost of encoding/marking real-world financial objectives at scale is not addressed.
- #idea:quantum-advantage — The paper derives an analytic expected speedup expression (lim_{μ→0} e_C/e_Q = 2r+1) showing a linear advantage in the achievable rotation count r under the authors' cost model.
## Contradictions
- The paper's claim of near-term applicability conflicts with its reliance on full enumeration / precomputed solution-quality distributions for the tested instances (enumerating all feasible portfolios for 20 assets and all routes for a 10-location VRP) — this classical preprocessing is exponential and not feasible for large N, undermining practical scalability.
- The analytic speedup (e_C/e_Q = 2r+1) and simulation results assume an idealised cost model (each Grover phase = two objective calls) and noise-free amplitude amplification; these assumptions conflict with real-device constraints (noise, oracle construction costs), so the claimed scaling advantage may not hold in practice.
- MAOA is presented as a near-term alternative to variational methods (e.g., QAOA) yet all evidence is from ideal simulations; absence of noise modelling and hardware validation contradicts strong claims about NISQ-era feasibility.
