## Abstract summary
The paper develops a simple, transparent country-level banking sector resilience metric called the Consolidated Distance to Breakpoint (CDBP), based on individual-bank reverse stress tests that estimate the increase in non-performing loans required to deplete capital buffers for the most fragile banks representing at least 20% of system assets. Applied to public data for over 1,500 banks in 59 emerging market and developing economies during the COVID-19 pandemic, the CDBP appears to capture information complementary to standard macro-financial and financial soundness indicators, offering a pragmatic surveillance tool where detailed data are limited.
## Methodology
The paper develops a simple reverse stress testing framework to produce a country-level banking sector resilience metric (Consolidated Distance to Break Point, CDBP). The method models a credit shock as an increase in non-performing loans (NPLs) that triggers additional provisioning which is assumed to be absorbed by regulatory capital. For each bank the Break Point (BP) is computed as the NPL ratio (percent of gross loans) that reduces Total Regulatory Capital (TRC) down to the country minimum capital requirement (MCR), taking into account the impact of provisioning on capital and risk-weighted assets (RWA). The authors assume a 55% provisioning rate on newly non-performing exposures (average across substandard/doubtful/loss buckets), no collateral, and (in the baseline) no profits to absorb provisions. The Distance from Break Point (DBP) is defined per bank as max(BP − current NPL ratio, 0) (banks already undercapitalized have DBP=0). Banks are sorted by DBP (ties broken by total assets) and the subset of most fragile banks that together represent at least 20% of total banking system assets ("Banks at Risk") is selected. For these Banks at Risk the authors compute consolidated metrics: Consolidated Break Point (CBP) and the Consolidated Distance from Break Point (CDBP), weighting banks by gross loans. The CDBP is reported in percentage points of NPL increase required to deplete capital buffers of the Banks at Risk. The paper applies this procedure to bank-level balance-sheet data (≈1,500 commercial banks across 59 emerging/developing economies), and then relates country-level CDBP values to macro-financial and Financial Soundness Indicators via cross-sectional regressions and simple comparisons. Sensitivity checks include incorporating bank profits as an additional buffer and discussion of alternative parameter choices; the authors also document assumptions and limitations (no contagion modelling, no collateral, provisioning composition assumption, data coverage limitations).

**Dataset:** Bank-level balance-sheet data (Fitch) for approx. 1,500 commercial banks headquartered in 59 emerging market and developing economies (EMDEs), primarily Q3 2020 data (or latest available up to 2019). Key variables: NPL ratio (% gross loans), Total Regulatory Capital (USD), Risk-weighted Assets (USD), Gross Loans (USD), Total Assets (USD), and country Minimum Capital Requirement (from World Bank BRSS, 2016).
## Experiment details
### Input
Source: Fitch bank balance-sheet database (primary), World Bank Bank Regulation and Supervision Survey (minimum capital requirement, 2016). Sample size: ~1,500 banks across 59 non-high-income countries. Time coverage: Q3 2020 where available, otherwise latest balance-sheet data not older than 2019. Preprocessing: compute or collect key fields listed above; convert values to USD; compute aggregate banking system totals per country; for banks with equal DBP use total assets to break ties; weight banks by gross loans when aggregating CBP/CDBP.

### Process
Stepwise pipeline: 1) For each bank compute BP by solving the accounting identity where additional provisioning from a rise in NPLs reduces TRC to the MCR threshold; provisioning on new NPLs assumed 55% and provisioning reduces both capital and RWA (formulas provided). 2) Compute DBP = max(BP − actual NPL ratio, 0). 3) Sort banks in each country by DBP ascending; select the smallest-DBP banks until cumulative total assets ≥ 20% of country banking system assets (Banks at Risk). 4) For Banks at Risk calculate consolidated CBP and CDBP by weighting bank-level BP and DBP by gross loans. 5) Produce country-level CDBP distribution and conduct cross-sectional regressions relating CDBP to Financial Soundness Indicators (FSIs) and macro-fiscal variables. 6) Conduct sensitivity checks, e.g., include bank profits as an offset to provisioning (modified BP equation), vary assumptions as discussed. No iterative numerical optimization beyond solving closed-form algebraic expressions per bank is required.

### Output
Primary outputs: bank-level BP and DBP (NPL % and percentage-point distances), country-level CBP and CDBP (percentage-point increase in consolidated NPLs required to deplete capital buffers of Banks at Risk), distributions across regions, and cross-sectional regression results (CDBP regressed on FSIs). Format: tabular bank-level and country-level metrics; regression coefficients with standard errors and R-squared reported. Baselines/sensitivity: alternative CDBP computed including bank profits; comparisons with external indicators (EIU banking risk index, GDP growth forecasts, government gross debt).

## Findings
- [supported] Reverse stress tests can be a useful tool to evaluate bank resilience to credit shocks, especially where financial data are limited or opaque.
- [supported] The paper develops a simple, transparent country-level banking sector resilience metric called the Consolidated Distance to Breakpoint (CDBP).
- [supported] The CDBP measures the percentage-point increase in non-performing loans (NPLs) that would deplete capital buffers for the subset of most fragile banks that together represent at least 20% of banking system assets.
- [supported] The authors computed the CDBP using publicly available balance-sheet data for more than 1,500 banks in 59 emerging market and developing economies (EMDEs) around the COVID-19 pandemic period.
- [supported] The CDBP has only modest (weak) association with commonly used macro-financial and financial soundness indicators, implying it captures complementary information.
- [supported] Empirical results show substantial cross-country and regional variation in CDBP values (median CDBP = 9.8 percentage points across the sample).
- [supported] In the sample, 10 countries would see 'Banks at Risk' become undercapitalized after a 5 percentage-point increase in NPLs; two countries had 'Banks at Risk' already undercapitalized.
- [supported] Regional findings reported: South Asia had the lowest median CDBP (~3.8 pp) while Sub-Saharan Africa showed a high median and wide dispersion.
- [supported] CDBP correlates weakly with the Economist Intelligence Unit (EIU) banking risk indicator.
- [supported] Regression analysis indicates positive association between CDBP and liquidity buffer indicators and negative association with cost-of-intermediation; overall explanatory power of standard Financial Soundness Indicators (FSIs) for CDBP is low (R-squared modest).
- [supported] Incorporating bank profits as an initial buffer in the BP computation yields very similar CDBP values (pairwise correlation 0.953 with the baseline approach).
- [supported] The CDBP framework is intentionally focused on credit risk and omits other risk types (FX, liquidity, sovereign nexus), contagion effects, likelihood/timing of shocks, and may be affected by underreported NPLs and incomplete bank coverage.
- [speculative] The CDBP could serve as a useful, low-data-input complement to existing country-level indicators and as a first-step screening tool to flag banking sectors requiring more detailed supervisory review.
- [speculative] The 20%-of-assets threshold for selecting 'Banks at Risk' is justified by literature that associates distress of banks representing 20% of assets with systemic banking crises, but the threshold could be adapted for country-specific banking sector structure.

**Results summary:** The paper introduces the Consolidated Distance to Breakpoint (CDBP), a reverse stress-testing based metric that quantifies how much non-performing loans must increase to exhaust capital buffers of the most vulnerable banks representing at least 20% of system assets. Applied to >1,500 banks in 59 EMDEs around the COVID-19 episode, the median CDBP is 9.8 percentage points, with strong cross-country and regional variation (e.g., South Asia low, Sub-Saharan Africa high dispersion). The CDBP shows only weak correlations with standard financial soundness indicators and the EIU banking risk index, suggesting it captures complementary information focused on tail vulnerabilities. Including profits as an absorbing buffer produces very similar results. The authors emphasize the metric's simplicity and data-frugality but note important limitations: focus on credit risk only, no contagion/interdependence modeling, reliance on potentially underreported NPLs, and lack of probabilistic/timing interpretation. They propose the CDBP as a rough screening tool to inform further supervisory investigation.

## Limitations
- Does not capture interdependence structure among banks (no contagion, knock-on effects, or network/second-round effects).
- Focuses only on credit risk; excludes other important bank risks (liquidity, foreign exchange, market, sovereign-bank nexus).
- Provides no estimate of the likelihood or timing (time horizon) of the NPL increase required to reach the break point.
- Relies on NPL data that are backward-looking, lagged, and may be underreported—data quality and transparency issues.
- Sample coverage may be incomplete (missing unlisted banks, mutual banks, state-owned banks, small rural institutions, and other credit institutions).
- Assumes provisioning is fully absorbed by capital (no profits available to offset provisions) — a conservative assumption that may overstate vulnerability.
- Assumes a uniform NPL provisioning rate (55%) and ignores collateral values and supervisory haircuts.
- Assumes minimum capital requirements are homogeneous and does not incorporate bank-specific additional capital buffers (e.g., conservation buffers, systemic buffers) or other regulatory constraints (e.g., leverage ratio).
- Ignores tax implications (tax credits or deferred tax assets) that could mitigate capital impacts.
- Does not model changes in risk-weighted assets aside from a simplified treatment, potentially missing complexity in RWA dynamics.
- Uses a fixed 20%-of-assets threshold to define ‘Banks at Risk’, which may not be appropriate for all jurisdictions or banking sector granularities.
- Aggregates by gross-loan weights and focuses on weakest tail banks, which may obscure different forms of systemic risk emerging from concentrated exposures or large single institutions.
- Empirical associations with standard FSIs are modest and explanatory power is low, leaving open the question of predictive validity for crises.
- [inferred] Sensitive to modelling choices and parameter assumptions (provisioning rate, risk-weight density, treatment of profits, selection of threshold), which can materially affect results.
- [inferred] Potential selection and reporting biases due to reliance on publicly available commercial data sources (Fitch) and use of latest available balance sheets (some from 2019).
- [inferred] May underperform in banking systems where non-credit risks drive fragility (e.g., FX mismatches, sovereign exposure) because these are not modeled.
## Open questions
- How can interbank linkages, contagion channels, and non-linear systemic dependencies be incorporated into the reverse stress-testing/CDBP framework?
- How sensitive are CDBP results to key assumptions (provisioning percentage, treatment of collateral, inclusion of profits, risk weight assumptions, and the 20% assets threshold)?
- How should the framework be extended to capture other shock types (foreign exchange shocks, sovereign stress, liquidity runs, market risk) and combined/multi-factor shocks?
- Can the approach be adapted to attach probabilities or likely time horizons to the NPL increases it computes?
- How should temporary regulatory forbearance and policy support measures (debt moratoria, loan classification relaxations) be treated to avoid understating true vulnerabilities?
- How to incorporate bank-specific capital buffers and additional regulatory constraints (capital conservation buffers, systemic buffers, leverage ratios) into the calculations?
- What methods can improve sample coverage to include non-listed and small/heterogeneous institutions, and how would that affect the CDBP?
- Does the CDBP provide incremental early-warning or predictive power for systemic banking crises beyond standard macro-financial and FSI indicators?
- How could tax effects (e.g., use of tax credits or deferred tax assets) be accommodated in the stress-test impact on regulatory capital?
- What validation exercises (backtesting against historical crises) are appropriate to assess the reliability and calibration of CDBP as a surveillance metric?
- How to refine the treatment of RWA impacts when loans migrate into non-performing status (more granular modeling of risk weights)?
- [inferred] How robust are results across different data providers and vintages (e.g., 2019 vs 2020 balance sheets) and what is the impact of data lags?

**Future work:**
- Complement the CDBP with additional stress tests and with supervisory, on-site, and qualitative information (asset quality reviews, on-site inspections).
- Adjust and relax core assumptions (e.g., include profits as a first buffer, vary provisioning rates, account for collateral) to reflect country-specific conditions and test robustness.
- Develop extensions to incorporate other risk types (liquidity, FX, sovereign) and potential contagion channels.
- Use the metric as a screening tool to trigger more in-depth supervisory reviews and targeted analyses of vulnerable banks.
- Perform robustness checks and alternative specifications (e.g., different thresholds for 'Banks at Risk', alternative weighting schemes, inclusion of bank-level buffers).
- Investigate the empirical relationship and incremental informational value of CDBP relative to standard macro-financial and FSI indicators and explore its predictive power for systemic events.
