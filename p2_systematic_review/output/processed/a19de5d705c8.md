## Abstract summary
The paper introduces a quantum option pricing model that incorporates quantum walk theory into the binomial tree framework by using quantum probability amplitudes (superposition states) instead of classical probabilities under a risk-neutral assumption. It derives one-step and multi-step quantum binomial tree pricing formulas and shows via numerical experiments that the quantum model produces option prices very close to classical binomial/Black–Scholes results while capturing additional quantum-style dynamics.
## Methodology
The paper develops a theoretical quantum-extension of the binomial tree option pricing model. Methodologically, the authors model a one-step market in the two-dimensional Hilbert space C^2 by associating the upward and downward stock-return states with the standard basis |↑> and |↓>. They define an observable return operator R = b|↑><↑| + a|↓><↓| and assume the pre-measurement market state |ψ> = β|↑> + α|↓> with density ρ = |ψ><ψ|. Imposing the risk-neutral condition <R> = r yields constraints on |α|^2 and |β|^2, producing the analogues of up/down probabilities without assuming them a priori. The one-step option payoff is represented as an operator H = (S0(1+R) - K I)+, and the current option price is computed as the discounted quantum expectation C = (1+r)^{-1} tr(ρ H). The approach is then extended to an N-step model by forming tensor-product states |ψ_N> = |ψ_1>^{⊗N}, expressing S_N and H_N as tensor products, defining ρ_N = |ψ_N><ψ_N|, and evaluating tr(ρ_N H_N) using combinatorial sums (binomial coefficients / Maxwell–Boltzmann counting) to obtain closed-form binomial-sum pricing formulas. The final N-step quantum pricing formula has the same structural form as the classical binomial sum but with modified probabilities q = (r-a)/(b-a) and q' = q(1+b)/(1+r). The paper also includes a numerical simulation comparison (classical Cox–Ross–Rubinstein binomial model vs. the proposed quantum binomial model) using a fixed parameter set (e.g., S0=100, K=100, r=0.05, T=1, N=100, and specified a,b or u,d) to illustrate numerical proximity between the models.

## Contradictions
- The paper derives quantum pricing formulas that are structurally equivalent to classical binomial sums and numerically yield prices very close to CRR/Black–Scholes; this undermines any strong claim that the quantum formulation by itself delivers clear pricing superiority or computational advantage.
- No computational speedup or hardware-based advantage is demonstrated—results are analytic and classically simulated—so the novelty is representational (a quantum formalism) rather than evidencing improved performance versus classical methods.
