## Abstract summary
This paper proposes an end-to-end quantum circuit approach for pricing rainbow (multi-asset) options using Iterative Quantum Amplitude Estimation, emphasizing an efficient strategy that delays the transition from return space to price space. It analyzes two amplitude-loading techniques for exponential functions (direct exponential loading and integration-based loading), provides complexity (T-depth) analysis, and validates the approach with simulations on the IBM QASM simulator using up to 24 qubits.
## Methodology
The authors implement an end-to-end quantum-circuit pipeline to price rainbow (best-of) call options by estimating the expected discounted payoff with Iterative Quantum Amplitude Estimation (IQAE). They prepare asset log-returns in return space as discretized Gaussians, impose correlations via a classical Cholesky decomposition and affine quantum transformations, then compute the maximum across assets directly in return space (using an arithmetic maximum module). They delay the expensive exponential transition to price space until the payoff stage: after computing the maximum in return space they evaluate the payoff branch condition (comparing z against a threshold derived from log(K)) and, depending on the comparator outcome, either (i) load a constant payoff amplitude for the below-threshold branch or (ii) load an exponential function into an auxiliary qubit amplitude for the above-threshold branch. Two amplitude-loading strategies are developed and compared: (a) direct exponential amplitude loading (controlled Ry rotations per x-bit with an MCX to transfer the combined |0⟩ probabilities to the target amplitude) and (b) integration amplitude loading (prepare an exponential state g(r) and use a register-to-register comparator to accumulate g(r) up to x yielding ˜h(x)). The target-qubit amplitude encodes a scaled payoff ˜f(x), and IQAE is run to estimate the probability of measuring |1⟩ of the target qubit; classical post-processing reconstructs the expected payoff and performs discounting. The implementation uses the Classiq Platform for high-level functional construction and optimization, and experiments are executed on the IBM QASM noiseless simulator for validation versus a classical baseline. The method reports resources (qubit counts) and analyzes T-depth for the payoff subroutines under different amplitude-loading methods.

**Algorithms used:** Iterative Quantum Amplitude Estimation (IQAE), Amplitude Estimation (without Phase Estimation paradigm), Gaussian state preparation (discretized normal distributions), Exponential state preparation, Direct exponential amplitude loading, Integration amplitude loading (comparator-based integration), Classical Cholesky decomposition for correlations, Quantum comparator and arithmetic modules (max, affine transforms), Multi-controlled X (MCX) and Toffoli-based constructions
**Frameworks:** Classiq Platform, IBM QASM simulator

**Experimental setup:** Experiments run on the IBM QASM noiseless simulator using circuits compiled/constructed with the Classiq Platform. Demonstration used up to 24 qubits, 1000 shots per experiment, IQAE precision ε = 0.01 and confidence α = 0.05. Two qubits per Gaussian distribution (m = 2) were used; arithmetic rounded to one decimal place; an exponential register sized to match the arithmetic output was included; one target qubit encoded the payoff amplitude. Strike K = $190 and maturity = 250 days.

**Dataset:** Two-asset financial example with initial prices S0 = [$193.97, $189.12]; mean daily log-returns µ = [5.096e-4, 6.255e-4] and covariance matrix entries given ([3.35, 2.57]·10^-4 ; [2.57, 4.18]·10^-4). Data described as real-world historical returns but the precise external data source is not specified in the paper.

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** theoretical

The paper relies on the established theoretical quadratic improvement in sample complexity offered by Amplitude Estimation (1/M vs 1/√M) and argues that careful circuit design (e.g., delaying price conversion and bespoke amplitude-loading) reduces arithmetic overhead and T-depth, potentially lowering the threshold for a practical advantage. However, no experimental demonstration of a practical quantum advantage on hardware is provided; validation is limited to noiseless simulator runs and analytic resource estimates.
## Limitations
- Experiments were run on an ideal (noiseless) IBM QASM simulator rather than on real noisy quantum hardware.
- Proof-of-concept implementation limited to two assets and up to 24 qubits (coarse discretization: two qubits per Gaussian), restricting resolution and realism.
- End-to-end resource estimation is not provided; the paper analyzes T-depth only for the payoff block and explicitly excludes the asset state preparation and arithmetic maximum modules.
- The T-depth and gate-depth analysis assumes availability of many ancilla qubits (optimistic/unlimited-qubits model) which may not hold on near-term devices.
- The direct exponential amplitude-loading method is applicable only to exponential functions; it is not general.
- The integration amplitude-loading method requires monotonic functions that are strictly positive or strictly negative (or a variational state-prep for the difference function), limiting generality.
- The delayed transition to price space requires post-processing that amplifies estimation infidelity; the scaling of this amplification depends on problem parameters and differs between the two loading methods.
- [inferred] No noise modelling, error mitigation, or analysis of robustness to realistic gate errors and decoherence was performed.
- [inferred] Scalability to larger numbers of assets, increased resolution, and practical problem sizes (where quantum advantage could matter) was not demonstrated.
- [inferred] Practical implementability of the amplitude-loading blocks (controlled rotations, MCX implementations) on constrained hardware with limited ancillas and noisy gates remains uncertain.
- [inferred] The choice of discretization granularity, fixed-point encoding, and rounding may materially affect accuracy and error amplification, but was only explored at a coarse level in experiments.
## Open questions
- How will the proposed algorithms and amplitude-loading techniques behave on noisy, near-term quantum hardware, including the impact of realistic gate errors and limited qubit counts?
- What are the full end-to-end resource requirements (qubit count, gate counts, T-depth, wall-clock time) for practical problem sizes (many assets, fine discretization) required to realize a quantum advantage?
- For which problem instances and parameter regimes (strike price, asset correlations, time-to-maturity, discretization) is the delayed transition-to-price-space strategy most beneficial, and when does it degrade accuracy due to infidelity amplification?
- Under which conditions (precision targets, available ancillas, circuit depth constraints) is the direct exponential loading preferable to the integration method, and vice versa?
- Can the integration amplitude-loading approach be generalized efficiently (without costly variational training) to non-exponential monotonous functions or to functions that are not strictly monotonic?
- How does the choice of comparator and MCX implementations (especially under limited ancillas) affect practical T-depth and error rates, and can these be optimized further for real hardware?
- How sensitive are pricing results to discretization choices, fixed-point encoding precision, and rounding, particularly after the post-processing that amplifies errors?
- What noise-mitigation techniques and circuit optimizations (e.g., reduced T-depth decompositions) are most effective for the amplitude-loading primitives used here?
- How readily can the modules and strategies presented (delayed price transition, amplitude loading) be extended to more complex option types, especially path-dependent derivatives?
- Given the overhead of implementing the Grover-like operator (state preparation + payoff oracle), what are the realistic practical limits on achieving the theoretical quadratic sample complexity speedup in derivative pricing?
