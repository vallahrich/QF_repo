## Abstract summary
The paper develops a quantum-inspired framework for economics that replaces classical utility with a probabilistic propensity function, using quantum probability and simple quantum circuits to model decision-making phenomena such as order effects, the disjunction effect, and preference reversal. It links propensity distributions to entropic forces and a quantum harmonic-oscillator analogy to derive dynamics, masses, and energies for economic transactions, and discusses applications to financial modelling including option pricing and market behavior.
## Methodology
This is a theoretical and conceptual study that develops a quantum-inspired mathematical framework for modelling economic decision-making. The authors construct and analyse simple quantum circuits (notably a two-qubit circuit with local rotations and a C-NOT gate) to represent context-dependent binary decision processes (e.g. coin toss, order effects, disjunction effect, preference reversal and prisoner’s dilemma). They derive probability outcomes using quantum probability (2-norm, complex amplitudes) and show equivalence between sequential one-qubit measurements in non-computational bases and an entangled two-qubit circuit (Appendix proof). The paper also maps continuous propensity functions to quantum wavefunctions: a normal propensity is associated with the ground state of a quantum harmonic oscillator, from which they derive entropic forces, an effective mass, and an energy scale for preference changes. The methodology is analytic and mathematical, combining quantum-information style circuit representations, linear-algebra/unitary matrix derivations, entropic-force formulas from statistical physics, and interpretive mapping between propensity distributions and quantum oscillator states. No empirical experiments, datasets, or numerical simulations are reported — the work is illustrative and derivational rather than experimental.

## Findings
- [speculative] Quantum probability (2-norm, complex amplitudes) is a natural and useful mathematical framework to model human decision-making and economic behavior, because it can represent superposition, interference, and entanglement.
- [speculative] Decision-makers can be modelled by a propensity function (probability of transacting) rather than by a classical utility function, and this propensity can be represented as the squared amplitude of an underlying wavefunction.
- [speculative] A simple two-qubit quantum circuit (unitary rotations + C-NOT) can reproduce the same outcome probabilities as sequential non-commuting measurements and can be used to simulate cognitive phenomena such as order effects, the disjunction effect, and preference reversal.
- [supported] The order effect in human judgments (responses depend on question order) has been empirically demonstrated in prior literature and can be represented in the quantum/sequential-projection formalism (cited: Wang et al. 2014).
- [supported] The preference-reversal threshold criterion (a switch from a more attractive to a less attractive option requires x2/x1 > 3) has been proposed and reported as empirically tested and robust in the cited literature (Yukalov & Sornette references).
- [speculative] Mapping propensity distributions to entropic forces provides a dynamical interpretation (entropic force F(x) = γ P'(x)/P(x)) for mental/transactional pressures toward preferred prices, enabling a dynamics-based view of supply/demand in consistent physical units.
- [speculative] A Gaussian (normal) propensity corresponds to the ground state of a quantum harmonic oscillator; this mapping yields an associated economic 'mass' m = ħ/(2 ω σ^2) (mass inversely related to variance) and a frequency ω that can be interpreted as resistance to change or mean reversion speed.
- [speculative] The information/energy required to change a decision (preference reversal of typical factor ~3) is on the order of the base quantum oscillator energy: ΔE ≈ ħ ω / 2 (derived theoretically in the paper).
- [speculative] Quantum models (propensity + entropic force + oscillator quantization) can in principle be applied to financial problems such as modeling return distributions, pricing, and option volume dynamics (citations to Ahn et al. 2017 and Orrell 2020a are provided).
- [speculative] Entanglement in quantum decision models naturally captures correlated, socially-influenced, or context-dependent behavior (e.g., prisoner’s dilemma-like social coupling) better than isolated utility-maximizer models.
- [speculative] The quantum-circuit representation provides a flexible template for encoding subjective context (gate A) and objective utility (gate B), enabling formal study of threshold and switch-like context effects in decisions.
- [speculative] Adopting a quantum propensity framework helps place subjective and objective factors on an equal footing and offers a route to quantify phenomena (e.g., 'mental energy' or social power) that are otherwise treated qualitatively.

**Results summary:** This preprint develops a theoretical framework for 'quantum propensity' in economics: replacing classical utility with a propensity function that can be represented by quantum probability amplitudes. The paper shows mathematically how simple quantum circuits (rotations and C-NOT) are equivalent to sequences of non-commuting measurements and can reproduce cognitive phenomena such as order effects, disjunction effects, and preference reversal. It further maps propensity distributions to entropic forces and proposes a quantization via the quantum harmonic oscillator, yielding interpretations for economic mass, resistance to change, and a base energy scale for decision flips. The approach is presented as broadly applicable to economic decision modeling and some financial problems, with links to prior empirical findings on order effects and preference-reversal thresholds in the literature.

- Economic 'mass' associated with a Gaussian propensity: m = ħ / (2 ω σ^2) (mass inversely proportional to variance).
## Quantum advantage claim

However, it does not present new empirical demonstrations of computational or practical quantum advantage in financial services (no benchmarked speedup or accuracy gain on operational financial tasks), so the asserted advantages remain theoretical/speculative rather than demonstrated.
## Limitations
- Conceptual/preliminary nature: the paper is largely theoretical and conceptual rather than providing extensive empirical validation.
- Scope limitation: examples and demonstrations are mainly small-scale (e.g., two-question/order-effect circuits) rather than large-scale market implementations.
- [inferred] Parameter interpretation: physical constants used in the mapping (e.g., ℏ, ω, temperature analogues, γ) lack clear operational definitions or measurement protocols in economic/social settings.
- [inferred] Calibration and estimation: no detailed method is given for empirically estimating propensity functions from observed market/behavioral data, or for fitting the quantum-oscillator parameters to real markets.
- [inferred] Normality assumption in example: the mapping from a normal propensity curve to the quantum harmonic oscillator ground state assumes normality; extensions to empirically observed non-normal propensities are not fully developed.
- Philosophical/normative objection acknowledged: some social scientists may object to quantifying social constructs such as power or mental energy, and the paper recognises this critique.
- [inferred] Scaling and complexity: the paper does not address computational or representational challenges of scaling quantum-circuit style models (interference/entanglement) to large numbers of agents or high-dimensional economic variables.
- [inferred] Empirical testing of entropic force/energy interpretation: while entropic-force and energy analogues are proposed, empirical tests confirming these interpretations in economics are not provided.
## Open questions
- How can propensity functions be estimated robustly from empirical market data or observed decision-making behaviour?
- What are the appropriate operational definitions and empirical values (or estimation procedures) for ℏ, ω, γ, and other parameters when mapping quantum-oscillator quantities to economic systems?
- How well do quantum-propensity models perform (predictively and explanatorily) compared with classical and behavioural models across real financial datasets?
- How should models be extended or adapted when propensity distributions are non-normal or multimodal?
- How can interference and entanglement effects among many agents be represented, estimated, and validated in realistic multi-agent economic settings?
- What empirical designs and datasets are best suited to detect and validate quantum-like interference effects (e.g., disjunction effect, order effects, preference reversal) in economic decisions at scale?
- To what extent is it legitimate or useful to quantify social constructs such as power or mental energy as energetic/propensity quantities in policy or normative analysis?
- How can the threshold/criterion results (e.g., preference reversal factor ≈ 3) be generalized, calibrated, and applied in operational economic or marketing contexts?
- What are the practical implications for market microstructure, price formation, and liquidity when agents' propensities are treated as quantum-like wavefunctions?
- Can the proposed quantum propensity models be implemented and tested on existing quantum computing hardware or simulated effectively on classical hardware for real-world financial-service applications?
