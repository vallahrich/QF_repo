## Abstract summary
Implements Iterative Amplitude Estimation (IAE) to price European call options under a log-normal model and compares results to Monte Carlo simulation and Black–Scholes benchmarks. Experiments on Qiskit simulators and IBM Quantum hardware show QAE estimates close to classical methods (deviations <0.02) but with wider confidence intervals due to discretization and hardware noise, while highlighting QAE's theoretical quadratic speedup (O(1/ε)).
## Methodology
The study prices a European call option under a Black–Scholes geometric Brownian motion model using three approaches: (1) analytical Black–Scholes closed-form pricing as a benchmark, (2) classical Monte Carlo simulation (risk-neutral sampling of ST from a log-normal GBM), and (3) a quantum approach based on Quantum Amplitude Estimation (QAE). The quantum implementation uses an Iterative Amplitude Estimation (IAE) variant suitable for NISQ devices: the terminal asset-price distribution is discretized over an interval covering ±3 standard deviations and amplitude-encoded into an n-qubit register; a controlled rotation on an ancilla embeds a scaled payoff f~(ST) into the ancilla amplitude; iterative Grover-like reflections are applied with increasing powers and classical post-processing (Clopper–Pearson intervals) to estimate the target amplitude a; the option price is recovered as C = exp(-rT) · a · B, where B is the payoff scaling factor. Experiments compare IAE on a Qiskit Aer simulator (2,000 shots per circuit, IAE max iterations = 16, 95% confidence) and on IBM Quantum hardware (ibm_brisbane) with hardware-friendly reductions (n = 3–4 qubits), and contrast results against Monte Carlo (N = 100,000, seed = 42) and the Black–Scholes analytical value. Evaluation metrics include absolute and relative error to the analytical value, 95% confidence intervals, and runtime/resources (shots, iterations). The methodology also documents state-preparation discretization, payoff normalization, and reproducibility choices (software libraries and seeds).

**Algorithms used:** Quantum Amplitude Estimation (QAE), Iterative Amplitude Estimation (IAE), Amplitude encoding, Grover-style reflections / amplitude amplification, Monte Carlo simulation, Black–Scholes analytical formula
**Frameworks:** Qiskit, Qiskit Finance, Qiskit Algorithms, Qiskit Aer, Qiskit Runtime, NumPy, SciPy

**Experimental setup:** Simulator: Qiskit Aer Sampler with 2,000 shots per circuit; IAE max iterations = 16; confidence level = 95%. Hardware: IBM Quantum backend ibm_brisbane via Qiskit Runtime, hardware-friendly configuration using 3–4 qubits for state encoding. Classical Monte Carlo baseline: N = 100,000 trials (seed = 42). Option parameters: S0 = 2.0, K = 2.0, r = 0.05, sigma = 0.2, T = 1.

**Dataset:** No real-market dataset. Synthetic/simulated data: terminal asset-price ST samples drawn from the log-normal distribution implied by a Geometric Brownian Motion under the risk-neutral measure. The distribution is discretized over an interval [L,H] covering ±3 standard deviations for amplitude encoding.

## Findings
- [supported] Iterative Amplitude Estimation (IAE) can be implemented to price a European call option and produces estimates consistent with Monte Carlo and Black–Scholes benchmarks.
- [supported] On Qiskit simulators, QAE achieved accuracy similar to Monte Carlo (reported absolute error ≈ 0.0080 vs Monte Carlo ≈ 0.0077).
- [supported] QAE executed on IBM hardware (ibm_brisbane) produced valid price estimates but with larger deviations and wider confidence intervals due to noise.
- [supported] Monte Carlo (N=100,000) produced a narrower 95% confidence interval (~0.0026) compared to QAE (CI ~0.015 on simulator, ~0.029 on hardware).
- [supported] Both Monte Carlo and QAE estimates were slightly higher than the analytical Black–Scholes price in the reported experiments.
- [supported] QAE preserved the structural (nonlinear) payoff characteristics of the option (payoff shape captured).
- [speculative] QAE provides an asymptotic quadratic speedup over classical Monte Carlo (O(1/ε) vs O(1/ε^2)) and therefore has potential scalability advantages for high-precision or high-dimensional problems.
- [speculative] Future improvements in qubit fidelity, circuit optimization, and error mitigation will make QAE practically advantageous for production-scale finance.
- [speculative] Techniques such as qGANs and hybrid quantum–classical architectures can improve state preparation and scalability for more realistic asset models.
- [supported] Current NISQ hardware limitations (limited qubit counts, short coherence, gate noise) materially widen QAE confidence intervals and limit numerical stability.

**Results summary:** The paper implements Iterative Amplitude Estimation for pricing a European call option and compares results to Monte Carlo (N=100,000) and the Black–Scholes analytic solution. On simulators, QAE achieves accuracy comparable to Monte Carlo (absolute errors ~0.008), while Monte Carlo yields tighter confidence intervals (~0.0026). Real-hardware runs on ibm_brisbane validate feasibility but show larger deviations and wider CIs (~0.029) due to noise and limited qubits (3–4 used for state encoding). The authors report that QAE with 16 iterations and 2,000 shots per circuit captures the payoff shape and produces estimates within acceptable error bounds (<0.02 reported), but practical scalability awaits improved hardware and error mitigation.

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** theoretical

The paper claims the known theoretical quadratic speedup of QAE (O(1/ε) vs Monte Carlo O(1/ε^2)) and demonstrates that QAE can achieve comparable accuracy with fewer quantum iterations in simulation; however, no practical speedup or resource advantage on real hardware is demonstrated due to NISQ noise and limited qubits, so the advantage remains theoretical/speculative in the present work.
## Limitations
- Practical deployment constrained by limited qubit coherence times, circuit depth, and cumulative error rates on NISQ devices (author-stated).
- Quantum hardware (ibm_brisbane) executions exhibit larger deviations and substantially wider confidence intervals due to noise and gate errors (author-stated).
- Finite discretization of the continuous log-normal distribution and small numbers of encoding qubits (n=3–4) produce discretization error and contribute to wider estimation intervals (author-stated).
- QAE results on current hardware are less numerically stable and have higher variance than classical Monte Carlo, limiting immediate production use (author-stated: 'not yet a substitute for Monte Carlo in production-scale finance').
- Qubit count and gate depth scale with the number of discretized asset states and with the complexity of amplitude encoding, limiting scalability to realistic/high-dimensional problems (author-stated).
- [inferred] Experiments are limited to small, single-asset European call instances (toy/problem-size small), so empirical conclusions may not generalize to high-dimensional, multi-asset, or path-dependent derivatives.
- [inferred] State-preparation (amplitude loading) overhead and associated circuit depth for realistic distributions are likely large and were not fully addressed or benchmarked in the experiments.
- [inferred] The study does not demonstrate the practical crossover point (resource threshold) where QAE outperforms classical Monte Carlo on real hardware; runtime and end-to-end cost comparisons are limited.
- [inferred] Error mitigation and noise-resilience techniques were not fully integrated or evaluated; thus robustness claims are preliminary.
- [inferred] Using a coarse discretization to keep circuits shallow may introduce bias in the estimated option price that was not exhaustively quantified.

## Key ideas

- #idea:near-term-feasibility — Experiments were run both on Qiskit Aer simulator and IBM Quantum hardware (ibm_brisbane) using hardware-friendly configurations (state register n = 3–4 qubits) demonstrating feasibility on NISQ devices but with degraded precision.
- #idea:hybrid-approach — The workflow combines quantum state preparation and amplitude estimation with classical preprocessing/postprocessing (discretization, payoff scaling, Clopper–Pearson intervals) and uses classical computation for benchmarking (Black–Scholes and Monte Carlo).
- #limitation:noise — Hardware QAE results show wider confidence intervals (~0.029) and increased error due to device noise versus simulator (~0.015) and Monte Carlo (~0.0026).
- #limitation:qubit-count — Practical hardware runs used only 3–4 state qubits (plus ancilla), limiting discretization granularity (grid size 2^n) and achievable accuracy.
- #limitation:data-encoding — The approach requires discretizing the log-normal terminal-price distribution and amplitude-encoding probabilities into the quantum register; this discretization and payoff scaling introduce approximation error and overhead.
- #idea:quantum-advantage — Despite absolute errors often < 0.02 relative to analytical Black–Scholes, the QAE confidence intervals are larger than classical Monte Carlo in these experiments, indicating the gap between theoretical speedup and current empirical performance.
## Contradictions
- contradiction:scalability — The paper presents the theoretical quadratic speedup of QAE (O(1/ε)) but its empirical results on simulator and real hardware show that discretization, limited qubit counts (3–4), and hardware noise produce wider confidence intervals and reduce practical advantage versus classical Monte Carlo (MC CI ~0.0026 vs simulator QAE ~0.015 vs hardware QAE ~0.029). This highlights a concrete tension between asymptotic theoretical claims and near-term/empirical scalability.
