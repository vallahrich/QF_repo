## Abstract summary
The paper reformulates portfolio optimization as a binary knapsack problem and proposes a hybrid quantum-classical approach that integrates a Quantum Walk Mixer with the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) and a QFT-based feasibility oracle to restrict the search to valid portfolios. Empirical simulations show high approximation ratios (≈95–100% for p ≥ 3) in noise-free settings, while experiments on noisy simulated and real IBM hardware reveal degradation due to gate errors and decoherence, highlighting hardware limitations and the need for error mitigation.
## Methodology
The authors reformulate a portfolio optimization task as a binary 0-1 knapsack problem by computing expected returns for candidate stocks using a Markowitz-style mean return estimator and mapping expected returns to item values, setting item weights uniformly to 1 and knapsack capacity to half the number of stocks. They design a quantum-enhanced optimization pipeline that combines a feasibility oracle (implemented using quantum addition in the Fourier basis via the Quantum Fourier Transform to compute total weights plus an offset, followed by a multi-controlled check) with a Quantum Walk Mixer (QWM) embedded into the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA). The mixing operator is implemented approximately using auxiliary qubits, controlled neighbor-mapping unitaries Vi and their inverses, single-qubit X operations, RX(2β) rotations, and trotterization to approximate e^{-iβB}. The phase (cost) unitary applies phases according to the knapsack value function. The variational parameters (γ, β) of a p-layer QAOA are optimized classically using the SHGO optimizer. The final state is measured to produce binary portfolio selections which are compared to classical best-known solutions (BKS) via an approximation ratio metric. The implementation is in Qiskit and experiments are run on a noise-free QASM simulator, a fake noisy backend (IBM FakeWashington), and a real IBM device (IBM Nazca). Empirical evaluations vary the number of stocks (2–8), circuit depth p and trotter steps m to study sensitivity and approximation performance under different noise conditions.

**Algorithms used:** Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA), Quantum Walk Mixer (QWM), Quantum Fourier Transform (QFT), Trotterization (Suzuki–Trotter approximation), SHGO (simplicial homology global optimizer, classical)
**Frameworks:** Qiskit, PyPortfolioOpt

**Experimental setup:** Implementation in Qiskit. Experiments run on QASM simulator (noise-free), on a fake noisy backend (IBM FakeWashington noise model, 127 qubits), and on a real IBM quantum device (IBM Nazca, 127 qubits). Classical optimizer: SHGO. Financial data sourced from Yahoo Finance. Typical QAOA configuration used: circuit depth p=3 and trotter steps m=3 (also varied m in 1–5 and p in 1–5 for sensitivity analysis).

**Dataset:** Historical daily stock prices from Yahoo Finance for selected tickers (examples used: AAPL, AMZN, GOOGL, MSFT, NVDA, TSLA, NFLX, V) covering Jan 1, 2018 to Jan 1, 2023.

## Findings
- [supported] The authors reformulate portfolio optimization (Markowitz expected returns) as a binary knapsack problem (values = expected returns, weights = 1, capacity = floor(N/2)) and implement this mapping.
- [supported] They propose a hybrid quantum-classical algorithm: a Quantum Walk Mixer combined with QAOA (QWM-QAOA) that uses a QFT-based feasibility oracle to restrict the search to feasible knapsack solutions.
- [supported] In noise-free simulation (QASM simulator) their QWM-QAOA achieved very high approximation ratios vs. the Best-Known Solution (BKS), reported as 100% for small cases and down to about 96% for an 8-stock instance.
- [supported] On a noisy simulated backend (IBM FakeWashington) the approximation ratios degrade (reported range ≈98% down to ≈70% depending on problem size), showing sensitivity to noise.
- [supported] On real hardware (IBM Nazca) the approximation ratios fall further (reported ≈96% for 2 stocks down to ≈51% for 6 stocks; 7–8 stocks reported ≈52%–55%), demonstrating practical hardware limitations.
- [supported] Empirically they find circuit depth p ≥ 3 and trotter steps m ≥ 3 produce the best results in their experiments (they used p=3, m=3 as a recommended setting).
- [supported] The implementation requires (N + nf + 3) qubits when using the feasibility oracle plus the three ancillae for the mixer construction (architecture/qubit-cost claim).
- [speculative] The paper claims that integrating QFT into the feasibility oracle 'ensures feasible states are targeted while reducing computational complexity'; this is proposed as an advantage but not proven analytically or rigorously compared with classical cost models.
- [speculative] The authors claim the QWM-QAOA reduces computational complexity and improves solution quality relative to classical methods; this is asserted as a potential benefit but not demonstrated as a general (or asymptotic) quantum advantage.
- [speculative] The paper positions the method as a potentially useful contribution to 'quantum finance' and suggests broader applicability to NP-hard financial optimization; this is a forward-looking claim without broad empirical validation.
- [supported] The authors explicitly acknowledge and document key practical limitations (noise, gate errors, decoherence, limited qubit counts, small dataset sizes) and state that error mitigation and hardware improvements are necessary for real-world applicability.

**Results summary:** This preprint presents a knapsack-based mapping of a discrete portfolio selection problem (values from Markowitz expected returns, unit weights, capacity ≈ N/2) and a hybrid QAOA variant (QWM-QAOA) that adds a QFT-based feasibility oracle and a trotterized quantum-walk-style mixer. In simulation (noise-free) the method attains approximation ratios close to or matching the classical Best-Known Solution (100% down to ≈96% for up to 8 stocks). Performance degrades under realistic noise models (FakeWashington) and on real hardware (IBM Nazca), where approximation ratios can drop to roughly 50% for larger instances. Empirically p ≥ 3 and m ≥ 3 were the best parameter settings in their tests. The authors highlight both the promise of the approach in idealized settings and the current practical limitations imposed by NISQ hardware.

**Performance claims:**
- Noise-free simulator: approximation ratios between 100% (2–3 stocks) and ~96% (8 stocks) relative to the classical Best-Known Solution (BKS).
- Noisy simulated backend (IBM FakeWashington): approximation ratios reported in range ≈98% down to ≈70% depending on portfolio size (e.g., 2 stocks ≈98%, 6–7 stocks ≈70%, 8 stocks ≈78%).
- Real device (IBM Nazca): approximation ratios reported ≈96% for 2 stocks, dropping to ≈51% for 6 stocks, with 7 and 8 stocks at ≈52% and ≈55% respectively.
- Empirical recommendation: circuit depth p ≥ 3 and trotter steps m ≥ 3 yielded stable/high approximation ratios in their experiments (they used p=3, m=3 as default).
- Qubit requirement statement: total qubits required = N + n_f + 3 (where n_f is number of ancilla qubits for the feasibility oracle plus 3 ancillae for the mixer).
## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** speculative

The authors report that their QWM-QAOA attains approximation ratios comparable to classical best-known solutions in noise-free simulation, but they do not demonstrate runtime or scaling advantages, and real-device results degrade substantially due to noise. Therefore any claim of practical or provable quantum advantage is speculative: performance parity in simulators is necessary but not sufficient evidence of quantum advantage, and the paper lacks conclusive demonstrations (speed, robustness, or asymptotic improvement) on real hardware or against optimized classical solvers.
## Limitations
- Noise and hardware constraints: real-device experiments (IBM Nazca) showed substantial performance degradation (approximation ratios dropping to ~50%) due to gate errors and decoherence (author-stated).
- Scalability: approach demonstrated only on small portfolios (2–8 stocks); scaling to larger numbers of assets is constrained by qubit count, coherence times, and increasing circuit complexity (author-stated).
- Limited dataset and problem realism: experiments used a small, curated set of stocks and a simplified binary knapsack formulation (weights set to 1, capacity = half the assets); real-world factors such as transaction costs, market dynamics, larger universes and richer constraints were not included (author-stated).
- Qubit and ancilla overhead: the feasibility oracle and the Quantum Walk Mixer implementation require additional ancilla qubits (noted requirement of N + nf + 3 qubits), increasing hardware demands (author-stated).
- Trotter approximation and non‑commuting operators: the implementation of the mixer relies on trotterization; approximation errors and the need to tune trotter steps m (and restriction of β to [0,mπ)) limit fidelity and parameter ranges (author-discussed).
- Parameter sensitivity and tuning: performance depends on circuit depth p and trotter step m; proper selection (they report p ≥ 3 and m ≥ 3 work well) is required and may be instance-dependent (author-discussed).
- [inferred] Simplified risk modeling: although the Markowitz model is referenced, the quantum formulation used only expected returns as values and uniform unit weights for assets; the covariance structure and portfolio variance are not integrated into the quantum cost or constraints, which could limit financial realism.
- [inferred] Arbitrary capacity choice: capacity C was set to half the number of assets (Length(E(R))/2) without discussion of economic justification; this may bias results and limit generalizability.
- [inferred] Limited benchmarking against classical methods: although BKS (best-known solution) is used as a reference, comprehensive runtime/resource-comparison and statistical benchmarking against state-of-the-art classical solvers on larger instances is not provided.
- [inferred] Noise mitigation and error correction absent: no concrete error-mitigation or fault-tolerant strategies were implemented in experiments on real hardware, limiting conclusions about near-term practicality.
- [inferred] Potential overhead of QFT-based arithmetic: using QFT to perform additions in the feasibility oracle increases circuit depth and may be costly on NISQ devices.
## Open questions

- Can the observed non-linear/noise effects (e.g., approximation ratio improving for larger portfolios in some noisy runs) be explained and predicted, and can they be exploited?
- What are the trade-offs between trotter-step count m, circuit depth p, and noise for achieving the best practical performance on NISQ devices?
- How much do practical market considerations (transaction costs, liquidity, slippage, dynamic rebalancing) change the problem mapping and the applicability of the proposed approach?
- Can the ancilla/qubit overhead be reduced (e.g., via more compact arithmetic or alternative oracles) while preserving or improving feasibility checking and mixing quality?
- What formal approximation guarantees (if any) can be proven for QWM-QAOA on knapsack-style portfolio instances as p and m scale?

- While algorithmic components (QFT oracle + QWM + QAOA) are presented as scalable, empirical experiments are limited to N <= 8 and require multiple ancillas and deep circuits; this contradicts optimistic scalability implications given current qubit counts and noise levels.
