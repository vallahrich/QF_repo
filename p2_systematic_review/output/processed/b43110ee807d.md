## Abstract summary
The paper introduces two quantum algorithms for estimating Value at Risk (VaR) and Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR) for financial derivative portfolios: an extension of Quantum Amplitude Estimation (QAE) and a novel approach based on Quantum Signal Processing (QSP). They provide complexity and error analyses and show that while both methods share asymptotic scaling, the QSP-based algorithm requires significantly fewer quantum resources and can, under certain scenario-preparation assumptions, reduce the logical clock rate needed for quantum advantage (compared to individual derivative pricing) by up to ~30x.
## Methodology
The authors develop and analyze two quantum algorithms to compute Value at Risk (VaR) and Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR) for portfolios of financial derivatives. The first algorithm extends prior Quantum Amplitude Estimation (QAE) methods by coherently encoding portfolio prices under many scenarios: they load a superposition of scenarios S, apply a pricing oracle A that encodes each scenario's portfolio value into an amplitude, use an inner QAE to produce a binary approximation register of prices, then apply a comparator and an outer QAE plus bisection search to locate the VaR threshold. The second (novel) algorithm uses Quantum Signal Processing (QSP)/Quantum Singular Value Transformation to implement polynomial approximations of threshold (and inverted-threshold) functions directly on the block-encoded A operator, avoiding intermediate digital encoding; QAE is then used only to read out the transformed amplitude and bisection search finds the VaR. They present construction of the oracles, block-encoding interpretation, polynomial approximation procedures (optimization-based Chebyshev/minimax over discretized grids), error and complexity analyses, and resource estimates (oracle calls, T-depth). They perform numerical simulations (classical emulation) comparing the QAE and QSP approaches: sampling scenario prices from normal distributions, simulating the encoding/transformation and amplitude estimation with specified accuracies, counting oracle calls and computing VaR estimation error relative to classical percentile-based baseline; and they compute T-depth and logical-clock-rate requirements for practical advantage using a T-depth model (including an autocallable derivative A operator T-depth from prior work).

**Algorithms used:** Quantum Amplitude Estimation (QAE), Canonical QAE, Iterative QAE / IQAE (discussed for adaptive accuracy), Quantum Signal Processing (QSP) / Quantum Singular Value Transformation (QSVT), Amplitude encoding / block-encoding, Comparator circuit (binary comparison), Polynomial approximation via Chebyshev / minimax optimization, Bisection search (classical outer loop), Multi-round adaptive amplitude estimation

**Experimental setup:** Numerical (classical) simulations/emulations of the quantum algorithms. Scenario prices were synthetically generated by sampling from normal distributions (e.g., N(mean=0.5, sigma=0.09)); experiments used scenario set sizes such as N=5,000, 10,000, 50,000. The quantum procedures (QAE inner/outer, QSP polynomial transforms, comparator) were emulated to compute probabilities, amplitude-encoding errors, and to count oracle invocations. No specific quantum simulator or hardware backend is specified.

**Dataset:** Synthetic scenario price data generated from normal distributions (e.g. mean 0.5, std dev 0.09). Additionally, an autocallable derivative benchmark is referenced using resource parameters (A operator T-depth = 3900) taken from prior work (Ref. [19]). No real market data was used.

## Findings
- [speculative] The paper introduces two quantum algorithms (QAE-based and QSP-based) to compute Value at Risk (VaR) and Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR) for derivative portfolios.

- [supported] Numerical simulations indicate the QSP-based algorithm requires significantly fewer quantum oracle calls than the QAE-based variant for the same target VaR error (on average ~10x fewer oracle calls in the presented tests).
- [speculative] The QSP framework can coherently apply near-arbitrary polynomial transformations to block-encoded derivative prices, enabling threshold-like operations needed for VaR/CVaR estimation without intermediate digitization.
- [speculative] Approximating the VaR threshold function with QSP introduces controllable polynomial approximation error that scales inversely with polynomial degree (ϵ_θ ≈ O(1/d)).
- [supported] The authors performed numerical simulations of both algorithms using sampled scenario prices (normal distribution examples) and compared VaR estimation error versus oracle calls; results favor QSP for the explored parameter regimes.
- [speculative] The QSP method can be used to compute CVaR by applying a modified (linear-below-threshold) polynomial transformation and then using amplitude estimation to read out the required expectation.
- [speculative] The possibility of quantum advantage for portfolio VaR hinges critically on the ability to prepare the required scenario superposition efficiently (scenario-loading cost is a key resource).
- [supported] Under the authors' resource model and assumptions (including a pricing A operator T-depth TA = 3900 and specific scenario models), numerical estimates show that QSP-based VaR could reduce the minimum logical QPU clock rate required for quantum advantage (relative to single-derivative pricing estimates) by up to ~30x in favorable scenario-loading regimes.

**Results summary:** The preprint proposes two quantum algorithms for VaR and CVaR of derivative portfolios: an extension of existing QAE-based risk-analysis methods and a novel Quantum Signal Processing (QSP)-based algorithm. Theoretical analysis shows similar asymptotic error scalings, but numerical simulations and resource accounting indicate the QSP-based method has substantially lower constant-factor quantum-resource requirements than the QAE variant (simulated ~10x reduction in oracle calls). The work includes polynomial-approximation constructions for threshold functions, complexity/error analyses, and scenario-based numerical studies. Under certain assumptions about scenario loading and a specific resource model (including T-depth for the pricing oracle), the authors estimate that QSP-based VaR estimation could lower the logical clock rate required for quantum advantage (vs. single-derivative pricing estimates) by up to ≈30×. The authors emphasize that the feasibility of practical advantage depends strongly on efficient preparation of scenario superpositions and leave scenario-preparation as an open research question.

**Performance claims:**
- Classical nested Monte Carlo VaR (pricing inner loop and scenario outer loop) scales as O(N / ϵ_p^2) for N scenarios and pricing accuracy ϵ_p; nested error analysis in paper yields classical nested complexity asymptotically described as O(ϵ^{-4}) in their formulation.
- Semi-classical (quantum pricing inner loop, classical outer sampling) reduces complexity by one power: claimed O(ϵ^{-3}).
- QAE and QSP methods (when scenario sampling is used) achieve an overall scaling of ~O(ϵ^{-2}) (up to polylog factors) for VaR estimation.
- Numerical simulation: QSP required on average ~10× fewer oracle calls than QAE to reach the same VaR error across tested instances (N=5k sampled scenarios from N(0.5, 0.09^2)).
- Polynomial approximation error for QSP threshold function scales approximately as ϵ_θ ≈ a/d + b (numerical fit gave a≈0.31 for a test), i.e., ∼O(1/d).
- Resource parameters used in advantage estimates: assumed pricing oracle T-depth TA = 3900 and classical single-derivative pricing time of 1 s for ϵ_p = 2×10^{-3} (from prior work cited in the paper).
- For benchmarking (numerical): to match classical VaR error in the studied setting, authors find for their simulations the QSP method requires at least polynomial degree d=600 and amplitude-estimation accuracy ϵ_A ≈ 1.2×10^{-3}.
- Under their resource model and N=50k scenarios, if scenario superposition preparation T-depth TS ≤ 3×10^5, QSP-based VaR could reduce the logical QPU clock rate required for total-runtime parity with classical processing by up to ≈30× compared to single-derivative pricing estimates; the threshold for any advantage (vs. single-derivative baseline) was TS ≲ 8×10^7 in their modeling.
## Quantum advantage claim

## Limitations
- Quantum resource requirements for practical advantage remain significant (circuit depth, number of qubits, and logical clock rate).
- The original QAE-based VaR method cannot be directly applied when derivative prices are themselves obtained by sampling; requires additional QAE invocation.
- Coherent (non-iterative) amplitude estimation variants are required for the inner QAE used to digitize amplitudes, which incurs extra resource/ancilla overhead (e.g., QFT).
- Finite binary representation of prices: in practice V(si) cannot be represented exactly, producing leakage/error in comparator outcomes unless many ancilla qubits are used.
- QSP requires polynomial approximations to discontinuous threshold functions; approximating such discontinuities can require high-degree polynomials (high circuit depth) for tight error guarantees.
- The QSP approach’s error is dominated by the polynomial approximation error ϵθ which scales as O(1/d), so achieving very small ϵθ may require large d.
- The analysis and numerical benchmarks assume a cost model and resource numbers (e.g., TA = 3900 T-depth for the pricing oracle) from prior work — results depend on those assumptions.
- Scenario-superposition preparation is not solved in this work — the overall algorithmic cost and possibility of advantage critically depend on the (currently open) cost TS to prepare scenario states.
- When scenario superpositions are prepared by sampling, the outer sampling error (classical Monte Carlo-like) imposes a lower bound ˜O(ϵ^{-2}) on achievable scaling.
- The CVaR polynomial approximation is harder due to discontinuity; although an inverted threshold mitigates this, CVaR still requires additional steps (VaR must be computed first).
- Error analysis assumes regularity (analyticity) of the inverse CDF at p = 1 − α; tail irregularities or heavy-tailed distributions could amplify VaR errors or invalidate approximation bounds.
- Comparisons to classical methods assume fixed-number scenario lists (historical/hypothesized scenarios) rather than fully adaptive or multilevel sampling which can change classical complexity assumptions.
- [inferred] The numerical simulations use normal-distributed prices and small problem sizes; performance and resource scaling under realistic, multimodal, or heavy-tailed market distributions and high-dimensional risk-factor spaces is not demonstrated.
- [inferred] The practical polynomial degrees and QSP circuit depths required for very high confidence (e.g., regulatory α) could be prohibitive on near-/mid-term fault-tolerant hardware.
- [inferred] The work treats approximation error of the A operator as negligibly small by adding qubits (logarithmic overhead) — this may understate implementation/cost tradeoffs in realistic encodings.
- [inferred] The QAE inner→digitization→comparator→outer QAE workflow introduces multiple coherent stages that may be sensitive to cumulative noise and require high overall fidelity.
- [inferred] The parity constraints and mapping restrictions inherent to QSP (even/odd polynomials, domain scaling) may complicate polynomial construction for some target functions and practical encodings.
## Open questions
- Can the relevant multivariate scenario probability distributions (for realistic, high-dimensional risk factors) be efficiently loaded/prepared on a quantum computer, and for which classes of distributions?
- What are the practical costs (T-depth, T-count, ancilla) and scalings for preparing scenario superpositions for real-world VaR problems, and which state-preparation methods are most suitable?
- Is the recent state-preparation method for uniform superpositions of subsets (Ref. [37]) applicable to the scenario encodings used here, and what resource savings would it yield?
- How well do QSP-based VaR/CVaR algorithms perform for realistic derivative payoff distributions (e.g., heavy tails, multi-modality, high kurtosis) compared to the normal-distribution toy model used in simulations?
- How does the choice of polynomial approximation parameters (degree d, gap ∆, tolerance ϵ) trade off with hardware constraints and final VaR/CVaR estimation error in realistic settings?
- Can quantum multi-level Monte Carlo or adaptive sampling strategies be integrated with QSP/QAE approaches to further reduce complexity, and what are the concrete resource/error tradeoffs?
- Which quantum representations of copulas or other statistical models scale to the dimensionality needed for portfolio VaR, and can they be used to load scenario distributions efficiently?
- How sensitive are the end-to-end runtime and required logical clock rate to inaccuracies in the assumed oracle costs (e.g., TA) and to practical compilation/decomposition costs (e.g., controlled rotations, reflections)?
- Can the QSP formulation of derivative-pricing oracles be leveraged for other higher-level financial applications beyond VaR/CVaR, and what are promising target problems?
- What are the implications of cumulative coherence and noise across the multiple QSP/QAE iterations (and bisection rounds) for fault-tolerant error budgets and qubit counts?

**Future work:**
- Study which (relevant) probability distributions can be loaded efficiently on a quantum computer and quantify the impact on potential quantum advantage.
- Determine the applicability of the new state-preparation method for uniform superpositions of subsets (Ref. [37]) to the VaR algorithms in this work.
- Explore quantum representations of financially relevant copulas and assess their scalability to high-dimensional scenarios for scenario generation/loading.
- Perform a detailed analysis of applying Multi-level Monte Carlo and adaptive sampling techniques in the quantum setting (integration with QSP/QAE).
- Extend the QSP formulation of derivative pricing to other relevant financial applications where aggregate computations over scenario superpositions may yield advantage.
- Conduct more extensive numerical studies with realistic, high-dimensional market models and non-Gaussian payoff distributions to validate algorithmic performance and resource scaling.
