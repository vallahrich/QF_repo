## Abstract summary
This preprint benchmarks Maximum Likelihood Amplitude Estimation (MLAE) on a superconducting IBM Nairobi quantum computer using inner-product (unary data loader) circuits and aggressive compilation/optimization with Qiskit and Pytket. The authors find that optimally compiled circuits allow MLAE to outperform naive sampling for up to three Grover iterations (circuit depth ≈131) on 4-qubit experiments (best reported mean error ≈0.0442), and they provide empirical upper bounds on feasible AE circuit depths for current NISQ hardware.
## Methodology
The authors benchmark Maximum Likelihood Amplitude Estimation (MLAE) on a superconducting quantum processor by constructing quantum circuits that encode the squared inner product of pairs of unit vectors into the amplitude of a designated qubit. Circuits implement state-preparation operator A (unary data loader style using RBS two-qubit unitaries) and the Grover iterate Q = A S0 A† Sψ. They generated synthetic random unit-vector pairs in 2 and 4 dimensions (inner products approximately uniformly distributed), compiled the high-level circuits to IBM Nairobi basis gates and then applied aggressive optimizations using Qiskit and Pytket. Using a linear sequencing schedule (LIS) they evaluated mk = 0..7 Grover repetitions, executing each compiled subcircuit with 500 shots and repeating the whole experiment 50 times to obtain statistics. Measurement counts hk for the target outcome were fed into the MLAE likelihood function and an MLE over θ (with an initial uniform prior over [0, π/2]) produced amplitude estimates a = sin^2 θ, which were compared against the exact inner products. They also compared MLAE results to a naive sampling baseline (classical averaging) with up to 3000 shots in increments of 500. The study reports mean errors, variance, circuit depths and gate counts across compilation strategies, and identifies empirical depth thresholds (e.g., deterioration of accuracy beyond ~3 Grover iterations for the tested hardware and compilations).

**Algorithms used:** Maximum Likelihood Amplitude Estimation (MLAE), Grover iterate (amplitude amplification), Naive sampling (classical baseline)
**Frameworks:** Qiskit, Pytket

**Experimental setup:** IBM Nairobi superconducting quantum processor (7 qubits, quantum volume 32). Synthetic experiments used 2-qubit and 4-qubit circuits to encode inner products via unary-style data loaders (RBS gates). Circuits compiled to hardware basis gates then optimized with Qiskit (optimization level 3) and Pytket (level 2). MLAE run with linear sequencing mk = 0..7, 500 shots per subcircuit, 50 independent repetitions; naive-sampling baseline run up to 3000 shots in increments of 500. Initial MLE prior uniform over [0, π/2]. Reported measured metrics include mean relative error, variance, circuit depth and gate counts.

**Results summary:** This preprint reports experimental benchmarks of Maximum Likelihood Amplitude Estimation (MLAE) on IBM's Nairobi 7-qubit superconducting device. Using unary-style RBS-based inner-product circuits for 2- and 4-qubit tasks and aggressive compilation (Qiskit and Pytket), the authors show MLAE can outperform naive sampling: for 2 qubits, a minimum mean error of 0.0017 was observed after 7 Grover iterations; for 4 qubits, Pytket-optimized circuits attained mean error 0.0442 after 3 Grover iterations at compiled depth ~131. ## Limitations
- Canonical QPE-based AE is unsuitable for current NISQ devices because controlled gates and QFT are expensive and increase circuit depth (author-stated).
- Compilation to hardware-native gates and qubit topology can dramatically increase circuit depth, gate counts, and alter algorithm performance (author-stated).
- Empirical deterioration of AE results after ~3 Grover iterations due to decoherence and accumulated gate errors; practical upper bound on useful circuit depth observed (author-stated).
- RBS gates compile into large-depth two-qubit interactions on superconducting hardware unless aggressively optimized (author-stated).
- Naive sampling did not improve with larger shot counts (up to 3000) due to hardware noise limiting estimation accuracy (author-stated).
- [inferred] Experiments are limited in scope to unary data-loader inner-product circuits (2- and 4-dimensional vectors), so results may not generalize to other problem types or higher dimensions.
- [inferred] Results are specific to a single superconducting device (IBM Nairobi, 7 qubits, QV=32); generalizability across different superconducting backends or larger devices is unproven.
- No systematic, quantitative head-to-head comparison of compilation toolchains (Qiskit vs Pytket) was performed; the presented results are not direct comparisons (author-stated).
- [inferred] Use of only a linear sequencing schedule (LIS) for MLAE leaves open whether other schedules (EIS, power-law, iterative AE variants) might perform better under noise.
- [inferred] Small sample sizes for random vectors (50 instances per experiment) could affect statistical robustness of the reported means and comparisons.

- #idea:hybrid-approach — Classical maximum-likelihood post-processing (MLE) combined with quantum subcircuit executions (MLAE) is an effective hybrid workflow for amplitude estimation on current hardware.

- #limitation:qubit-count — The experiments use unary-style data loading (RBS) and small 2- and 4-qubit circuits; the approach as tested is qubit-inefficient for higher-dimensional problems, limiting direct scalability.

## Contradictions
- contradiction:scalability — Although amplitude estimation promises asymptotic quadratic speedup versus classical sampling, this study demonstrates that on current NISQ hardware the depth and noise constraints limit practical advantage to very small instances (4 qubits, ≲3 Grover iterations), contradicting optimistic expectations that AE yields practical speedups without addressing hardware depth/noise.
- contradiction:scalability — The paper's empirical upper bounds on AE circuit depth challenge claims that QAE/AE variants are readily applicable to real-world Monte Carlo (e.g., finance) problems on near-term devices without significant algorithmic or hardware advances.
