## Abstract summary
The authors introduce Q-CHOP, an adiabatic quantum algorithm for constrained combinatorial optimization that enforces constraints via an always-on constraint Hamiltonian and uses a rotating objective Hamiltonian to adiabatically transform an easy-to-prepare worst feasible state into a high-quality (ideally optimal) feasible solution. They provide prescriptions for setting the constraint penalty, extensions to arbitrary objectives and inequality constraints, and benchmark Q-CHOP against standard penalty-based quantum annealing on a variety of problems (MIS, dominating set, knapsack, combinatorial auctions, and a bond ETF use case), finding consistently improved performance.
## Methodology
The authors propose Q-CHOP, an adiabatic quantum algorithm for constrained combinatorial optimization that enforces constraints throughout evolution via a constraint Hamiltonian H_con and uses a ramped objective Hamiltonian H_obj(θ) to adiabatically convert a (known) worst feasible state into an optimal feasible state. For odd (linear) objectives they construct Hramp by global Ry(θ) rotations of H_obj; for general objectives they form locally-rotated Pauli-string averages. Inequality constraints are handled with slack variables (qudit encoding) and associated mixing factors. They choose a penalty factor λ to suppress leakage out of the feasible subspace (guideline λ ≈ 2||Hramp||/Δ_con; in experiments they set λ = N). Numerical evaluation was done with exact classical state-vector simulations: time-evolution of the Schr¨odinger equation with the total Hamiltonian H_tot(s)=H_con + λ^{-1}Hramp(s) (Q-CHOP) and a standard linear- interpolation quantum-annealing Hamiltonian for the baseline SQAA. Ramps used were typically linear in the interpolation parameter (θ = π t/T for Q-CHOP); objective Hamiltonians were normalized by a variance-based norm N(H_obj). Performance was assessed via the in-constraint approximation ratio, probability of measuring an optimal solution (P_opt), and epsilon-optimal projectors. Multiple problem families were evaluated (MIS, directed MDS, knapsack, combinatorial auction, synthetic bond-ETF basket optimization) using ensembles (Erdos-R´enyi graphs, knapsack instance generator, CATS) and synthetic financial instances. Simulations integrated dynamics using the DOP853 ODE solver (scipy.integrate.solve_ivp) with absolute and relative tolerances of 1e-8.

**Algorithms used:** Q-CHOP (Quantum Constrained Hamiltonian Optimization), Standard Quantum Adiabatic Algorithm (SQAA) / Quantum Annealing
**Frameworks:** SciPy (scipy.integrate.solve_ivp, DOP853), CATS combinatorial auction test suite (for CA instance generation), Jooken knapsackProblemInstances (knapsack instance generator, GitHub)

**Experimental setup:** Exact classical (state-vector) simulations of time-dependent Schrödinger evolution. Q-CHOP: evolve initial feasible state under H_tot(θ)=H_con - λ^{-1} H_obj(θ) with θ swept linearly θ=π t/T. SQAA baseline: linear anneal HSQAA(t) = -(1-t/T) Sx + (t/T)(H_con + λ^{-1} H_obj). Hamiltonians built from Pauli strings; inequality constraints encoded by slack qudits. Time integration via DOP853 with abs/rel tol = 1e-8. Penalty factor λ = N in experiments. Objective Hamiltonians normalized by N(Hobj) as in paper.

**Dataset:** Synthetic and benchmark instance generators: Erdos-Renyi random graphs (p=0.3) for MIS and DMDS; Jooken's knapsack instance generator for knapsack (parameters specified in paper); CATS combinatorial auction instances for CA; synthetic bond-ETF instances generated by the authors (weights w_a sampled uniform(0,1) then normalized; Price(a) sampled Normal(mean=1, sd=0.1); sector constraints and parameters described in paper). No external real-world financial market dataset was used.

## Findings
- [speculative] Proposal of a new adiabatic quantum algorithm, Q-CHOP (Quantum Constrained Hamiltonian Optimization), which enforces constraints throughout evolution by keeping an always-on constraint Hamiltonian Hcon and uses a ramped/rotated objective Hamiltonian to adiabatically transform a known (easy-to-prepare) worst-feasible state toward better feasible solutions.
- [speculative] Q-CHOP assigns distinct roles to constraints (enforced at all times via Hcon) and objectives (used to construct the adiabatic ramp), and provides a principled guideline for selecting the penalty-like scalar λ (e.g., λ ≲ 2 ||Hramp|| / Δcon) based on problem data.
- [supported] Empirical numerical benchmarks (exact classical simulation) on a variety of problems (maximum independent set (MIS), directed minimum dominating set (DMDS), knapsack, combinatorial auction, and a bond ETF basket optimization use case) show that Q-CHOP consistently outperforms the standard penalty-based adiabatic algorithm (SQAA) on the tested instances and sizes.
- [supported] In the simulated instances Q-CHOP produced higher in-constraint approximation ratios and higher probabilities of finding optimal or near-optimal feasible solutions than SQAA.
- [supported] For MIS and DMDS instances tested (Erdos-Rényi graphs), Q-CHOP achieved higher optimal-state probabilities than SQAA, and the optimal-state probability for Q-CHOP did not decay with problem size N at the tested runtimes T ~ N^2 (statistical tests reported: SQAA trend negative p=6×10^-4; Q-CHOP trend non-decreasing p=0.01).
- [supported] For knapsack and combinatorial auction instances (small-size examples, e.g., N=8), Q-CHOP outperformed SQAA on all simulated instances (the paper reports Q-CHOP better in every simulated knapsack and combinatorial auction instance at N=8).
- [supported] For the bond ETF basket optimization experiments (20 random instances), Q-CHOP outperformed SQAA on the in-constraint approximation ratio in all instances and on the 0.01-optimal-state probability in 16/20 (80%) instances; where SQAA did better it was by a small margin (≤ 0.03), whereas Q-CHOP's average advantage across instances was reported as ~0.22 on one metric.
- [supported] Experiments used a canonical choice λ = N (penalty scalar), normalized objective Hamiltonians by N(Hobj) as defined in the paper, and typical runtime choices included T = 2π N^2 for many comparisons.
- [supported] Analytic connection: Q-CHOP reduces (is essentially equivalent) to the previously proposed adiabatic method for MIS by Yu/Wilczek/Wu (Refs. cited) after accounting for parameter and sign conventions; the equivalence is demonstrated in the appendix.
- [speculative] Proposed relaxation: if an arbitrary easy-to-prepare feasible state |x⋆⟩ (not necessarily the worst feasible) is available, one can form a subproblem with H⋆_obj = −(Hobj − E⋆)^2 so that |x⋆⟩ is the worst feasible state for the subproblem and then use Q-CHOP; this comes with costs (quadratically larger norms, larger λ and T) and open caveats (degeneracy issues).
- [speculative] Treatment of inequality constraints via slack variables is possible (introduce slack qudits / truncated bosonic modes), but dimensions scale with constraint magnitude; the authors propose additional mixing factors to avoid fragmenting the feasible subspace—but note significant scaling caveats and point to the need for better methods for many/large inequality constraints.
- [speculative] Counterdiabatic insight: for single-body (linear) objectives an additional ∂tθ Sy term appears naturally in the derived effective Hamiltonian and can be interpreted as an approximate counterdiabatic drive that may marginally improve performance; the suggestion to use counterdiabatic or shortcuts-to-adiabaticity techniques is left to future work.
- [speculative] Limitations identified include potential failure when the feasible-state subspace is loosely connected or disconnected under the adiabatic mixer induced by the rotated objective; in such cases Q-CHOP may be unable to reach optimal feasible states from the chosen initialization.
- [speculative] The authors note possible broader directions including discretized/variational versions of Q-CHOP (analogue of QAOA), and that Q-CHOP ideas could be hybridized with other adiabatic/annealing approaches.

**Results summary:** The paper introduces Q-CHOP, an adiabatic quantum algorithm that enforces constraints at all times via an always-on constraint Hamiltonian and uses a rotated objective Hamiltonian to adiabatically move from a known (often worst) feasible state toward better feasible solutions. The authors provide a theoretical framework (including a guideline for selecting the penalty-like scalar λ) and several analytic observations (including equivalence to a prior MIS-specific method and an approximate counterdiabatic term for linear objectives). They present exact classical simulations comparing Q-CHOP to the common penalty-based adiabatic approach (SQAA) across multiple constrained combinatorial optimization problems (MIS, DMDS, knapsack, combinatorial auctions, and a bond-ETF use case). On the tested small instances and chosen runtimes (frequently T = 2π N^2) Q-CHOP consistently produced higher in-constraint approximation ratios and higher probabilities of finding optimal or near-optimal feasible solutions than SQAA. The paper outlines practical caveats (handling inequality constraints, disconnected feasible spaces) and proposes future directions including discretized/variational variants and refined counterdiabatic control.

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** speculative

The paper presents numerical (classical) simulations showing that Q-CHOP outperforms the penalty-based adiabatic algorithm (SQAA) on small, diverse problem instances and specific runtimes, but does not prove an asymptotic quantum speedup or advantage over classical algorithms; the reported improvements are empirical on limited-size instances and relative to one quantum baseline, making claims of general or asymptotic quantum advantage speculative and requiring further theoretical and larger-scale empirical validation.
## Limitations
- Q-CHOP can fail on problems whose feasible subspace is loosely connected or disconnected by the mixer constructed from the objective Hamiltonian; in such cases Q-CHOP may be unable to convert the worst feasible state into the best feasible state.
- Selecting the penalty factor λ and the total runtime T remains a key sensitivity; while Q-CHOP gives guidance (e.g., λ ≈ 2||Hramp||/Δcon), finding optimal λ can be difficult and is NP-hard in general.
- The basic Q-CHOP formulation assumes an easy-to-prepare worst feasible state; relaxing this assumption (to allow any easy-to-prepare feasible state) requires solving an auxiliary subproblem whose objective has quadratically larger norm, implying larger λ and longer quantum runtime and possible degeneracy issues.
- Treatment of inequality constraints using slack variables leads to substantial overhead: slack dimensions may be large (up to max D(x)), and the additional mixing operator S(θ) has norm that grows with the product of slack-variable dimensions (∥S(θ)∥ scales like ∏_D |S_D|), which is impractical for many constraints.
- The proposed strategy for inequality constraints (adding mixing via S(θ)) does not scale multiplicatively with the number of inequality constraints and is recognized as an unsatisfactory approach for large numbers of such constraints.
- Implementing the rotated objective Hamiltonian H_obj(θ) (Eq. 9) is likely difficult in analog quantum hardware; in gate-based settings it increases the number of Pauli strings (by O(k) for k-body terms), increasing circuit complexity.
- Comparison to the standard quantum adiabatic algorithm (SQAA) can be sensitive to hyperparameter choices (λ, choice and prefactor of driver Hamiltonian, adiabatic schedule); keeping λ fixed for comparisons may bias results.
- The theoretical understanding of Q-CHOP is incomplete: performance guarantees or quantitative bounds (e.g., gap-dependent runtime scaling) are not provided, and the relationship between feasible-subspace connectivity and performance requires further analysis.
- Methods that enforce constraints via measurements (quantum Zeno dynamics, subspace correction) may introduce considerable measurement overhead; Q-CHOP aims to be unitary, but integration/combination with measurement-based approaches is not solved.
- The relaxation approach (constructing H_obj* = −(H_obj − E_*)^2) can produce large objective norms and degeneracies (if E_* has large degeneracy), which may suppress population transfer to desired ground states.
- [inferred] Practical implementation overhead (gate counts, circuit depth) for realistic problem sizes and for H_obj(θ) could be large, potentially limiting near-term realizations on NISQ devices.
- [inferred] Q-CHOP performance may degrade on problems requiring highly correlated variable updates (e.g., permutation or assignment constraints) because simple local mixers may fail to connect relevant feasible configurations.
- [inferred] Classical simulation limitations (used for numerical benchmarking) restrict problem sizes explored; empirical behavior at larger scales and on hardware remains uncertain.
## Open questions
- How to design alternative adiabatic paths (different strategies to 'invert' or rotate H_obj) that improve Q-CHOP performance across broader problem classes?
- What are robust, scalable strategies to enforce inequality constraints without exponential growth in resource cost (slack-variable dimensions or mixing norms)?
- How viable is the relaxation from needing an easy-to-prepare worst feasible state to needing any easy-to-prepare feasible state in practice? Under what conditions does the relaxation succeed or fail (including degeneracy effects)?
- When and why does feasible-subspace shattering (loose connectivity or fragmentation of H_feas) occur for practical optimization problems, and how can it be detected and mitigated?
- Can one derive rigorous theoretical performance bounds or gap-dependent guarantees for Q-CHOP, and relate them to structural properties of H_con and H_obj?
- How effective are approximate counterdiabatic (or other shortcut-to-adiabaticity) terms for Q-CHOP in improving runtimes, and what are practical, implementable forms of such drivings for general objectives?
- How does Q-CHOP compare to SQAA and other quantum optimization methods when all relevant hyperparameters (λ, driver Hamiltonian, schedules) are jointly optimized for each method?
- Can discretized/variational variants of Q-CHOP (QAOA-like or recursive QAOA variants) be formulated that inherit Q-CHOP's advantages and exhibit better scaling?
- What are the best encodings and hardware primitives (qudits, bosonic modes, specific analog platforms) to represent slack variables and manage inequality constraints efficiently?
- How do finite-size effects observed in simulations (e.g., scaling with N in MIS/DMDS) extrapolate to larger instances — is there an asymptotic separation between Q-CHOP and SQAA?
- How to design mixing operators that act jointly on decision and slack variables to respect inequality constraints while remaining experimentally feasible?
- Can ideas from Q-CHOP be ported to modify other algorithms (e.g., reverse annealing or variational methods) to enforce constraints throughout evolution?

**Future work:**
- Investigate Q-CHOP with arbitrary-feasible-state initialization (the worst-to-any feasible-state relaxation) to test, validate, and refine its practicality and limitations.
- Design and study different strategies to 'rotate' or invert objective Hamiltonians (alternative adiabatic paths) to potentially improve performance.
- Develop better strategies for handling inequality constraints that avoid the exponential growth in resource cost associated with the proposed slack-variable mixing approach.
- Develop a stronger theoretical understanding of Q-CHOP, including quantitative guarantees or bounds, and analyses of spectral gaps and feasible-subspace connectivity (e.g., study subspace shattering).
- Explore the inclusion of counterdiabatic driving and other shortcut-to-adiabaticity techniques in Q-CHOP to speed up evolution; derive implementable approximate counterdiabatic terms for general objectives.
- Perform broader empirical comparisons between Q-CHOP and SQAA when optimizing over hyperparameters (λ, driver Hamiltonian, schedules) and study robustness to those choices.
- Investigate discretized, variational versions of Q-CHOP (QAOA-like ansatzes and recursive variants) and study whether they offer advantages similar to continuous Q-CHOP.
- Design improved encodings and primitives (qudits, bosonic modes) for slack variables and explore alternative adiabatic paths or mixers that couple decision and slack variables efficiently.
- Study asymptotic scaling and finite-size effects more extensively to assess whether observed empirical benefits persist and to identify regimes of advantage.
- Explore borrowing Q-CHOP ideas to modify other quantum optimization algorithms (e.g., reverse annealing) to enforce constraints throughout evolution.
