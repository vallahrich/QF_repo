## Abstract summary
This preprint explores how quantum computing can enhance data quality, with a particular focus on anomaly detection by substituting computationally intensive classical subroutines with quantum techniques. It includes a practical demonstration using quantum reservoir computing to detect volatility regime changes in stock market time series, reports that quantum embeddings can be competitive with classical ones, and outlines remaining challenges and future research directions.
## Methodology
The paper combines two literature reviews and an empirical experiment. First, the authors performed two systematic literature reviews: (1) to identify classical anomaly-detection subroutines that could benefit from quantum drop-in replacements and to derive a taxonomy mapping classical subroutines to quantum algorithms, and (2) to survey existing quantum anomaly-detection approaches and build a taxonomy of quantum methods used. Second, they implemented an empirical quantum-reservoir-computing experiment for volatility-regime detection on financial time series. The experimental pipeline encodes rolling windows of log-returns into Hamiltonians (HX, HZ, HZZ), evolves the quantum reservoir (unitary evolution U(t)=e^{-iHt}), measures single- and pairwise Pauli-Z expectation values to form embeddings, and trains classical readout models on these embeddings. Simulations were run in Qiskit without noise; classical reservoirs were built with ReservoirPy and classical classifiers (logistic regression, SVM, ridge, XGBoost, random forest, neural nets, Gaussian mixture, MLP) were trained (grid-search hyperparameter tuning). Evaluation used an 80/20 train/test split per stock and averaged accuracy and average precision across 29 Dow Jones stocks (2015–2020).

**Algorithms used:** Quantum Reservoir Computing, Hamiltonian-based time evolution (HX, HZ, HZZ), Pauli-Z expectation measurements, Classical readouts: Logistic Regression, Support Vector Machine, Ridge Classifier, XGBoost, Random Forest, Neural Network (Vanilla NN), Gaussian Mixture, Multi-layer Perceptron
**Frameworks:** Qiskit, ReservoirPy, scikit-learn, PyTorch, yfinance, XGBoost (library)

**Experimental setup:** Quantum circuits simulated with Qiskit without noise (no physical QPU). Classical reservoirs built with ReservoirPy. Grid search hyperparameter tuning for classical models. 80/20 train/test split per stock; results averaged across 29 stocks.

**Dataset:** Market data for 29 publicly traded Dow Jones Industrial Average stocks spanning five years (2015–2020) obtained via yfinance (Yahoo! Finance API). Time series were converted to log-returns and processed into rolling-window volatility features.

## Findings
- [supported] The authors performed two literature reviews and produced two taxonomies: (1) quantum algorithms that can serve as drop-in replacements for compute-intensive subroutines in classical anomaly detection approaches, and (2) quantum algorithms that have been used in proposed quantum anomaly detection approaches.
- [supported] There is (so far) a lack of purely quantum-native anomaly detection methods in the literature—most published approaches are hybrid or adapt classical designs with quantum subroutines.
- [speculative] Quantum computing techniques (e.g., HHL, quantum PCA, quantum SVM, q-means) could reduce computational cost of several data-quality subroutines and in some cases provide quadratic or exponential speedups, although practical applicability has important caveats.
- [supported] The authors implemented and evaluated a quantum reservoir computing (QRC) model (simulated with Qiskit, noiseless) for detecting volatility regime changes in stock return time series (29 DJIA stocks, 2015–2020) and report predictive performance.
- [supported] In the simulated experiments (9-qubit reservoir), QRC embeddings with simple linear classifiers (e.g., logistic regression, ridge) achieved higher average accuracy than the corresponding classical reservoir with the same readouts, and were competitive with non-linear classical models.
- [supported] Applying a reservoir (quantum or classical) before a simple linear classifier improved classification performance compared to training the same linear classifier on raw input windows.
- [speculative] Noisy quantum gates might act as a form of regularization improving generalization of QML models (cited as an observation in recent works), but this remains an open area for further empirical verification.
- [supported] The paper identifies three practical barriers for QML adoption in data-quality tasks: the 'Quantum Computer Wall' (hardware errors, coherence limits, error correction gap), the 'Simulation Wall' (classical limits to simulating > ~50 qubits), and the 'Data (and Use Case) Wall' (data availability/quality and intrinsic performance limits imposed by data noise).
- [supported] The QRC experiment used a 9-qubit Hamiltonian-based encoding (specific Pauli-based Hamiltonians), measured single- and pairwise Pauli-Z expectation values, and used classical readouts trained on those expectation vectors.
- [speculative] The authors argue that QML may require substantially fewer trainable parameters than large classical models (claim example: ≈100 parameters vs thousands/millions), making QML attractive for resource-constrained settings; this is a projected benefit, not demonstrated at scale in this work.
- [supported] The paper documents open research directions (quantum-native algorithms for data cleaning/deduplication/imputation/schema matching, improved data encoding/loading, hybrid NISQ validation, integration with data platforms) and highlights ethical/energy considerations.
- [supported] The authors released implementation details and hyperparameter settings on GitHub for reproducibility of the QRC experiments (repository cited).

**Results summary:** The paper provides a literature-driven mapping of where quantum algorithms could replace compute-heavy subroutines in classical anomaly detection workflows and surveys existing quantum anomaly-detection proposals. It also presents an empirical study (noiseless Qiskit simulation) of a 9-qubit quantum reservoir computing model applied to volatility-regime detection on 29 Dow Jones stocks (2015–2020). The simulated QRC embeddings coupled with simple classical readouts achieved competitive or better average accuracy versus classical reservoirs and improved linear-model performance relative to raw inputs. The authors emphasize practical limitations (hardware noise, simulation scale, data availability) and identify concrete future research directions.

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** speculative

The paper argues potential computational advantages of quantum algorithms (citing known quantum algorithms with theoretical quadratic/exponential speedups) and demonstrates competitive predictive performance of a simulated 9‑qubit quantum reservoir embedding on a specific finance use case. However, it does not demonstrate empirical runtime, scaling, or provable quantum speedup on real quantum hardware; practical advantages remain speculative given hardware noise, data-loading bottlenecks, and simulation limits.
## Limitations
- Current NISQ hardware limitations: high error rates, limited qubit coherence, and restricted gate depth that hinder practical quantum advantage for data quality tasks (author-stated).
- Simulation wall: classical simulation of quantum circuits becomes infeasible beyond ~50 qubits when storing all amplitudes, restricting testing and validation of algorithms on classical infrastructure (author-stated).
- Data (and use-case) wall: scarcity of high-quality training data and intrinsic noise/limits in datasets that may cap achievable performance and reduce potential quantum advantage (author-stated).
- Data-loading bottleneck: efficient encoding of large classical datasets onto quantum hardware is unresolved and may negate theoretical speedups (author-stated).
- Lack of mature, true quantum-native anomaly detection algorithms; most existing work is hybrid or adapts classical approaches rather than providing end-to-end quantum solutions (author-stated).
- Practicality of quantum drop-in replacements is uncertain: implementing quantum counterparts for classical subroutines (e.g., POT, ARIMA) remains speculative and faces engineering challenges (author-stated).
- Unresolved caveats for exponential speedups: theoretical quantum algorithms that promise exponential gains may have applicability restrictions that limit real-world benefit (author-stated, referencing caveats).
- Experimental limitations in the presented study: quantum reservoir computing experiments were run in noise-free simulation (Qiskit) and used only 9 qubits, so results may not generalize to real noisy hardware or larger problem sizes (author-stated).
- Flat training landscapes and costly training/evaluation: common QML problems (e.g., barren plateaus) and a high number of quantum evaluations can impede training efficiency and scalability (author-stated).
- Error correction overhead: the computational cost of quantum error correction/mitigation might outweigh benefits for many practical data-quality tasks, especially simpler ones (author-stated).
- Lack of standardized quantum software integration for data pipelines: absence of frameworks integrating quantum subroutines with big-data platforms (e.g., Apache Spark) (author-stated).
- Maturity gap in specific applications: quantum methods for tasks like schema matching, approximate deduplication, and embedding-based semantic alignment are immature (author-stated).
- Uncertainty about when fault-tolerant, error-corrected systems with sufficient logical qubits and gates will be available to enable practical large-scale QML (author-stated).
- Potential limited benefit for simple classical methods: some classical algorithms (e.g., median-based methods) may not benefit from quantum replacements and could be made more complex without performance gains (author-stated).
- [inferred] Limited external validity of the financial experiment: applicability to other financial instruments, markets, longer horizons, or non-financial domains is not demonstrated.
- [inferred] Evaluation lacks statistical robustness: averaged accuracy/precision across 29 stocks is reported but statistical significance, variance, or robustness checks are not described.
- [inferred] Measurement and readout overhead: the need to compute many expectation values (single- and multi-qubit Pauli terms) could add classical postprocessing cost and measurement rounds that limit speedups.
- [inferred] Hamiltonian design and encoding sensitivity: performance likely depends heavily on choices of Hamiltonians, scalers, and encoding schemes, which are nontrivial to tune for general datasets.
- [inferred] Scalability of quantum reservoir computing unclear: scaling to larger windows (more qubits) or multivariate time series may encounter exponential resource or noise limitations.
