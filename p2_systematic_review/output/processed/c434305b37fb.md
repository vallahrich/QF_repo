## Abstract summary
This experimental preprint demonstrates hardware-aware variational quantum circuits on a superconducting QPU to model distributions used in Credit Risk Analysis, focusing on loading discrete approximations of standard normal distributions for a Gaussian conditional-independence model. The authors employ topology-aware transpilation, pulse-level gate calibration, and in-situ variational tuning to optimize two- and three-qubit Gaussian loaders and a small GCI circuit, showing that hardware-specific parameter adaptation yields experimentally consistent results (e.g., matching VaR conclusions) in the NISQ era.
## Methodology
The study implements and experimentally evaluates hardware-aware variational quantum circuits to encode discrete approximations of standard normal distributions and a one-factor Gaussian Conditional-Independence (GCI) credit-risk model on a superconducting transmon QPU. The latent factor z is discretized on an n-qubit register (n=2 in the main experiments) and mapped to rotation angles via an affine transform; the asset default probability PD(z) is linearized through arcsin(sqrt(PD)) to obtain slope and offset parameters (α, β) such that single-qubit Ry rotations encode PD ≈ sin^2(α z + β). For distribution loading the authors adopt a shallow hardware-efficient ansatz (single-qubit Ry on each qubit followed by CNOT entanglers) and train parameters θ to match a target discrete Gaussian histogram p* by minimizing an L2 loss L(θ)=∑(p_b(θ)-p*_b)^2 using parameter-shift gradients and the Adam optimizer. On-device, they perform systematic angle sweeps and fine-grained hyperparameter tuning of Ry angles (including replacement of transpiled subcircuits with the hardware-efficient ansatz when necessary) to account for NISQ noise and connectivity constraints. Circuit transpilation used Qiskit’s preset pass manager with SABRE routing and synthesis-based approximations; low-level gate implementation leveraged DRAG microwave pulses (60 ns) for X/Y rotations, virtual Z, and a native CZ gate implemented with Sudden-Net-Zero (SNZ) flux pulses. They characterized and calibrated the QPU (Rabi, Ramsey, AllXY, Motzoi/DRAG tuning), measured gate fidelities via Randomized Benchmarking (RB) and Interleaved RB (IRB), calibrated readout (single-shot readout matrices), ran repeated shot statistics (100 repeated experiments for error estimation), and evaluated outputs as probability amplitudes per basis state, reconstructed PDFs/CDFs of portfolio loss, and 95% VaR to compare hardware output with noiseless simulations and a classical baseline.

**Algorithms used:** Variational Quantum Circuits (hardware-efficient ansatz), Parameter-shift gradient rule, Adam optimizer, SABRE qubit mapping (SWAP-based routing), Qiskit transpilation (preset pass manager), Randomized Benchmarking (RB), Interleaved Randomized Benchmarking (IRB)
**Frameworks:** Qiskit, Quantify (Qblox control framework), Qblox electronics stack

**Experimental setup:** Contralto-D superconducting transmon QPU (Quantware) with 17 flux-tunable transmons in a 2D lattice; experiments used qubits A6 (high-frequency), D3 (medium), and C4 (low). Native two-qubit gate: CZ implemented via Sudden-Net-Zero flux pulses on the higher-frequency qubit; single-qubit X/Y implemented via DRAG microwave pulses (60 ns); Z gates are virtual (frame updates). Readout via dedicated resonators multiplexed over four feedlines, amplified by HEMT; control and pulse sequencing via a Qblox cluster and Quantify Python framework. Qiskit used for circuit design/transpilation and simulation. Calibration protocols included Rabi, Ramsey, AllXY, Motzoi/DRAG tuning; gate fidelities measured with RB/IRB; single-shot readout discrimination matrices measured before experiments.

**Dataset:** Synthetic credit-risk scenario based on the Gaussian Conditional-Independence (GCI) model: one asset, one latent Gaussian factor Z ~ N(0,1). Model parameters used in experiments include baseline default probability p0 = 0.25 and asset correlation ρ = 0.027 (used to compute α, β). The latent factor is discretized on a 2-qubit register (4 grid points) for main experiments and on 3 qubits for extended tests.

## Findings
- [supported] Implemented hardware-aware variational quantum circuits on a transmon-based superconducting QPU (Contralto-D) to prepare two- and three-qubit discrete approximations of standard normal (Gaussian) distributions for a one-factor Gaussian Conditional-Independence (GCI) credit-risk model.
- [supported] Demonstrated experimental calibration/tuning of single-qubit rotation angles to shape the probability amplitudes (histograms) on the device, with identified optimal angle settings for specific qubit registers (examples: D3–C4 two-qubit optimal θ0=90°, θ1≈237°; D3–A6 two-qubit optimal θ0=90°, θ1≈224°; D3–A6–C4 three-qubit optimal θ0=90°, θ1≈212.5°, θ2≈104.5°).
- [supported] Showed empirically that the rotation angles required to realize a normal-like histogram are not universal but depend on the specific physical qubits (hardware-dependent tuning is required).
- [supported] Used a hardware-aware transpilation/ pulse-level approach (including replacing a SABRE-transpiled subcircuit with a manually tuned shallow ansatz) to improve circuit output on the real device; found that a hardware-agnostic transpilation produced a uniform three-qubit distribution on the machine and had to be replaced to recover the intended distribution.
- [supported] Executed a small GCI use-case (one asset, one latent factor) on-hardware, produced a CDF of portfolio losses, and obtained the same 95% VaR level (L = 1000) as the classical baseline and as the simulated hardware-ready transpiled circuit within experimental uncertainty.
- [supported] Reported device performance and calibration metrics (representative values): single-qubit average gate fidelities ~99.855%–99.924%; CZ two-qubit interleaved fidelities reported ~99.7% (A6–D3) and ~99.05% (D3–C4); coherence times T1 ~32–43 μs, T2* ~20–37 μs, T2,Echo ~37–42 μs (Table I).
- [supported] Quantified experimental variability and error bars by repeating runs (100-shot statistics) to estimate fluctuations in prepared probability amplitudes (examples: two-qubit amplitude asymmetry metrics ΔP up to ~0.06 in some pairings; three-qubit ΔP values ~0.01–0.02).
- [speculative] The authors suggest that hardware-aware, pulse-level variational tuning and hardware-specific transpilation will be an effective general strategy for other NISQ-era financial algorithms and that quantum compilers should adapt circuit structure and parameterization to specific QPUs rather than relying on hardware-agnostic approaches.
- [speculative] The work is positioned as a useful starting point toward practical NISQ deployments in finance, but scalability beyond the demonstrated small registers is not experimentally established in this paper.

**Results summary:** This preprint reports an experimental study on a transmon-based 17-qubit superconducting QPU implementing hardware-aware variational circuits to load discrete Gaussian distributions (two- and three-qubit registers) and a small Gaussian Conditional-Independence (GCI) credit-risk subcircuit (one latent factor, one asset). The team calibrated single-qubit rotation angles and adapted transpiled subcircuits at the pulse level to account for device-specific connectivity and noise. They found that optimal rotation parameters are device- and register-dependent, and that replacing a hardware-agnostic transpiled subcircuit with a shallow manual ansatz recovered the intended distribution. The experimental GCI output (CDF) matched the classical baseline VaR (95%) at the same loss level (L = 1000) within measured experimental uncertainties (~5%). Device coherence and gate fidelity metrics are reported, and empirical error bars were estimated via repeated runs.

## Limitations
- Author-stated: Experiments conducted on NISQ hardware are sensitive to noise, decoherence, and lack continuous quantum error correction.
- Author-stated: Small-scale, proof-of-concept implementation (three qubits: two for latent factor, one asset); the study does not implement the full Quantum Amplitude Estimation (QAE) pipeline or large portfolios.
- Author-stated: Transpilation pipelines (e.g., SABRE / Qiskit preset) do not incorporate device-specific noise sensitivities; noiseless simulators therefore fail to reproduce experimental outputs exactly.
- Author-stated: The rotation angles required to realize normal-like histograms are device- and register-specific and must be retuned on hardware (limited transferability of optimized parameters).
- Author-stated: No readout error mitigation techniques were applied and no quantum-noise-limited cryogenic amplifiers were integrated for these experiments.
- Author-stated: Experimental outputs exhibit measurement/SPAM fluctuations; reported experimental CDFs have an error on the order of ~5%.
- Author-stated: Some lower-level pulse-level engineering (e.g., counter-phase Rz gates, pulse calibration) was necessary to obtain acceptable two-qubit gate performance — increasing implementation complexity.
- Author-stated: Results and conclusions are tied to a specific superconducting processor (Quantware Contralto-D) and specific qubit choices (A6, D3, C4); hardware-specificity limits generalization.
- [inferred] Limited scalability: the paper demonstrates up to three qubits only; it is unclear how well the variational/pulse-level tuning and shallow ansatz will scale to 2^n amplitudes for larger n.
- [inferred] Generalizability across platforms: the dependence of optimal parameters on device particulars suggests transfer to other superconducting layouts (or different hardware technologies) will require significant rework.
- [inferred] Stability/time-dependence: results rely on calibrations taken immediately before runs; calibration drift and run-to-run variability could degrade reproducibility over longer campaigns.
- [inferred] Resource and automation gaps: the approach required manual identification and replacement of transpiled subcircuits and manual hyperparameter sweeps; an automated hardware-aware optimization flow is not demonstrated.
- [inferred] Evaluation scope: the paper focuses on distribution loading and CDF/VaR comparison for a toy instance; broader performance metrics (e.g., execution time, scaling of sampling error, end-to-end QAE performance) are not evaluated.
## Open questions

- Can optimized rotation angles and pulse-level calibrations be made portable across different devices or different qubit pairs, or must they always be re-tuned per-device and per-register?
- How can transpilers be adapted to incorporate device noise models so that simulated transpiled circuits better predict experimental outcomes?
- What is the best way to integrate readout error mitigation and cryogenic quantum-noise-limited amplification into this pipeline, and how much will they improve fidelity of distribution loading and downstream risk metrics?
- How to automate the hardware-aware transpilation and hyperparameter tuning (including pulse-level adjustments) to reduce manual intervention and speed up deployment?
- What is the robustness of the trained loaders and tuned circuits to calibration drift over time and environmental variations?
- Can the demonstrated approach be extended from the uncertainty model (distribution loading) to the full QAE-based VaR/CVaR estimation at scales where quantum advantage might be relevant?
- How does the choice of ansatz (hardware-efficient shallow circuits) affect the expressivity required to approximate more complex or higher-resolution distributions?
- What are the limits imposed by connectivity and native gate sets on encoding more realistic multi-factor dependence structures used in industry-scale credit risk models?
- [inferred] What are the trade-offs between allowing approximate unitary synthesis during transpilation (to reduce two-qubit gates) and the impact of approximation error on final financial risk metrics?

## Key ideas
- #idea:near-term-feasibility — Hardware-aware variational circuits implemented on a superconducting QPU can load discrete Gaussian approximations and reproduce 95% VaR conclusions for a one-asset GCI model in 2-3 qubit experiments.
- #idea:hybrid-approach — A hybrid workflow (classical parameter-shift gradient training with Adam plus on-device angle sweeps and replacement of transpiled subcircuits by hardware-efficient ansatz) is effective to counteract NISQ limitations.
- #limitation:qubit-count — Main experiments use a 2-qubit latent-register (4 grid points) plus an asset qubit (3 total qubits), underscoring insufficient qubit counts for realistic multi-asset credit portfolios.
- #limitation:noise — Achieving experimentally consistent outputs required extensive pulse-level calibration (DRAG, SNZ), RB/IRB benchmarking, and fine-grained on-device tuning, indicating sensitivity to hardware noise and limited fidelities.
- #limitation:data-encoding — The approach relies on first-order linearization of arcsin(sqrt(PD)) and coarse discretization of the latent factor (2–3 qubits), which constrains representational fidelity and may not scale to richer PD models.
## Contradictions
- Although the paper achieves VaR-consistent results on-device for toy instances, it shows no empirical quantum advantage over classical baselines for the tested scenarios and instead matches classical/noiseless outputs — contradicting claims that quantum methods already outperform classical approaches for practical credit-risk tasks.
- The need for heavy hardware-specific adaptation (pulse calibration, subcircuit replacement, exhaustive angle sweeps) and the restriction to 2–3 qubits contradict optimistic assertions that variational NISQ circuits will scale straightforwardly to production-sized credit portfolios without substantial engineering and larger fault-tolerant devices.
