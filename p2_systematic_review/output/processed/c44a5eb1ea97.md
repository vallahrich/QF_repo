## Abstract summary
The paper introduces a quantum-accelerated multilevel Monte Carlo (QA-MLMC) method that achieves up to quadratic speedup in precision for estimating expectations determined by solutions of stochastic differential equations (SDEs). The authors apply QA-MLMC to financial problems (e.g., Black–Scholes, Local Volatility models, Greeks) and present a quantum sublinear binomial-sampling algorithm for the binomial option pricing model, improving classical complexities from roughly O(ϵ^{-2}) to about O(ϵ^{-1}) (up to logarithmic factors) under specified smoothness and scheme-order conditions.
## Methodology
The paper develops and analyses a quantum-accelerated multilevel Monte Carlo (QA-MLMC) methodology for estimating expectations defined by solutions of stochastic differential equations (SDEs) arising in mathematical finance. The theoretical methodology combines (i) classical multilevel Monte Carlo (MLMC) theory (Giles/Heinrich) with (ii) a quantum speedup for Monte Carlo sampling (Montanaro’s quantum-accelerated Monte Carlo/amplitude-estimation style bounds). The authors characterise the discretisation error and inter-level variance via strong-order r numerical SDE integrators (Taylor-Itô and Taylor-Stratonovich families, Euler–Maruyama, Milstein, higher-order schemes), derive the MLMC parameters α (bias decay), β (variance decay) and γ (cost growth per level) under Lipschitz / piecewise Lipschitz payoff assumptions, and then plug these into complexity formulas to obtain QA-MLMC runtime scalings. They also analyse a sublinear binomial sampling approach for binomial lattice option models. To validate aspects of the analysis they run classical numerical experiments on the Black–Scholes model: simulate SDE sample paths with several numerical schemes, compute level-wise means and variances of Pl and Pl−Pl−1, and estimate slopes (α, β) by linear regression on log-log plots. The methodological contribution is primarily algorithmic and complexity-theoretic, with supporting classical numerical simulations to estimate convergence exponents and to illustrate the scaling behavior.

**Algorithms used:** Quantum-accelerated Monte Carlo (QA-MC / amplitude-estimation style speedup), Quantum-accelerated multilevel Monte Carlo (QA-MLMC), Multilevel Monte Carlo (MLMC), Classical Monte Carlo (MC), Amplitude estimation (as conceptual quantum primitive), Euler–Maruyama discretization, Milstein discretization, Taylor–Itô high-order schemes, Taylor–Stratonovich high-order schemes, Sublinear binomial sampling (for binomial tree models)

**Experimental setup:** Classical numerical experiments were performed on synthetic SDE data (Black–Scholes geometric Brownian motion). The authors tested five numerical schemes (Euler–Maruyama, Milstein, strong order 1.5, strong order 2, strong order 3) and two payoff types (European call and digital/cash-or-nothing). Parameter choices: μ=0.05, σ=0.2, T=1, S0=100, K=100. They ran large numbers of independent simulations (reported: 1e6, 1e6, 1e7, 1e8, 1e9 independent simulations respectively across the five schemes) to estimate level-wise means and variances for MLMC levels l (uniform time refinements n_l=2^l). For the strong order 3 digital-option case they also used σ=1.5 and K=100 e^{-0.05} to increase randomness. Per-level statistics (E[Pl], Var[Pl−Pl−1]) were plotted as log2 versus level l and linear regression on the last four (small-h) points produced empirical slopes giving estimates of α (bias) and β (variance) decay rates.

**Dataset:** Synthetic SDE simulation data generated from the Black–Scholes geometric Brownian motion model (no external financial dataset).

## Limitations
- QA-MLMC provides guarantees in terms of additive error (with high probability) rather than mean-squared error; this requires slightly different assumptions and error treatments compared to classical MLMC.
- To achieve the optimal quadratic-in-precision quantum speedup (complexity ~ eO(1/ϵ)), the QA-MLMC analysis requires use of numerical schemes with strong order r > 2; lower-order schemes give weaker improvements.
- The theoretical results rely on several modelling/regularity assumptions: µ and σ are globally Lipschitz; Taylor-Itô / Taylor-Stratonovich coefficient functions are globally Lipschitz; payoﬀs are piecewise Lipschitz (or globally Lipschitz for improved bounds).
- The algorithms assume availability of oracles OI, OW, OP, Oµ, Oσ and that these can produce coherent quantum superpositions when required; the paper assumes this can usually be satisfied but does not provide construction details for general inputs.
- Complexity bounds are stated up to polylogarithmic factors (e.g. eO(·)), and hide constant factors; practical runtime and resource constants (and full gate counts) are not provided.
- The variance assumptions: several results assume payoﬀ evaluations (or their level differences) have bounded variance independent of discretization step h; this may not hold in all models or for some discontinuous payoﬀs.
- Numerical evaluation is limited in scope (Black–Scholes examples and certain tests of α, β) — comprehensive empirical validation across a broad class of SDEs / payoﬀs (including high-dimensional cases) is not provided.
- [inferred] High-order stochastic integration schemes (strong order r > 2) can be technically difficult to implement and may require higher smoothness of coefficients; that practical difficulty may limit the attainable quantum speedup in realistic models.
- [inferred] Implementing the quantum subroutines needed (coherent sampling of Brownian increments, coherent evaluation of payoﬀs, and the quantum-accelerated Monte Carlo primitives) may be resource-intensive; the paper does not analyze circuit depth/qubit counts or fault-tolerance requirements.
- [inferred] The asymptotic improvements are in ϵ but come with polylogarithmic overheads and unspecified constants; for realistic target error tolerances the net advantage over classical methods is uncertain without detailed cost constants.
- [inferred] Some intermediate technical bounds (e.g., estimates of α and β) are conservative or problem-dependent: tightness can depend on particular schemes or payoﬀ smoothness, which complicates predicting performance for a given application.
- [inferred] The binomial-sublinear-sampling approach is specialized (piecewise-constant payoﬀs) and may not generalize straightforwardly to arbitrary payoﬀs or models with more complex path dependence.
## Open questions
- Can the quantum-accelerated MLMC approach be extended to Poisson’s equation and elliptic PDEs (i.e., non-parabolic PDEs) where classical MLMC has been applied?
- Can a quadratic-in-precision quantum speedup be obtained for more general time-dependent payoﬀs that depend on the stochastic path at intermediate times (e.g., Lookback options), beyond the path-integral extension discussed?
- Can quantum algorithms be developed for pricing American options (and other problems formulated as variational inequalities) to obtain similar or better speedups?
- Can one provide practical constructions of the required quantum oracles (OI, OW, OP, Oµ, Oσ) for realistic input models, and analyse the true quantum resource costs (qubits, gates, depth) including state preparation overheads?
- How do the polylogarithmic factors and hidden constants in the eO(·) notation affect practical runtimes, and for what problem sizes / precisions does QA-MLMC yield a concrete advantage over classical MLMC?
- Are there alternative quantum input–output models or problem formulations (beyond estimating a single classical mean) where quantum algorithms can provide useful or stronger advantages in finance or other applications?
- Can the theoretical bounds on α and β (convergence rates of level means and variances) be tightened for particular high-order schemes or specific classes of payoﬀs, improving required smoothness assumptions?
- How robust is the QA-MLMC speedup to violations of the bounded-variance or Lipschitz assumptions (e.g., models with jumps, heavy tails, or highly non-smooth payoﬀs)?
- What are the requirements and costs to implement coherent sampling of Brownian motions and multi-level coupling (same random seed across levels) in realistic quantum hardware?
- Can the sublinear binomial sampling techniques be generalized to broader discrete models or to multidimensional lattice models used in finance?

**Future work:**
- Investigate extensions of the QA-MLMC framework to Poisson’s equation and elliptic PDEs (and more general PDE classes).
- Study whether the quadratic quantum speedup can be preserved for more general time-dependent payoﬀs (e.g., Lookback options) and other path-dependent derivatives.
- Develop quantum algorithms for problems formulated as variational inequalities, such as American option pricing.
- Explore other estimands or characteristics of stochastic processes (beyond the mean of a payoﬀ) that could be estimated advantageously on a quantum computer.
- Design and analyze more practical quantum input–output models (state preparation and oracle implementations) suitable for finance applications.
- Refine theoretical analysis to obtain sharper estimates of the parameters α and β for specific numerical schemes and payoﬀ smoothness classes.
- Extend numerical experiments and empirical evaluation to a broader set of SDE models, payoﬀs (including high-dimensional cases), and realistic quantum-resource estimates to assess practical advantage.
- Examine the construction and cost of coherent sampling mechanisms (e.g., coherent Brownian paths) and the effect on overall quantum algorithm complexity.
- Investigate generalizations and limitations of sublinear binomial sampling approaches for broader option classes and lattice models.
