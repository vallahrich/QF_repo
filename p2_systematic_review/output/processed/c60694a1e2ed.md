## Abstract summary
This review examines the potential of quantum machine learning (QML) methods—such as Quantum SVMs, Quantum Neural Networks, and hybrid quantum-classical algorithms—to improve credit risk assessment by exploiting quantum principles like superposition and entanglement for high-dimensional classification, optimization, and Monte Carlo tasks. It highlights promising early applications in default forecasting, portfolio optimization, and alternative credit scoring using non-traditional data, while noting current practical limits due to NISQ-era hardware, data-loading challenges, and the need for hybrid implementation strategies.
## Methodology
This paper is a non-empirical literature review and conceptual synthesis. The author surveys existing theoretical and experimental work in quantum computing and quantum machine learning for finance, synthesizes foundational quantum algorithms (e.g., quantum PCA, HHL, quantum phase estimation, amplitude estimation), and reviews QML methodologies (Quantum SVMs, Quantum Neural Networks, variational/hybrid approaches and quantum kernels). The work organizes findings into comparative analyses (predictive accuracy, computational efficiency, scaling behavior), summarizes implementation challenges (hardware, noise, data encoding, software frameworks), and proposes conceptual architectures and transition strategies (hybrid quantum-classical pipelines, cloud access, modular adoption). The paper relies on secondary sources (academic papers, arXiv preprints, industry reports) and presents tables and a conceptual figure rather than original experimental data or new algorithm implementations.

**Frameworks:** Qiskit, PennyLane

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** theoretical

The paper presents quantum advantage primarily as a theoretical/asymptotic prospect (exponential or quadratic speedups for specific subroutines such as kernel evaluation, linear-system solving, eigenvalue computations and Monte Carlo). It acknowledges current NISQ hardware, data-encoding, and software limitations that prevent broad, empirically demonstrated advantage, recommending hybrid modular approaches and targeted prototype use-cases as the near-term pathway.
## Limitations
- Current quantum hardware is in the NISQ era with limited qubit counts and high error rates.
- Short coherence times (microseconds to milliseconds) restrict feasible circuit runtimes.
- Decoherence and noise sensitivity limit the depth and reliability of quantum algorithms.
- Practical error correction requires large numbers of additional qubits that are not yet available.
- Circuit depth limitations constrain the complexity of implementable financial algorithms.
- Data encoding / data-loading is a major bottleneck (e.g., O(n) gates for n features; amplitude encoding scales poorly).
- Encoding errors can propagate and degrade model performance on noisy hardware.
- Theoretical quantum speedups (e.g., exponential or quadratic) remain largely unrealized in practice due to hardware and circuit-depth constraints.
- Quantum SVMs and quantum kernel methods trade interpretability for representational power; high-dimensional Hilbert spaces lack straightforward visualization.
- Hybrid approaches are currently necessary; fully quantum implementations are impractical in the near term.
- Quantum software frameworks (e.g., Qiskit, PennyLane) have limited support for complex financial data structures and require extensive preprocessing.
- Existing quantum libraries lack standardized approaches for error mitigation and have limited finance-specific optimizations.
- Significant development overhead and specialized expertise are required to implement QML in financial contexts.
- Benchmarking, standardized datasets, and agreed protocols for fair comparison are currently insufficient.
- Problem-size thresholds and concrete conditions under which quantum advantage emerges are not yet established in real-world financial applications.
- Error mitigation techniques increase implementation complexity and may limit achievable speedups.
