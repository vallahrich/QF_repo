## Abstract summary
This conference paper empirically compares three quantum machine learning architectures—Variational Quantum Classifier (VQC), Sampler Quantum Neural Network (SQNN), and Estimator Quantum Neural Network (EQNN)—on two credit-card fraud datasets (BankSim synthetic data and a PCA-processed European dataset). The study evaluates combinations of three feature maps (Pauli, ZZ, Z) and four ansatz designs (RealAmplitudes, EfficientSU2, TwoLocal, PauliTwoDesign), finding that VQC and SQNN consistently outperform EQNN (VQC achieved an F1 score up to 0.88), with ANOVA confirming statistically significant effects of model, feature map, and ansatz; robustness tests under five simulated quantum noise channels show reasonable resilience for the best models.
## Methodology
The study performs a systematic empirical comparison of three quantum machine learning architectures — Variational Quantum Classifier (VQC), Sampler Quantum Neural Network (SQNN) and Estimator Quantum Neural Network (EQNN) — for credit-card fraud detection. Two benchmark financial datasets are used (a synthetic BankSim dataset and a real European credit-card dataset). For each QML model the authors evaluate three quantum feature maps (Z, ZZ, Pauli) and four ansatz designs (RealAmplitudes, EfficientSU2, TwoLocal, PauliTwoDesign), producing twelve feature-map/ansatz configurations per model. Data preprocessing includes MinMax scaling, removal of low-variability zipcode features, encoding of categorical fields, random under-sampling to obtain balanced 50/50 fraud/non-fraud subsets (492 fraud + 492 non-fraud samples), and PCA (European dataset reduced to seven components). Models are trained with an 80/20 train/test split; quantum circuits are simulated with Qiskit Aer (QasmSimulator). Optimization uses classical optimizers (COBYLA primarily; ADAM and gradient descent are also referenced) with cross-entropy loss and up to 350 iterations. Performance is evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score and loss. Statistical validation is performed via one-way ANOVA to test significance of model, feature map and ansatz effects on F1. Robustness tests apply five noise channels (amplitude damping, bit flip, depolarizing, phase damping, phase flip implemented by Kraus operators) to the best-performing models (SQNN on BankSim, VQC on European) to assess resilience under NISQ-like noise.

**Algorithms used:** Variational Quantum Classifier (VQC), Sampler Quantum Neural Network (SQNN), Estimator Quantum Neural Network (EQNN)
**Frameworks:** Qiskit, Qiskit Aer (QasmSimulator), PennyLane (referenced), scikit-learn

**Experimental setup:** Quantum circuit simulation using Qiskit Aer QasmSimulator. Classical optimizers used include COBYLA (primary), ADAM and gradient descent. Cross-entropy loss. Training/test split 80/20. Max optimization iterations 350. Feature-map repetition 1-2 and ansatz repetition 1-2 layers tested. Multiple runs (boxplots indicate ~10 repetitions per configuration).

**Dataset:** Two datasets: (1) BankSim synthetic bank payments dataset (originally ~594,643 records; synthetic frauds ~7,200) — reduced via random under-sampling to a balanced set of 492 fraud + 492 non-fraud transactions for experiments. (2) European credit card transactions dataset (Kaggle ULB creditcardfraud) with 284,807 transactions and 492 fraud cases — reduced to a balanced subset of 492 fraud + 492 non-fraud; PCA applied to reduce to seven features. Preprocessing: categorical encoding, removal of zipcode features, MinMax scaling.

## Findings
- [supported] The Variational Quantum Classifier (VQC) produced the best empirical performance among the three studied QML architectures, achieving an F1-score of 0.88 on the evaluated European credit-card dataset.
- [supported] The Sampler Quantum Neural Network (SQNN) achieved strong performance on the BankSim dataset (balanced subset), with reported F1-scores up to ~0.85 for some feature-map/ansatz combinations.
- [supported] The Estimator Quantum Neural Network (EQNN) underperformed relative to VQC and SQNN across the tested configurations, with peak F1-scores substantially lower (best reported ~0.59).
- [supported] Feature-map choice materially affects performance: entangling feature maps (ZZ, Pauli) improved results in configurations where the ansatz could exploit that entanglement; simpler Z feature maps were more resilient/efficient in low-depth or noisy settings.
- [supported] Ansatz choice materially affects performance: TwoLocal and EfficientSU2 ansatz variants were among the most effective/robust across experiments; RealAmplitudes was efficient but less able to capture complex interactions in some models.
- [supported] A one-way ANOVA on F1-score shows that model type, feature map, and ansatz have statistically significant effects on performance; the dataset factor (European vs. BankSim balanced subsets) was not statistically significant in their test.
- [supported] Under simulated quantum noise channels (amplitude damping, bit flip, depolarizing, phase damping, phase flip) the best-performing models degraded in accuracy and F1 but retained non-trivial predictive ability; VQC displayed higher robustness than SQNN in these simulations.
- [supported] All three QML architectures analyzed have the same asymptotic computational complexity on ideal quantum hardware for a parametrized circuit (O(d * n) gates for depth d and n qubits); practical runtimes differ by measurement/output primitives and hardware mapping overhead.
- [speculative] Quantum Machine Learning (QML) has potential advantages for fraud detection (e.g., handling high-dimensional feature spaces, capturing complex interactions, and potential computational speedups) but the paper does not demonstrate a provable quantum advantage over classical methods.
- [speculative] Entanglement-enabled feature maps (e.g., ZZ or Pauli) can improve classification accuracy provided the variational ansatz and measurement/postprocessing are capable of leveraging the induced correlations; effectiveness depends on matching encoding and ansatz.
- [speculative] Careful configuration selection (feature map + ansatz + model type) is critical to achieving strong QML performance on financial fraud detection tasks and should guide future QML model design for similar applications.

**Results summary:** This conference study empirically compared three hybrid quantum machine learning architectures (VQC, SQNN, EQNN) on balanced subsets of two credit-card fraud datasets (BankSim and an EU PCA-transformed dataset). VQC produced the highest measured F1 (0.88 on the European dataset) and demonstrated greater resilience to simulated quantum noise; SQNN also delivered strong results (up to ~0.85 F1 on BankSim) for particular feature-map/ansatz pairings. EQNN consistently lagged behind, with peak F1 around 0.59. The experiments show that both feature map (entangling vs. non-entangling) and ansatz design significantly affect performance, and one-way ANOVA confirmed model, feature map, and ansatz as statistically significant factors, while the dataset choice (between the two evaluated) was not. Robustness tests under five noise channels showed accuracy/F1 degradation but indicated that the best models maintain non-trivial performance under noise. The work emphasizes the importance of matching encoding and variational circuit design, but does not demonstrate a practical quantum advantage compared to classical baselines.

## Limitations

- Model performance is highly sensitive to feature map and ansatz choices; some combinations produce oscillatory / slow convergence and poor optimization (author-stated).
- Optimization instability and oscillatory convergence behavior observed for several configurations, indicating challenging loss landscapes and optimizer difficulties (author-stated).
- Class imbalance in original datasets required heavy undersampling to create balanced 50/50 training sets (author-stated).
- Noise evaluation limited to five basic quantum noise channels (amplitude damping, bit flip, depolarizing, phase damping, phase flip); more realistic noise sources and correlated noise models were not explored (inferred).
- [inferred] Use of simulator (QasmSimulator) rather than experiments on real NISQ hardware limits conclusions about practical performance and hardware-specific overheads.
