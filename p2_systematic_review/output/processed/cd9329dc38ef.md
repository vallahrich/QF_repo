## Abstract summary
The paper evaluates the threat quantum computers pose to Bitcoin, finding that Bitcoin's proof-of-work (SHA256 Hashcash) is relatively resistant to substantial quantum speedups in the next decade due to very fast ASIC miners and limited near-term quantum clock speeds. In contrast, Bitcoin's elliptic-curve signatures (ECDSA/secp256k1) are highly vulnerable to Shor's algorithm and could be broken on realistic future devices (potentially within a decade), so the authors analyze alternative PoW (Momentum) and review post-quantum signature schemes to mitigate these risks.
## Methodology
This is a theoretical and quantitative analysis of quantum threats to Bitcoin and countermeasures. The authors model the Bitcoin proof-of-work (PoW) and signature schemes in the random oracle model and analyze quantum attack strategies using known quantum algorithms. For the PoW they model mining as searching for block-headers h(header) ≤ t and apply Grover-style quantum search to estimate oracle-call counts and derive an "effective hash rate" for a quantum device. For signature attacks they apply Shor's algorithm resource estimates for solving the elliptic-curve discrete log problem (ECDLP). Concrete logical gate and qubit resource counts (T-gate and Toffoli counts, logical qubits) are taken from recent circuit decompositions and compilations; these are combined with overhead models for fault-tolerant quantum computing using the surface code and magic-state distillation to obtain end-to-end time and physical-qubit estimates. The paper provides formulas for run-time (τ = #O × #G / s) and effective hashing h_QC = 0.28 × s √D / c_τ(D,p_g) (with parallelization scaling), and includes pseudo-code (Appendix) to calculate error-correction factory resources and circuit resources as functions of physical gate error rate p_g, logical gate counts (n_T, n_C), logical qubit count n_L, and circuit code distances. The authors also model technological progress and the Bitcoin network: they project future quantum device parameters (qubit counts, gate frequencies, gate fidelities) with optimistic and pessimistic growth curves, and separately extrapolate Bitcoin network hash-rate/difficulty. They analyze an alternative PoW (Momentum) and use quantum query complexity lower bounds (Aaronson–Shi collision bound and Ambainis element-distinctness algorithm) to argue reduced quantum speedup. Finally, they survey candidate post-quantum signature schemes, comparing key and signature sizes and security levels.

**Algorithms used:** Grover's algorithm (quantum search, including parallelized Grover), Shor's algorithm (discrete logarithm / factoring), Ambainis' quantum element distinctness algorithm, Magic-state distillation (for T-gate fault-tolerance), Surface-code based fault-tolerant error correction

## Findings
- [supported] Bitcoin currently uses double-SHA256 (SHA256(SHA256(.))) for its proof-of-work and the secp256k1 elliptic-curve signature scheme for transactions.
- [speculative] Grover’s algorithm gives a quadratic query-speedup for the hashcash-style PoW, but practical end-to-end advantage for quantum miners is limited by slow quantum gate clocks and large error-correction overheads.
- [speculative] Under reasonable near-term quantum hardware models, a single quantum computer is unlikely to outperform currently available ASIC miners for Bitcoin PoW within the next decade.
- [speculative] The elliptic-curve signature scheme (ECDSA/secp256k1) is fundamentally vulnerable to Shor’s algorithm and could be practically broken on a future quantum computer; the authors estimate this could be possible as early as ~2027 under optimistic assumptions.
- [speculative] Transactions whose public keys are broadcast but not yet included in a block are the most vulnerable to quantum attacks (an attacker could derive the private key and double-spend before inclusion).
- [speculative] Alternative PoW designs that rely on collision or memory hardness (e.g., Momentum) reduce the quantum speedup achievable (best known quantum runtime scales worse than Grover’s square-root advantage in that setting).
- [speculative] Lattice-based post-quantum signature schemes (e.g., CRYSTALS-Dilithium, qTESLA, FALCON) offer favorable trade-offs (signature + public key size and verification cost) for blockchain use compared to many hash- or code-based alternatives.
- [speculative] Hash-based signature schemes (e.g., XMSS, SPHINCS) are quantum-resilient but often have much larger combined public-key-plus-signature sizes than lattice schemes and/or other operational drawbacks.
- [speculative] Even before full dominance of quantum hashing power, hybrid attacks (e.g., bribing pool miners to withhold blocks) could be profitable to an adversary with modest quantum-enabled hashing advantage.
- [speculative] The concrete resource estimates (qubit counts, T-gate counts, clock-speed dependencies) indicate extremely large physical-qubit requirements and/or long runtimes for the relevant quantum algorithms when realistic error-correction is included.

**Results summary:** This theoretical analysis estimates the risk quantum computers pose to Bitcoin: PoW (double-SHA256) gets a theoretical quadratic speedup via Grover but, given current and near-term projected quantum clock speeds and error-correction overheads, quantum miners are unlikely to outcompete specialized ASICs in the next ~10 years. In contrast, Bitcoin's elliptic-curve signatures are categorically broken by Shor's algorithm; depending on optimistic hardware and error-correction improvements, key recovery of secp256k1 could become feasible on time scales comparable to Bitcoin block times (creating a practical vulnerability between broadcast and block inclusion). The paper analyzes alternative PoW (Momentum) and post-quantum signature candidates, arguing memory/collision-based PoW and lattice-based signatures are preferable mitigations.

**Performance claims:**
- Effective quantum mining hash-rate formula: h_QC ≡ (N/t) / τ = 0.28 × s × sqrt(D) / c_τ(D, p_g) (paper-derived model).
- Example (PoW): With superconducting gate speed s = 66.7 MHz, physical gate error rate p_g = 5×10^-4, and difficulty D = 10^12, the model yields c_τ = 538.6, c_nQ = 1810.7, effective quantum hash rate h_QC = 13.8 GH/s using n_Q = 4.4×10^6 physical qubits — which is ~1000× slower than an off-the-shelf ASIC (~14 TH/s).
- Grover parallelization: Using d quantum processors in parallel, effective hash rate scales as h_QC,∥ = 2.56 × h_QC × sqrt(d) (from cited parallel-search analysis).
- Example (ECDLP/Shor resources from prior work): For 256-bit curve, algorithm requires ~1.28×10^11 Toffoli gates and ~2334 logical qubits (citing Roetteler et al.).
- Example (ECDLP runtime with surface code overheads): With p_g = 5×10^-4 and superconducting clock s = 66.6 MHz the estimated time is ~6.49 days using ~1.7×10^6 physical qubits; with s = 10 GHz and p_g = 10^-5 the estimated time is ~30 minutes using ~485,550 qubits (model-derived estimates in paper).
- Optimistic 'no-distillation' limiting case: if error-correction/ distillation overheads could be removed, an optimistic effective quantum PoW hash rate could reach ~0.04 × s × sqrt(D), and at D = 10^12 and s = 50 GHz could be ~2.0×10^3 TH/s (paper notes this is a very optimistic scenario).
- Attack economics example: an adversary with 0.1% of total network hashing power could (with bribes) reduce pool revenue by ~10%; authors estimate 0.1% quantum hashing power could be realized by 20 quantum computers running in parallel under minimum optimistic assumptions (model-based claim).
- Momentum PoW analysis: classical optimal running time T ≈ sqrt(2^n+ℓ / t); best quantum algorithms achieve at best runtime scaling proportional to T^(2/3) (due to element-distinctness lower bound), so Momentum admits less quantum speedup than Hashcash (paper-theoretical result).
## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** theoretical

The paper argues quantum algorithms (Grover, Shor) provide provable theoretical speedups for PoW search and ECDLP respectively. However, the claimed practical advantage for PoW is constrained by hardware gate speeds and error-correction overheads; the signature-break advantage is theoretically certain (Shor) but practical timing estimates (when it becomes feasible) are speculative and model-dependent.
## Limitations
- Estimates depend heavily on assumptions about future quantum hardware (gate speeds, physical gate error rates, qubit counts) and the chosen extrapolation models; the authors acknowledge substantial uncertainty in these forecasts.
- Resource and time estimates rely on specific error-correction assumptions (primarily the surface code and magic-state distillation) and particular compiled circuit decompositions (e.g., SHA256 T-counts); different codes or optimizations could materially change results.
- Analysis is performed in the random-oracle model and assumes idealized hash function behaviour (e.g., uniform distribution of SHA256 outputs).
- Only Grover's and Shor's broadly-known algorithms are considered in depth; potential future quantum-algorithmic improvements are not fully modelled.
- The Momentum proof-of-work is analysed as an alternative PoW, but other proposed memory-hard or ASIC-resistant PoWs (e.g., Cuckoo Cycle, Equihash) are only mentioned and not analyzed in detail.
- The signature attack analysis assumes an attacker can allocate the necessary quantum hardware and ignores non-algorithmic operational constraints (e.g., network propagation, wallet software behavior) that affect exploitability.
- Post-quantum signature schemes are surveyed primarily along signature and key size and known attacks; practical deployment issues (wallet compatibility, verification performance under real network conditions) are not empirically evaluated.
- Security assessments of particular PQ schemes note known practical weaknesses (e.g., BLISS side-channel vulnerabilities) but full real-world deployment risk analysis is out of scope.

## Open questions
- Precisely when will quantum computers be capable of breaking secp256k1 ECDSA signatures within typical Bitcoin transaction confirmation windows (e.g., ten minutes)?
- How rapidly will advances in error correction (alternate codes, measurement-free protocols) and circuit optimization reduce the resource/time overheads for Shor’s and Grover’s algorithms?
- To what extent can alternative proof-of-work designs (Momentum, Cuckoo Cycle, Equihash, Equihash-like schemes) resist quantum speedups in practice, across realistic hardware and algorithmic advances?
- Which post-quantum signature schemes offer the best trade-offs for blockchain use (signature size, public-key size, verification time, security margins) and how practically feasible is migration for large-scale ledgers?
- What are secure, practical migration strategies for cryptocurrencies to transition from ECDSA to post-quantum signatures while preserving backward compatibility and preventing new attack vectors?
- Could new quantum algorithms or substantial optimizations (e.g., Kaliski-style magic-box methods or other yet-unknown techniques) significantly reduce the cost of solving ECDLP or other relevant problems beyond current estimates?
- How will mining-pool strategies and economic incentives change if some miners acquire quantum capabilities (e.g., bribery/withhold attacks), and what countermeasures are economically and technically viable?
- What are the impacts of side-channel and implementation-level attacks (classical or quantum-era) on both current and candidate post-quantum signature schemes when deployed in wallet and node software?
- How sensitive are the paper’s conclusions to alternative, perhaps more pessimistic or optimistic, models for classical ASIC progress versus quantum hardware progress?
- What is the practical feasibility and cost of deploying the physical qubit counts (millions) and classical control infrastructure assumed in some of the resource scenarios?

**Future work:**
- Refine and update resource and timeline estimates as quantum hardware, fault-tolerance techniques, and circuit-optimization methods evolve (including exploration of alternative error-correction codes and measurement-free approaches).
- Perform detailed analyses of other proposed PoW alternatives (e.g., Cuckoo Cycle, Equihash) with respect to quantum speedups and memory intensity, not just Momentum.
- Investigate algorithmic improvements and optimizations for ECDLP and PoW oracles (including exploring less well-known or emerging quantum algorithms) to tighten bounds on attack feasibility.
- Study migration strategies and practical deployment plans for post-quantum signature schemes in blockchain settings, including verification costs, storage impacts, and backward compatibility mechanisms.
- Evaluate post-quantum signature candidates under realistic implementation scenarios (wallets, nodes) including side-channel resistance, verification latency, and collective storage/computation impacts on the network.
- Model the economic dynamics of partial quantum adoption among miners and pools to assess real-world attack incentives and to design economically robust countermeasures.
- Empirically validate assumed gate counts, T-counts and compiled circuit depths for relevant cryptographic primitives (e.g., SHA256) with evolving quantum compilers and synthesis tools.
- Explore combined network/protocol-level mitigations (e.g., changing address-use policies, adjusting confirmation thresholds, smart-contract based bribe-prevention) and analyze their effectiveness against quantum-enabled attackers.
