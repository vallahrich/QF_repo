## Abstract summary
This preprint presents an algorithmic framework to efficiently prepare n-qubit quantum states whose amplitudes are proportional to samples of arbitrary continuous (and related) functions. The approach embeds the target vector into a rank-one projector and leverages low-rank and 1-sparse Hamiltonian simulation combined with adiabatic evolution (or phase estimation/Hadamard tests) to achieve query complexity constant in the number of qubits and overall complexity logarithmic in the desired resolution, with rigorous error bounds that depend on a defined "filling ratio". The techniques enable applications such as state preparation, numerical integration of Lipschitz functions, and improved sampling from probability densities, with relevance to finance and quantum simulation.
## Methodology
The paper develops a theoretical and algorithmic framework to prepare quantum states whose amplitudes are proportional to samples of arbitrary efficiently-computable continuous functions. Core methodology: (1) encode pointwise samples f(x_j) (or integral-encoded samples g(x_j)) into a rank-1 matrix A proportional to |ψ><ψ| where |ψ> is the desired state; (2) embed A into a larger one-sparse matrix S_A and simulate S_A using 1-sparse Hamiltonian simulation techniques; (3) use a low-rank simulation approach (building on Rebentrost et al.) to implement e^{-i t A/N} efficiently by simulating S_A and tracing out ancilla; (4) prepare the final state by performing an adiabatic interpolation of functions f_s = (1-s) f_0 + s f_1 and evolving under the time-dependent rank-1 Hamiltonian H(s) = -A(s)/N, discretized into r piecewise-constant time steps e^{-i (T/r) H(s_j)}; (5) implement each piecewise evolution via the low-rank -> one-sparse embedding and perform ancilla measurements (Hadamard) to effect the desired evolution on the main register. The authors give rigorous error bounds (adiabatic discretization error, Hamiltonian simulation error) and introduce the "filling ratio" F = ||f||_1 / ||f||_max as an intrinsic quantity controlling complexity; asymptotic query complexity scales as O(F^p / ε^2) with p = -4 (pointwise) or p = -2 (integral encoding). They also present alternative subroutines and uses: phase estimation and a Hadamard destructive-interference test to (i) verify/precondition state preparation, (ii) estimate normalization/integrals, and (iii) produce variationally useful initial states. Numerical validation: classical simulations of the exact 1-sparse dynamics on two n-qubit registers (explicit tensor representation), digitization of function values (d = 32 bits), Taylor expansion of exponentials (cutoff m = 7), and empirical scaling studies varying n and r to confirm asymptotic bounds and practical error scalings (observed ~ r^{-1.2}..r^{-1.3}). The methodology is primarily algorithmic/theoretical with analytic proofs of bounds and limited classical numerical experiments for demonstration.

**Algorithms used:** Low-rank Hamiltonian simulation (Rebentrost et al.), 1-sparse Hamiltonian simulation (Childs / Aharonov et al. style), Adiabatic state preparation (piecewise-constant discretisation), Quantum phase estimation, Hadamard test / destructive interference, Grover–Rudolph state preparation (discussed / compared)

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** theoretical

The paper claims an asymptotic quantum advantage for preparing arbitrary continuous functions because query complexity is independent of the qubit count (logarithmic complexity in desired resolution), enabling exponential savings over naive classical sampling of all grid points. These advantage claims are supported by theoretical proofs and small-scale numerical simulations; however they depend on assumptions (efficient oracle for f, dependence on the filling ratio F, and availability of fault-tolerant quantum hardware), so the advantage is demonstrated at the theoretical/proof level rather than experimentally.
