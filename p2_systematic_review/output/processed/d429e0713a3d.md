## Abstract summary
This dissertation develops and compares quantum algorithms for option pricing, focusing on amplitude estimation to accelerate Monte Carlo methods and adaptations of Fourier-based pricing using the Quantum Fourier Transform. It presents a genetic quantum architecture search to automate construction of efficient payoff circuits, proposes conditional parameterized quantum circuits for Monte Carlo integration, and benchmarks methods with respect to accuracy, scalability and quantum-hardware requirements.
## Methodology
This work explored quantum approaches to option pricing by following two main directions: (1) a Fourier-based method that adapts the classical Carr–Madan FFT pricing to quantum hardware by replacing the FFT with the Quantum Fourier Transform (QFT) and deriving suitable damped Fourier representations; and (2) a Monte-Carlo–based approach that improves classical Monte-Carlo via Amplitude Estimation (AE). For (2) the project developed an automated pipeline to learn good parameterized quantum circuits (PQCs) for option payoff functions using a multi-objective genetic architecture search (QAS). The QAS optimizes Pareto fronts over (i) approximation quality (MSE) and (ii) circuit-complexity measures (depth, number of two-qubit gates/CNOTs, number of input encodings). Learned PQCs were converted to conditional PQCs (CPQCs) by replacing data-encoding rotations with controlled fixed rotations across discretization registers; these CPQCs encode a discretized distribution for the underlying into the control register. Expected payoffs were then read out either by repeated measurements (shot estimates) or by Amplitude Estimation. Experiments were conducted in classical simulation (statevector and noisy backends), using automatic differentiation (jax) for parameter training of PQCs, and comparing methods across varying numbers of discretization qubits, damping factors (α), noise models and shot budgets. ZX-calculus–based graph mutations were also investigated as an alternative mutation space for QAS.

**Algorithms used:** Quantum Fourier Transform (QFT), Discrete Fourier Transform / FFT (classical baseline), Amplitude Estimation (AE), Amplitude Amplification / Grover operator, Variational / Parameterized Quantum Circuits (VQA/PQC), Genetic Programming / Genetic Quantum Architecture Search (multi-objective), ZX-calculus rewrites (local complementation, pivoting, etc.), Hadamard test, State preparation (arbitrary amplitude loading - discussed as bottleneck)
**Frameworks:** jax, jaxopt, PennyLane, pyzx, sympy, Qiskit (qiskit, qiskit-aer, qiskit-finance)

**Experimental setup:** All numerical experiments were performed in classical simulation. Automatic differentiation via jax/jaxopt was used to train PQC parameters. Quantum circuits were represented via PennyLane and pyzx (ZX-diagrams). Simulators: Qiskit Aer StatevectorSimulator for shot-noise emulation and Qiskit GenericBackendV2 with the default noise model for device-noise emulation. Experiments ran on a classical 48-core machine (training of 48 models in parallel per generation).

**Dataset:** Synthetic payoff datasets (no market data). Payoff functions include European call, basket call, basket call with variable weight, rainbow call and an Asian barrier spread. Inputs were generated on equidistant grids over chosen ranges (typically underlying prices S in [50,200]; for 2D/3D cases grids over [50,200]×[50,200] (and [0,1] for weight) ). Typical dataset sizes: K=400 (2D grid 20x20 or 1D 400 equally spaced), and for the 3D basket-weight case K=8000.

## Findings
- [speculative] Amplitude Estimation (AE) offers a theoretical quadratic speedup for Monte-Carlo (MC) integration (error scaling O(1/N) vs classical O(1/√N)).
- [supported] Conditional Parameterized Quantum Circuits (CPQCs) can be constructed from trained PQCs and used to encode an input distribution so that a single quantum circuit yields the expected payoff (QMCI) for that distribution.
- [supported] A multi-objective genetic Quantum Architecture Search (QAS) procedure can automatically find Pareto-optimal parameterized circuit structures (trade-offs between MSE and circuit complexity) for option payoff approximation.
- [supported] Variational/Variable-Ansatz (VAns)-based QAS produced circuits with the lowest empirical MSE in the tested examples compared to the other automated approaches.
- [supported] ZX-calculus based mutations (local complement, pivot, phase gadget add, edge operations) are usable for QAS; empirical tests quantify their success rates and impacts on function-approximation performance.
- [supported] Two different FFT-style discretizations for QFT-based option pricing were implemented; the second discretization (version 2) demonstrates substantially faster numerical convergence in the Black–Scholes test (fewer qubits needed for a given accuracy) in simulation.
- [supported] The QFT-based adaptation of classical FFT pricing methods can produce much lower pricing error (in simulation) than the tested PQC/AE-based approaches, given idealized assumptions (noise-free or very low noise, efficient state/distribution loading).
- [supported] The QFT approach requires efficient state/distribution loading and very low device noise (practically fault-tolerant hardware) to realize its advantages; simulations show it becomes preferable only under those conditions.
- [supported] The CPQC/AE pipeline (automated payoff circuit → controlled encoding → AE) works in simulation to output expected payoffs, but its practical success is highly sensitive to state/distribution-loading cost and device noise.
- [supported] Circuit resource scaling analyses (simulated transpilation counts) show QFT-based circuits have much lower asymptotic depth (O(n^2) for QFT) than classical FFT computational effort would suggest, but overall end-to-end scaling must include state loading and repeated measurements.
- [supported] Empirical experiments on simulated noise models indicate that circuits optimized for lower depth produce more robust payoff approximations under realistic device noise than circuits optimized only for raw MSE in noise-free simulation.
- [speculative] ZX-diagram-based QAS appears promising for low-complexity circuits, but extraction and mapping back to device-native circuits require more work; further research is needed to assess benefits on actual hardware.

**Results summary:** This work explores two main quantum approaches to option pricing: (1) a QFT-based adaptation of classical FFT pricing methods and (2) a Monte-Carlo-focused pipeline using automated PQC discovery (genetic QAS) and Conditional PQCs combined with Amplitude Estimation. The author(s) implemented multi-objective genetic search that yields Pareto fronts of PQC architectures (tradeoffs between MSE, depth, two-qubit gates, and number of encodings), demonstrated the automatic discovery of viable payoff circuits, and developed CPQCs to encode input distributions for QMCI. Numerical simulation experiments (noise-free and noisy backends) show that QFT-based pricing attains the best accuracy under ideal state-loading and low-noise assumptions, while CPQC/AE approaches produce practical circuits but are more sensitive to state preparation and hardware noise. ZX-calculus based mutations are feasible and sometimes beneficial. A recurring conclusion is that significant practical quantum advantage requires either efficient state/distribution loading and/or fault-tolerant or much lower-noise devices.

**Performance claims:**
- AE theoretical error scaling improvement: quantum AE → O(1/N) vs classical MC → O(1/√N) [speculative].
- QFT can be implemented with O(n^2) gates for n qubits (cited as Coppersmith result) vs classical FFT complexity O(n 2^n) when comparing naive counts — implying exponential difference in representation (the paper emphasizes the QFT 'is in some sense even faster') [speculative].
- In simulation, version 2 of the QFT discretization reached discretization-error saturation with ~7 qubits (2^7 amplitudes), whereas the version 1 discretization needed ~16 qubits for comparable accuracy (empirical result reported) [supported].
- Empirical Pareto results: VAns-based QAS yielded lower MSE models (example: European call MSE ~0.0396) compared to circuit-based and ZX-based variants in the presented experiments [supported].
- Empirical observation: QFT-based pricing produced much lower absolute pricing errors (figures shown) than the PQC-based AE methods in simulated experiments provided sufficient qubits and idealized loading [supported].
- Resource scaling fits (empirical transpile counts): single-qubit and two-qubit gate counts and depth show dominant terms scaling like O(2^n) with qubit count n for the end-to-end QFT pipelines; version-specific coefficients were observed with version 1 generally needing fewer two-qubit gates than version 2 in the reported transpilation [supported].
- Empirical noise sensitivity: circuits optimized for minimal depth retained much more accuracy under device-noise simulations than circuits optimized solely for MSE in noise-free runs [supported].
## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** speculative

The thesis presents theoretical reasons and simulation evidence that quantum methods (QFT-based or AE-based) can outperform classical approaches in principle, but emphasizes practical constraints: efficient state/distribution preparation and low-noise or fault-tolerant hardware are required. Thus, a scalable, practical quantum advantage for option pricing is not demonstrated empirically on real noisy hardware and remains conditional/speculative.
## Limitations
- Quantum hardware noise (measurement errors, bit flips, decoherence) limits practical implementation and accuracy of algorithms (explicit).
- Shot noise / stochastic measurement means many repetitions are required to estimate amplitudes/probabilities (explicit).
- Current NISQ devices limit circuit width (qubit count), circuit depth (coherence time) and two-qubit gate counts (error rates) — constraining feasible algorithms (explicit).
- All arithmetic on quantum computers must be reversible, making basic numerical operations and payoff encodings difficult and costly (explicit).
- Efficient state preparation / loading arbitrary amplitude vectors (and distributions) is an unsolved bottleneck for both QFT-based and AE/CPQC approaches (explicit).
- Amplitude Estimation (AE) and related methods require expensive controlled/reflection operations and good oracles; implementing oracles and reflections is often hard and model-dependent (explicit).
- Hadamard-test-based extraction of real parts (for QFT method) introduces many controlled operations and is expensive/error-prone on hardware (explicit).
- QFT-based pricing requires analytic characteristic functions (or at least reliable tractable formulas); not all underlying models satisfy this (explicit).
- QFT pricing requires selecting a dampening factor alpha and cutoffs; inappropriate choices or lack of moment conditions (finite moments) break correctness/accuracy (explicit).
- Numerical quadrature / truncation and discretization in both QFT variants introduce approximation errors and require careful parameter choice (Δ, N) to balance error sources (explicit).
- ZX-diagram-based mutations sometimes produce non-extractable diagrams; extraction to circuits is not guaranteed (explicit).
- Circuit extraction from ZX-diagrams is nontrivial and may fail after mutations — complicating ZX-based QAS (explicit).
- QAS (genetic search) is computationally expensive because each candidate's evaluation requires parameter optimization (explicit).
- The multi-objective selection used is crude; Pareto-front size and selection/filling strategies are ad hoc and may be suboptimal (explicit).
- Barren plateaus and vanishing gradients during optimization of large parameterized circuits impede reliable training (explicit).
- Classical simulators impose strong limits on circuit sizes (so most experiments are simulation-only) and therefore cannot demonstrate quantum advantage (explicit).
- AE and QFT approaches need very low device error rates (near fault-tolerant) and/or efficient state loading to realize practical gains — otherwise classical methods may remain superior (explicit).
- [inferred] The Hadamard test and AE increase circuit complexity substantially, which may negate practical benefits on NISQ hardware even when asymptotic advantages exist.
- [inferred] PQC expressivity/periodicity (trigonometric encodings) can make point-wise approximation of non-periodic payoffs problematic unless careful input scaling/buffering is used.
- [inferred] Multi-asset or higher-dimensional payoffs increase required discretization qubits exponentially, making direct CPQC/QFT scaling challenging for many-asset derivatives.
- [inferred] The genetic QAS lacked a robust crossover operator — limiting evolutionary search power and variety of architectures explored.

## Key ideas

- #idea:hybrid-approach — A multi-objective genetic quantum architecture search (QAS) automates design of parameterized quantum circuits (PQCs) trading off MSE vs circuit complexity, with classical optimizers (jax/jaxopt) training parameters.

## Contradictions
- contradiction:classical-vs-quantum — The dissertation highlights substantial overheads (notably arbitrary amplitude/state preparation and deep reflection operators for AE) that offset QFT/AE theoretical speedups, bringing quantum methods closer to classical baselines in practical settings.
- contradiction:scalability — Experiments are limited to small discretizations (K up to 8000 in 3D synthetic grids) and classical simulation; scaling plots and reported resource counts (depth, CNOTs) suggest significant growth in hardware requirements, contradicting any implicit claim that methods are immediately scalable to market-sized problems.
- contradiction:classical-vs-quantum — Benchmarks against classical Carr–Madan FFT show that classical baselines remain competitive when accounting for state-preparation costs and noise — the quantum advantage is conditional and not demonstrated on hardware.
