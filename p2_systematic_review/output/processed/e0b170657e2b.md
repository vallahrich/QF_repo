## Abstract summary
The paper develops a quantum-computing-based method to estimate value at risk (VaR) for portfolios by extending Monte Carlo via random variable algebra and a Taylor series linearization to handle many assets. To overcome noise in current NISQ devices, the authors use neural networks to compensate circuit noise by tuning qubit rotations, combining real quantum hardware outputs with NN feedback, and validate the approach on real data (up to 139 assets) achieving empirical errors under 1% versus a parametric model.
## Methodology
The authors develop a pipeline to estimate portfolio VaR that combines classical Monte Carlo and parametric approaches, a linear (first-order Taylor) approximation to compress many-asset randomness into a single Gaussian random variable, quantum state preparation to generate samples from the focused tail of that distribution, and a classical neural network to mitigate noise in real NISQ hardware. They compute day-to-day increments, form the covariance matrix and a numerically stable Cholesky factorization (LDL/Cheng-modified Cholesky), then derive coefficients a = m·CHO and σ = sqrt(sum a_i^2) so the portfolio loss is approximated as s ≈ ∑ a_i v_i (v_i ~ N(0,1)). To reduce quantum resource needs they focus sampling on a window in the left tail (low = −3σ to high = −2σ), map computational basis indices to values in that window, and prepare the corresponding amplitude-encoded quantum state using Qiskit state-preparation routines. Circuits (RY rotations and CNOTs) are executed in simulation (StatevectorSimulator) and on IBM Lagos; measurement histograms are mapped back to loss values to obtain the β-adjusted percentile and VaR. A classical dense neural network (3 hidden layers, 500 neurons each) is trained with a custom cost function that runs the quantum circuit with candidate rotation parameters, computes the resulting VaR, and compares it to the target (historical/parametric VaR) to update rotation parameters and compensate for device noise. Experiments include small illustrative examples (3 assets, 80 days), neural-network-assisted runs with 5 qubits and 5 assets, and scalability tests up to 138 assets (6 months of market data) using the linear approximation and Cheng LDL Cholesky.

**Algorithms used:** Monte Carlo simulation (classical), Parametric VaR estimation (Gaussian/log-normal), Cholesky decomposition (Cheng/LDL-stabilized variant), First-order Taylor (linear) approximation of log-normal transform, Quantum state preparation (amplitude encoding; RY/CNOT circuits), Neural network-based noise mitigation (classical dense NN with custom cost)
**Frameworks:** Qiskit (IBM Qiskit, version 0.17.0 mentioned), Keras (for neural network), Qiskit StatevectorSimulator (version 0.1.547)

**Experimental setup:** Combined simulation and real-QPU experiments. Quantum circuits generated with Qiskit state-preparation routines (RY rotations + CNOT topology). Simulations run with Qiskit's StatevectorSimulator; real-device runs used IBM Lagos via IBM Quantum Experience. Neural network training and inference performed with Keras; the NN uses a custom cost that executes quantum circuits (or simulator) to obtain VaR and backpropagates to adjust rotation parameters. Typical real-device shots per circuit: ~1,000–2,000.

**Dataset:** Proprietary historical asset price data provided by a financial institution (paper references both a dataset of investments in the Peruvian market spanning six months comprising up to 138 assets, and smaller illustrative sets such as 3 assets with 80 days of history). Data is restricted and not publicly available per authors' Data Availability Statement.

## Findings
- [supported] A first-order Taylor (linear) approximation of the log-normal Monte Carlo VaR model reduces the multi-asset Monte Carlo sampling problem to sampling a single (or few) Gaussian(s) with analytically computed variance.
- [supported] The linear approximation closely matches classical Monte Carlo and parametric estimates in the tested examples (example: 3- to 5-asset tests and a 138-asset portfolio), with reported small errors.
- [supported] Numerical/stability issues in Cholesky decomposition for large covariance matrices can be mitigated by using LDL-based / Cheng-style Cholesky modifications, enabling calculations for large portfolios (they showed results for ~138 assets).
- [supported] Quantum state preparation circuits (Qiskit state-prep) can be used to generate the focused CDF-window samples required for the VaR tail estimation; circuits were constructed and executed in simulator and on IBM hardware.
- [supported] Current NISQ hardware noise substantially degrades the raw output distribution of the VaR-focused state-preparation circuits (evidence: simulator vs IBM Lagos comparison), displacing the estimated VaR if unmitigated.
- [supported] A classical neural network trained to output rotation parameters for the quantum state-preparation circuit can be used in closed-loop with real quantum hardware to partially compensate hardware noise and produce a VaR estimate close to a parametric target in the tested 5-qubit experiments.
- [supported] In the reported small-scale quantum experiments (5 qubits, 5 grouped assets), the neural-network-corrected quantum execution produced a VaR of USD 1376 vs a parametric target of USD 1380 (difference <1%).
- [supported] The linear approximation + improved Cholesky approach allowed the authors to perform VaR estimation for a real dataset consisting of ~138 assets (classical part).
- [supported] A second-order Taylor expansion reduces approximation error in tests (authors report a reduction from ~1.5% to ~0.22% in one example), at the cost of higher complexity.
- [speculative] The authors suggest that quantum-generated randomness might better capture extreme/’black swan’ market behavior than pseudo-random classical generators (this is proposed but not demonstrated at scale).
- [speculative] The paper asserts that fixed rotation corrections (from the NN) may remain valid over time or could be updated daily; the persistence/timeframe of corrections is unknown and cited as future work.
- [speculative] The authors claim that neural-network-based noise mitigation scales as a practical approach, but experiments demonstrating this at larger qubit counts and at production scale are not provided.
- [speculative] The statement that noise makes current quantum computers 'almost useless' for the task is presented as a generalization based on observed noise effects; this is interpretive and context-dependent rather than a formal measured threshold.

**Results summary:** The paper develops a pipeline for VaR estimation combining (a) a first-order Taylor linearization of the log-normal Monte Carlo model to collapse many-asset sampling into a single Gaussian sample problem, (b) numerically stable Cholesky/LDL factorization to enable scaling to large portfolios (demonstrated on a 138-asset Peru dataset), and (c) quantum state-preparation circuits to draw focused tail samples for VaR estimation. The authors show that noise in current NISQ hardware severely distorts raw quantum outputs, and they propose and empirically test a mitigation strategy in which a classical neural network predicts rotation parameters for the state-prep circuit to compensate noise. On small-scale quantum hardware (5 qubits, 5 grouped assets) they obtained a VaR estimate within <1% of a parametric target. They report modest approximation errors for the linear method versus full Monte Carlo in small tests (e.g., ~1.5%), and note that a second-order expansion can reduce this further (~0.22%). The work demonstrates classical scaling to ~138 assets and small-scale quantum-in-the-loop noise mitigation, while acknowledging limitations of NISQ devices and outlining future work (persistence of NN corrections, larger qubit counts, XAI analysis).

## Limitations
- Noise in current NISQ quantum computers severely degrades circuit outputs and VaR estimates (author-stated).
- Limited number of qubits available in current hardware restricts distribution resolution and precision (author-stated).
- Cholesky decomposition on large covariance matrices can accumulate arithmetic errors and is numerically challenging (author-stated).
- The neural-network-based noise mitigation was tested with a small-scale setup (5 qubits, 5 assets) due to time and resource constraints (author-stated).
- Quantum outputs are intrinsically non-reproducible, creating challenges for regulatory auditing and result replication (author-stated).
- Validated primarily against parametric models and limited real data; broader field validation is lacking (author-stated).
- Use of a first-order Taylor (linear) approximation may introduce approximation error; second-order reduces error but increases complexity (author-stated).
- Potential overfitting and training-stability issues inherent to neural network training and custom cost functions (author-stated/inferred).
- Scaling circuits (depth/qubits) to support amplitude-estimation style quantum speedups requires resources beyond current hardware and increases noise (author-stated/inferred).

## Open questions
- How stable are the learned qubit rotation corrections over time given temporal variations in hardware noise?
- How frequently would rotation corrections need to be recalculated in practical, daily VaR production workflows?
- To what extent do quantum-generated random fluctuations actually capture relevant market 'black swan' behavior compared with classical randomness?
- How well does the proposed mitigation generalize across different quantum hardware topologies and providers?
- What is the optimal balance between increasing qubit count (for precision) and the concomitant increase in noise and required corrections?
- How should regulators and auditors adapt protocols to evaluate and approve VaR estimates derived from intrinsically non-reproducible quantum measurements?
- Can explainable AI (XAI) methods reveal interpretable rules or simpler algorithms behind the neural network's learned rotation corrections?
- How does the choice of grouping strategy for assets affect accuracy and learnability of the neural network corrections?
- What are the scalability limits (number of assets/qubits) of this combined quantum+NN approach in practice?
- How sensitive are results to the choice of CDF windowing parameters and to the order of the Taylor approximation used?
- How robust is the approach to temporal changes in market behavior, i.e., does the model adapt to non-stationary financial time series?
- Can the neural network mitigation handle sudden shifts in hardware calibration or more severe noise events without retraining?
- What is the performance trade-off (accuracy vs. computation time and monetary cost) when moving from simulator to real quantum hardware at scale?
