## Abstract summary
The paper proposes replacing the usual expectation objective in hybrid variational quantum algorithms (VQE and QAOA) for combinatorial optimization with Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) to focus optimization on the low-energy tail of the measurement distribution. Through analytical arguments and experiments on classical simulators and IBM quantum hardware, the authors show CVaR-based optimization converges faster and yields better probabilities of sampling near-optimal solutions, and they analyze limitations of shallow QAOA and the importance of expressive variational forms.
## Methodology
The authors propose replacing the usual expected-value aggregation used in hybrid variational quantum algorithms for combinatorial optimization (VQE and QAOA) with Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) to focus the classical optimizer on the low tail of the objective-value distribution obtained from measurements. They map combinatorial optimization instances (QUBO/Ising) to diagonal Hamiltonians, prepare parameterized trial states with standard variational forms (VQE: single-qubit Y-rotations plus repeated layers of controlled-Z entangling gates; QAOA: alternating problem and mixer unitaries), measure samples of the diagonal Hamiltonian, compute empirical CVaRα by averaging the lowest ceil(αK) samples, and use a classical optimizer (COBYLA) to minimize CVaRα with respect to variational parameters. They evaluate performance analytically (landscape and amplitude-concentration arguments for QAOA) and empirically via (i) noiseless classical simulation using exact states and (ii) experiments on IBM quantum hardware (IBM Q Poughkeepsie). Empirical testing covers multiple combinatorial problems (max stable set, Max3Sat, number partitioning, MaxCut, market split, and portfolio optimization), multiple random instances and problem sizes (qubits from 6 up to 16), a range of CVaR confidence levels α ∈ {1%,5%,10%,25%,50%,75%,100%}, and different circuit depths p (VQE p = 0,1,2; QAOA p = 1,2,3). Performance metrics include probability of sampling an optimal solution (overlap with ground state) and CVaR objective value; comparisons are made against the standard expected-value objective (α = 100%). The implementations use Qiskit; hardware runs use nearest-neighbor entanglement consistent with device connectivity and 8192 measurement shots per parameter evaluation on the QPU. Code to reproduce experiments is provided.

**Algorithms used:** VQE, QAOA, CVaR-VQE (proposed), CVaR-QAOA (proposed), COBYLA (classical optimizer)
**Frameworks:** Qiskit

**Experimental setup:** Classical (noiseless) simulation using exact statevectors for objective evaluation; hardware experiments on IBM Q Poughkeepsie (20-qubit device). In simulation: all-to-all controlled-Z entanglement for the VQE variational form. On hardware: nearest-neighbor entanglement following device connectivity (qubits 5,6,7,10,11,12 in a ring). Classical optimizer: COBYLA. Experiments repeated (hardware runs repeated 5 times for each α).

**Dataset:** Combinatorial optimization benchmark instances generated randomly for: maximum stable set, maximum 3-SAT, number partitioning, maximum cut, market split, and portfolio optimization. Portfolio optimization: a randomized 6-asset instance was used on hardware; the authors provide the generated return vector (µ) and covariance matrix (σ). Portfolio parameters used: risk factor q = 0.5, budget B = 3, penalty λ = 12.

## Findings
- [supported] Replacing the expected-value objective in hybrid variational algorithms (VQE/QAOA) with Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) improves convergence speed and increases the probability of sampling near-optimal or optimal solutions for a variety of combinatorial optimization problems (shown by classical simulation across many random instances and confirmed in small-scale hardware experiments).
- [supported] CVaR-VQE with deeper variational form (example: p=2) and small α (example: α=1%) reaches a nonzero probability of sampling optimal solutions substantially faster than using the expected-value objective (α=100%) in the reported experiments.
- [supported] Empirical study: across the benchmark set, CVaR-VQE (p=2, α=1%) achieved at least 1% probability of sampling an optimal state within 50 normalized classical-optimizer iterations for almost all instances, whereas using α=100% achieved that level for about 60% of instances.
- [supported] On real hardware (IBM Q Poughkeepsie) for a 6-qubit portfolio instance, CVaR-VQE (p=1) with α=10% and α=25% showed earlier and larger gains in the probability of sampling optimal solutions than α=100%; experiments used 8,192 samples per trial wavefunction and were repeated five times.
- [supported] CVaR objective appears more robust to noise/artifacts of current NISQ hardware because it focuses optimization on the best measured samples and can therefore attenuate the impact of low-quality measurements.
- [supported] Practical recommendation from experiments: choosing α in the range ~0.1–0.25 yields good empirical performance (tradeoff between tail emphasis and estimator variance), at the cost that CVaR estimation requires more samples roughly proportional to 1/α to match the accuracy of the expected-value estimator.
- [supported] The variance of the empirical CVaR estimator scales as O(1/(K α^2)) (cited), implying standard error increases as 1/α and motivating more samples for small α.
- [supported] In the experiments QAOA (with its standard parameterization of 2p parameters) performed worse than a richer VQE variational form of comparable depth; VQE used n(1+p) parameters and typically obtained higher overlap with optimal solutions for the same depth.
- [speculative] Theoretical analysis (Proposition 5.1): the optimization landscape changes under CVaR — local minima of the expected-value objective and of the CVaR objective need not coincide; thus CVaR can both remove and introduce different local minima.
- [speculative] Theoretical analysis (Proposition 7.1): QAOA with small, fixed depth and only 2p parameters can produce relatively "flat" states (amplitudes of similar small magnitude), leading to exponentially small overlap with any particular basis state in some problem instances; this behavior can be alleviated by increasing depth or using variational forms with more parameters.
- [speculative] Based on experiments and analysis, for small-depth circuits it is important to use variational forms with a sufficient number of parameters (e.g., at least linear in the number of qubits) to obtain substantial overlap with optimal solutions.

**Results summary:** The paper proposes replacing the usual expected-value objective used in VQE/QAOA for combinatorial optimization with Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR). Across a benchmark suite (340 random problem instances, 14,280 test cases) using classical simulation and on a 6-qubit portfolio instance on IBM hardware, CVaR-based optimization (with small α like 0.01–0.25) consistently speeds up convergence of the classical optimizer and increases the probability of sampling optimal or near-optimal solutions compared to the expected-value objective. The authors provide theoretical analyses showing CVaR changes the optimization landscape and that QAOA with limited parameters at small depth can produce "flat" states with low overlap on optimal basis states. They also report practical guidance on α selection and note the tradeoff that smaller α requires more measurement samples (variance ∝ 1/(K α^2)).

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** not-applicable

The paper does not claim a provable quantum advantage over classical algorithms. It demonstrates improved empirical performance of CVaR-based variational optimization over the standard expected-value objective on small-scale simulated and noisy hardware experiments, and provides theoretical analysis of optimization landscape and QAOA parameterization limitations, but does not claim or demonstrate a general quantum computational speedup or superiority over classical methods.
## Limitations
- CVaR estimator has higher variance: the empirical CVaRα estimator variance scales as O(1/(K α^2)), so achieving the same accuracy as the expected value typically requires increasing the number of samples by a factor ~1/α (author-stated).
- For CVaR with small α there is a soft cap on the maximum probability of sampling the ground state: optimizing CVaRα does not incentivize increasing the ground-state overlap beyond α (author-stated).
- There is no simple mapping between local/global minima of the expected-value objective and the CVaR objective: a local minimum of one need not be a local minimum of the other (Prop. 5.1) (author-stated).
- QAOA with fixed (small) depth has relatively few variational parameters (2p) and may produce 'flat' states with exponentially small amplitude on any particular basis state, yielding low probability to sample optimal solutions (author-stated).
- Improving QAOA by increasing depth may be infeasible on current noisy hardware due to gate errors and decoherence (author-stated).
- The quoted sample-variance dependence (and some variability analyses) are derived for continuous distributions; the authors note the discrete (finite-qubit) case is not rigorously settled and they expect the continuous result to be an approximation as qubit count increases (author-stated).
- Analytical bounds on QAOA amplitude concentration (Proposition 7.1) are loose; the provided lower bounds on the fraction of equal amplitudes can be very loose/exponentially decreasing in p (author-stated).
- The authors explicitly do not claim that CVaR-based VQE/QAOA achieves parity with state-of-the-art classical heuristics (author-stated).
- [inferred] Scalability beyond the studied problem sizes (up to 16 qubits in simulation, 6 qubits on hardware) is unproven: performance and resource requirements on substantially larger instances are not demonstrated.
- [inferred] Generalization to a wider variety of combinatorial/financial problem instances and real-world constraints is not validated; experiments cover a specific set of problem classes only.
- [inferred] The paper uses a single classical optimizer (COBYLA); sensitivity of results to the choice of classical optimizer (gradient-based vs gradient-free, noisy-aware optimizers) is not explored.
- [inferred] Hardware experiments were limited to a specific device topology and nearest-neighbor connectivity; the impact of different hardware architectures and connectivity on CVaR performance is not characterized.
- [inferred] Robustness to realistic noise models, error mitigation strategies, and varying sampling budgets is not comprehensively studied.
- [inferred] Choice of α is empirical in experiments; there is no prescriptive or adaptive strategy provided for selecting α in practice across different problems and sizes.
## Open questions
- What is the optimal choice of α (CVaR tail fraction) for a given problem instance, circuit depth, and sampling budget? Can α be chosen adaptively or scheduled during optimization?
- How should the number of measurement samples K be scaled with α, problem size (number of qubits), and desired accuracy in the discrete (finite-qubit) setting? Can rigorous sample-complexity bounds be derived for discrete objective distributions?
- Can QAOA's 'flatness' at small depth be remedied by alternative variational forms, by increasing parameter counts, or by hybrid strategies that augment QAOA parameters? What is the minimal parameter/depth scaling needed to obtain meaningful ground-state overlap?
- How does CVaR-based optimization scale to larger problem sizes (beyond those tested) and how does it compare against state-of-the-art classical heuristics on practical financial optimization instances?
- How do noise, gate errors, and device-specific constraints affect the relative advantage of CVaR optimization? Can CVaR objectives be combined with error mitigation techniques for improved robustness?
- Does switching the objective from expectation to CVaR alter any approximation-ratio guarantees (e.g., those known for QAOA in some settings)? If so, can new theoretical performance guarantees be established for CVaR-QAOA/CVaR-VQE?
- Can the analytical characterization of amplitude distributions in QAOA (e.g., Proposition 7.1) be tightened to produce practical, less loose bounds that predict when states will be too flat?
- What are principled strategies to combine CVaR (tail-focused) objectives with objectives that incentivize improving average performance or increasing overlap beyond the α threshold?
- How sensitive are the observed empirical gains to the choice of classical optimizer (COBYLA was used) and to optimizer hyperparameters in noisy settings?
- How does connectivity/topology (all-to-all versus nearest-neighbor) and circuit compilation (SWAP overhead) influence CVaR-VQE/CVaR-QAOA effectiveness on real hardware?

**Future work:**
- Investigate increasing circuit depth and/or the number of variational parameters (and improved variational forms) to address QAOA flatness and improve sampling overlap as hardware improves (authors note this as required to improve QAOA performance).
- Provide tighter theoretical analysis and bounds for QAOA amplitude concentration and the behavior of variational algorithms (the authors note their current bounds are loose and may be improved).
- Derive rigorous sample-complexity results for empirical CVaR estimation in the discrete/finitesize (finite-qubit) regime, rather than relying on continuous-distribution results.
- Extend empirical studies to larger problem instances and more diverse combinatorial/financial optimization problems to evaluate scalability and real-world applicability.
- Study the robustness of CVaR-based optimization under realistic noise models and across different quantum hardware backends, including evaluation of error mitigation techniques combined with CVaR.
- Explore adaptive or scheduled schemes for selecting α during optimization, and systematic guidelines for balancing α and sampling budget K in practice.
- Examine the effect of different classical optimizers and optimizer settings on the performance of CVaR-VQE/CVaR-QAOA, particularly under shot noise and hardware errors.
- Analyze trade-offs between optimizing CVaR (tail focus) and other performance metrics (e.g., expected objective, approximation ratios), and develop hybrid objective strategies if beneficial.
## Key ideas
- #idea:quantum-advantage — Replacing the usual expectation objective with a CVaR-α objective in variational algorithms (VQE/QAOA) improves convergence and increases the probability of sampling near-optimal combinatorial solutions on small instances.
- #idea:hybrid-approach — The method is a hybrid workflow: quantum state preparation and sampling combined with classical optimization (COBYLA) using empirical CVaR computed from measurement samples to guide parameter updates.
- #idea:near-term-feasibility — Demonstrated on noiseless simulators and on IBM Q hardware (6 qubits for portfolio instance, additional simulated instances up to 16 qubits) indicating NISQ-era applicability, but with caveats.
- #idea:quantum-advantage — CVaR-α tuning (small α values) focuses the optimizer on the low-energy tail and yields better empirical overlap with ground states than standard expectation objectives across tested combinatorial problems.
- #idea:near-term-feasibility — Hardware experiments (8192 shots) show CVaR-based objectives remain beneficial under real-device noise, but performance is constrained by shallow circuit depth and device connectivity.
## Contradictions
- #contradiction:scalability — The paper documents limitations of shallow QAOA (amplitude concentration and unfavorable landscapes) and shows that expressive variational forms and increased depth are needed; this challenges optimistic claims that low-depth variational algorithms will scale straightforwardly to larger, practical problem sizes.
