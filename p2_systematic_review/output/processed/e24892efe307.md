## Abstract summary
The paper analyzes a recently proposed quantum blind signature scheme for supply chain finance and identifies critical weaknesses, including susceptibility to intercept-measurement-resend and entanglement-measurement attacks, ineffective blinding against insiders, and existential forgery by the blind signer. It proposes an improved scheme (SCF-IQBS) that reduces quantum resource use, removes the need for swap tests, extends the message space to classical bits, and provides a formal security model with proofs reducing blindness and unforgeability to quantum-mechanical principles, validated via Qiskit simulation.
## Methodology
The authors perform a theoretical cryptanalysis of an existing quantum blind signature scheme (SCF-QBS) and propose an improved scheme (SCF-IQBS). Their methodology combines analytic security proofs, explicit attack constructions, and small-scale quantum-circuit simulations to validate the attacks and the corrected design. Analytically they (1) identify and formalize specific attack vectors (intercept-measure-resend (IMR), entanglement-measurement (EM), and existential forgery) against the original scheme, (2) derive state-evolution equations showing why those attacks escape detection, (3) demonstrate how knowledge of measurement-derived keys breaks the blinding and enables forgery, and (4) propose an improved protocol using decoy-particle insertion, keyed basis rotations and hash-based classical checks. They formalize security via a game-based model (correctness, blindness, unforgeability in the random-oracle model) and reduce security to quantum primitives (indistinguishability / trace distance, no-cloning / inability to distinguish unknown non-orthogonal states). To validate the analytic claims they implement quantum-circuit simulations in Qiskit to (a) prepare the chi-state, (b) run the IMR and EM attack circuits (e.g., intercept-measure and CNOT-entangling ancilla), (c) map measurement bases to computational basis with gate transforms, and (d) demonstrate decryption/forgery operations and that attack traces are consistent with the theory. They also perform resource accounting (quantum-bit consumption) and provide a toy example (n=4) to illustrate protocol flow and verification.

**Algorithms used:** Quantum One-Time Pad (QOTP), Quantum Key Distribution (QKDP) - used as assumed secure key provision, Chi-state preparation (|χ⟩ four-particle entangled state), Pauli operations (X, Z, Y) used for encryption/manipulation, Controlled-NOT (CNOT) entangling operation (used in EM attack), Intercept-Measure-Resend attack (IMR) - implemented as measurement in specific basis and resend, Entanglement-Measurement (EM) attack (ancilla entanglement + measurement), Random-oracle (hash) model (classical hash functions modelled as random oracle)
**Frameworks:** Qiskit

**Experimental setup:** Quantum-circuit simulations performed using IBM Qiskit (Python 3.11.7, Qiskit 1.4.0). The authors constructed circuits to prepare the chi-state (|χ⟩), implement measurement-base mapping gates (Hadamard, CNOT, CZ where needed), simulate IMR by intermediate measurements and resend, simulate EM by CNOT entanglement with ancilla qubits, and run decryption/modification circuits to show plaintext recovery and forgery on small example inputs (example shown with n=4).
## Experiment details
### Input
No classical financial dataset was used. Simulation inputs consisted of prepared quantum states: n copies of the four-particle chi-state |χ⟩3214 (examples with n=4 shown), sequences Q1..Q4, randomly inserted decoy single-qubit states drawn from {|0>,|1>,|+>,|->}, example plaintext quantum messages such as |P> = |0>|1>|+>|->, example key strings (e.g. K_AC=01100101, K_BC=00101101) and random bitstrings (e.g. r=1001). The experiments explored both general- n parameters and a toy instance with n=4.

### Process
1) Prepare quantum circuits to generate |χ⟩ states using H, CNOT and CZ gates; 2) For channel tests, insert decoy single-qubit states into sequences and publish positions/states for detection; 3) Simulate IMR by intercepting particle sequences, measuring in the expected basis (Bz for particle 1, Bx for particle 4), recording results, and retransmitting measured states; 4) Simulate EM by applying CNOT with the target particle as control and an ancilla as target to entangle an auxiliary qubit, then measuring the ancilla to infer target outcomes; 5) Map AMB and Bx/Bz basis measurements to computational basis using gate transforms (Hadamard and multi-qubit unitaries) for analysis; 6) Run encryption/decryption circuits using Pauli operations representing QOTP to demonstrate plaintext recovery by an adversary and to construct forgery by applying uniform Pauli modifications; 7) Collect measurement outcomes (statevectors or measurement counts) and compare with theoretical predictions and verification checks (e.g. equality of recovered messages), and evaluate resource counts (quantum bits consumed). Specific numeric parameters used in illustrative examples: n=4 chi-states, example key strings and r bits as above. The simulator mode (statevector or qasm) and shot counts are not specified in the text.

### Output
Simulation outputs consisted of quantum measurement results and statevector consistency checks showing that (a) IMR and EM attacks yield measurement outcomes that match legitimate participants' announced values (i.e., attacks are undetectable under the original scheme), (b) an adversary with those measurement results can recover the plaintext |P> after QOTP decryption, and (c) Pauli-based modifications produce forged messages that pass the verification check. Outputs reported qualitatively and via example circuit measurement histograms/state results (toy example with n=4). No quantitative performance metrics (e.g., success probabilities beyond analytic expressions, timing, or shot-based error rates) or baselines are provided beyond analytic probability expressions for attack success.

## Findings

- [supported] The original SCF-QBS scheme is vulnerable to an entanglement-measure (EM) attack that lets an adversary entangle an auxiliary qubit to steal first-particle measurement outcomes without introducing detectable errors (verified via Qiskit simulation).

**Results summary:** This theoretical paper analyzes a previously proposed quantum blind signature scheme for supply-chain finance (SCF-QBS), demonstrating two practical attack classes (intercept-measure-resend and entanglement-measure) that allow adversaries—including insiders—to obtain measurement-derived keys and break blinding; these attacks and sample demonstrations were validated using Qiskit simulations. ## Limitations
- The original SCF-QBS scheme is vulnerable to intercept-measurement-resend (IMR) and entanglement-measurement (EM) attacks; its blinding is ineffective against insider attackers and the scheme allows existential forgery (author-stated).
- Security analysis of SCF-IQBS relies on the assumption of an authenticated classical channel and an unconditionally secure quantum key distribution protocol (QKDP) (author-stated).
- Security proofs for SCF-IQBS are given in the random oracle model (idealized hash functions), which is an idealization (author-stated).
- Feasibility, attack demonstrations, and verification are performed using the Qiskit simulator rather than experimental quantum hardware, limiting evidence about real-device behavior (author-stated).
- SCF-IQBS still requires significant quantum resources: n χ-states and 2n decoy particles for initialization and a total consumption of 7n quantum bits to sign an n-bit message (author-stated).
- [inferred] Robustness to realistic noise, decoherence, gate errors and imperfect measurements is not analyzed; security/correctness proofs assume ideal operations.
- [inferred] Practical scalability to larger message sizes, many participants, and networked supply-chain deployments is not evaluated (simulations were used but scope/scale are not reported).
- [inferred] Preparing, distributing and managing multipartite χ-states and decoy particles in current NISQ-era hardware may be challenging; implementation complexity and fault tolerance are not addressed.
- [inferred] The security reductions rely on the indistinguishability of quantum ciphertexts and inability to distinguish non-orthogonal states; real-world side-channels or device imperfections could weaken these assumptions.
