## Abstract summary
This preprint proposes a quantum algorithm to price multi-asset derivatives by solving the Black–Scholes PDE via a finite-difference method combined with quantum linear-system-based ODE solvers. To avoid costly amplitude readout of a single grid-point price, the authors compute the present price as the expected value of the future price, preparing quantum states for the price vector and the log-price distribution and estimating their inner product. The method achieves exponential speedup in dimensionality (logarithmic dependence on the number of assets) compared with classical FDM under stated assumptions.
## Methodology
This is a theoretical/methodological paper proposing a quantum algorithmic pipeline to price multi-asset derivatives by solving the Black–Scholes PDE via a finite-difference method (FDM) and quantum algorithms for linear ODEs/linear systems. The authors (1) transform the Black–Scholes PDE into log-asset coordinates and a backward-time PDE, (2) discretize spatial variables with an ngr^d finite-difference grid to produce an ODE system d/dτ ˜Y(τ)=F˜Y(τ)+C(τ), where the matrix F is expressed as sums of Kronecker products of tridiagonal matrices and C collects boundary conditions, (3) assume/choose t_ter > 0 and express the present price V0 as an expected value of the discounted price at time t_ter so as to avoid direct amplitude readout of a single amplitude, (4) prepare two quantum states: |¯p⟩ encoding the (discretized) probability distribution of log-prices at t_ter (via a multivariate extension of Grover–Rudolph style state preparation for Gaussian densities) and |˜Y(τ_ter)⟩ encoding the FDM solution at t_ter, (5) use a quantum algorithm for linear ODEs (based on quantum linear system algorithms and the Cm,k,p construction from Berry et al. / Childs et al.) to produce a quantum state that includes the discretized solution vector as amplitudes, (6) modify the linear system (adding repeated blocks and a uniform vector γ) so that the desired inner product p·˜Y(τ_ter) can be extracted by estimating overlaps, and (7) apply quantum amplitude estimation (QAE) to estimate the inner product ⟨¯p|˜Y⟩ (together with an estimate of a normalization/probability factor obtained from the modified system) and thereby compute an approximation to V0. The methodology includes detailed error analyses and parameter-setting criteria (grid spacing hi, choice of t_ter, assumptions of smoothness of solution and bounded derivatives, bounds on contributions from outside the truncated domain), explicit requirements for oracles (sparsity and entry-access oracles for F, state-preparation oracles for initial/payoff and boundary vectors), and complexity estimates showing exponential improvement in dependence on the PDE dimensionality d compared to classical FDM (up to factors that are products/logarithms in d). Several lemmas/theorems give bounds on discretization error, probability mass outside boundaries, and the complexity/precision trade-offs for the quantum subroutines. The paper is non-empirical and focuses on algorithm design, proofs, and query-complexity analysis rather than numerical experiments.

**Algorithms used:** Quantum algorithm for linear ODEs (Berry et al. construction, Cm,k,p), Quantum linear system algorithms (QLSA / HHL-family; Childs et al. 2017 precision-improved QLSA), Quantum Amplitude Estimation (QAE), Grover–Rudolph style state preparation (multivariate extension for Gaussian/marginal densities)

This claim is theoretical and supported by complexity analyses and proofs in the manuscript, contingent on several assumptions (availability of oracles/state-preparation, bounded condition numbers, time-independence of certain boundary terms, and smoothness). ## Limitations
- The algorithm requires the PDE solution (Y) to be smooth in asset-price directions (Assumption 2.1: 4th derivatives bounded).
- The method assumes the boundary contribution vector ⃗C(τ) is time-independent (Assumption 5.2).
- The payoff must be upper-bounded by a linear function in asset prices (Assumption 4.1).
- The probability density × solution product must have bounded second mixed derivatives (Assumption 5.1).
- Availability of oracles for the discretized PDE matrix F (OF,1, OF,2) and for state preparation of ⃗˜fpay and ⃗C is assumed (Assumption 5.3).
- Need to know (or obtain) a positive scalar γ comparable to the RMS of grid solution values (Assumption 5.4); authors note this is hard to determine a priori.
- The complexity bound depends on condition number κV of the diagonalizing matrix of F and on g := maxτ ∥⃗˜Y(τ)∥/∥⃗˜Y(τter)∥; these factors can increase cost and are problem-dependent.
- Although the algorithm removes (1/ϵ)^{poly(d)} factors, it still contains multiplicative factors that grow exponentially with d via products like ∏_i (ui − li) and ∏_i Δ_i (log-widths to the power d) — i.e. exponential-in-d dependence has not been fully eliminated.
- The approach requires careful choice of many numerical/algorithmic parameters (grid spacing h_i, p, k, mgr, tter); selecting them in practice is nontrivial and may require empirical tuning.
- The method relies on quantum linear-system subroutines and quantum amplitude estimation; resource counts assume availability of high-quality large-scale quantum hardware and efficient implementations of those primitives.
- [inferred] The requirement to prepare complex oracles (OF,1/2 and O_⃗˜fpay/O_⃗C) may be impractical for general payoffs or boundary conditions and could carry large classical precomputation or circuit depth.
- [inferred] The state-preparation procedure for the multivariate Gaussian-like distribution (|¯p⟩ / |ϕ⟩) will have nontrivial computational cost and might be challenging for large d or nontrivial covariance ρ.
- [inferred] The need to set tter small enough to avoid boundary-crossing but large enough to make amplitude-reading feasible presents a trade-off that can constrain problem instances.
- [inferred] The analysis assumes access to (or good bounds on) quantities like ∥⃗˜Y(τter)∥, ∥⃗˜fpay∥, ∥⃗C∥, ∥F∥ and det(ρ); obtaining/estimating these may be expensive or unstable in practice.
- [inferred] The algorithm does not address American/Bermudan (early-exercise) derivatives; nonlinear early-exercise (max) operations are incompatible with the linear-algebra based quantum subroutines used.
## Open questions
- How can the requirement of time-independent boundary/source vector ⃗C(τ) be relaxed so the algorithm handles time-dependent boundary conditions?
- Can the approach be extended to price early-exercisable derivatives (American/Bermudan), and if so how to represent/implement the non-linear max/exercise operation in a quantum algorithmic framework?
- How to efficiently construct the assumed oracles (OF,1, OF,2, O_⃗˜fpay, O_⃗C) for realistic, possibly high-dimensional and nontrivial payoffs and boundary conditions?
- What practical strategies can be used to choose numerical/algorithmic parameters (grid spacing h_i, p, k, γ, mgr, tter) robustly without knowing quantities like ∥⃗˜Y(τter)∥ in advance?
- How sensitive is the quantum algorithm's performance and error to the condition number κV and to the growth factor g for typical financial PDE discretizations? Are there effective preconditioning strategies in this PDE/FDM context?
- How large are the hidden (problem-dependent) constants in the complexity expressions for realistic multi-asset derivatives, and do they limit practical quantum advantage for moderate d?
- How to implement the multivariate state preparation (for the probability distribution of log-prices) with scalable gate depth and low error for correlated Gaussian distributions?
- How does the method compare empirically (in wall-clock and resource terms) to other quantum PDE solvers (e.g., spectral-method based algorithms) and to classical high-dimensional methods (Monte Carlo, quasi-Monte Carlo, sparse-grid FDM)?
- What are the error/variance trade-offs related to using tter > 0 (solving to a future time) versus attempting direct amplitude readout at t = 0?
- What is the expected performance on near to intermediate-scale quantum hardware (NISQ/early fault-tolerant): what are resource requirements (qubits, depth) for practically relevant instances?

**Future work:**
- Mitigate and relax the assumptions made in this work (for example, remove the time-independence requirement on ⃗C(τ)).
- Extend the algorithm to handle time-dependent boundary/source terms ⃗C(τ), possibly by adapting or extending the ODE/PDE quantum solvers used.
- Investigate how to price early-exercisable (American/Bermudan) derivatives with quantum FDM, addressing the nonlinearity of the exercise (max) operation.
- Study extensions/generalizations that allow application to a wider range of derivatives beyond the current assumptions.
- Explore practical parameter-selection procedures (empirical searches, heuristics) for the numerical and algorithmic parameters (p, k, h_i, γ, etc.) and study their impact on convergence and complexity.
- Investigate whether the methods of other quantum PDE algorithms (e.g., spectral method based algorithms) can complement or replace parts of the proposed approach when model parameters are smooth in time.
- Examine the construction and implementation of the required oracles and state preparation routines for realistic payoff and boundary models.
