## Abstract summary
The paper introduces two quantum algorithms to probe microcanonical and canonical properties of many-body systems at finite energies. The first is a hybrid quantum-classical method that uses a cosine filter and interferometric measurements to compute expectation values in a finite energy window without preparing the filtered state, with provable polynomial scaling in system size, inverse variance, and inverse error. The second is a quantum-assisted Monte Carlo sampling approach that employs a quantum device to evaluate sampling probabilities—circumventing the sign problem for suitable initial states—and both methods are designed to be practical on near-term digital and analog quantum simulators.
## Methodology
The paper proposes two complementary algorithmic methodologies to estimate microcanonical and canonical expectation values for many-body quantum systems without requiring preparation of narrow-energy (filtered) states. The first is a hybrid quantum-classical algorithm based on a cosine filter operator P_δ(E) (approximated by a Gaussian), which projects onto an energy window of width δ about energy E. P_δ(E) is expanded into a finite linear combination of real-time evolution operators with coefficients cm so that numerator and denominator of filtered expectation values A_{δ,ψ}(E) and A'_{δ,ψ}(E) can be expressed as linear combinations of interferometric time-dependent quantities a_ψ(t)=⟨ψ|e^{-iHt}|ψ⟩ and a_{A,ψ}(t)=⟨ψ|Ae^{-iHt}|ψ⟩. The quantum device is used only to estimate those time-series correlators for a discrete set of evolution times t_m and the classical computer performs the linear combination and postprocessing. The paper gives complexity proofs showing polynomial scaling in system size N, inverse filter width 1/δ, and inverse target error 1/ε. The second methodology is a quantum-assisted Monte Carlo approach: rewrite ensemble averages (microcanonical or canonical) as weighted integrals/sums over an (over)complete basis of easily-preparable states (e.g., product states), where the sampling weights are D_{δ,ψ}(E)=⟨ψ|P_δ(E)|ψ⟩. A classical Monte Carlo sampler (e.g., Metropolis–Hastings) proposes moves in the basis; the quantum device evaluates D_{δ,ψ}(E) (and A_{δ,ψ}(E) if needed) for acceptance probabilities, thereby avoiding sign-problem issues. The paper also provides a set of practical measurement/implementation alternatives tailored to analog simulators: (i) conditional dynamics with a control qubit (interferometric measurement), (ii) schemes using additional internal levels to avoid continuous coupling to a control qubit, (iii) cat-like state preparations to extract correlators without ancillas, (iv) sequential single-qubit superposition schemes (no cat states) at the cost of more measurements, and (v) simplifications exploiting symmetry relations of H and A. The methodologies are illustrated and benchmarked numerically on solvable fermionic/Ising-type models (Jordan–Wigner mapped Ising chain) using exact/numerical methods to compute the same quantities for comparison. Practical parameter choices and measurement-cost estimates (e.g., number of time-steps R, maximum evolution time t_max, and sample counts for Monte Carlo) are discussed in depth.

**Algorithms used:** Cosine-filtering (P_δ(E)) / Gaussian approximation, Quantum phase-estimation-like time-series expansion of filters, Quantum-assisted Monte Carlo (importance sampling using D_{δ,ψ}(E)), Interferometric measurement protocols (conditional dynamics), Time-series-based reconstruction of filtered expectation values, Chebyshev / alternative filter expansions (discussed as variants)

## Findings
- [speculative] Introduces two quantum-algorithmic approaches to compute microcanonical and canonical properties: (1) a hybrid quantum-classical algorithm based on a cosine filter and interferometric measurements that recovers expectation values in a finite energy window without preparing the filtered state; (2) quantum-assisted Monte Carlo methods that use a quantum device to evaluate sampling probabilities and thereby compute microcanonical/canonical quantities.
- [speculative] Proves (theoretically) that the hybrid algorithm can compute filtered-state expectation values in time scaling polynomially in the number of qubits N, the inverse filter width (1/δ), and the inverse precision (1/ε), given an initial state that can be efficiently prepared and access to interferometric measurements.
- [supported] Demonstrates numerically (for benchmark Ising-like models) that the algorithms are implementable for moderate sizes (examples up to N≈100 for certain quantities) and that practical variants (optimized choices of expansion parameters) yield sensible results.
- [speculative] Shows theoretically that one can choose an energy E in a small interval around the mean energy of the initial state so that the filter overlap is not exponentially small (hence the algorithm avoids exponentially small denominators), with explicit bounds derived.
- [speculative] Argues that the required coherent evolution times per run are not large (maximum evolution time scales as O(1/δ) with a modest constant factor; e.g., authors quote ≤6/δ in practice) but that the number of repeated measurements can be significant.
- [supported] Provides numerical benchmarks and resource estimates (measurement counts, Monte Carlo sampling sizes) exhibiting practical feasibility trade-offs for near-term/analog devices; e.g., example estimates and simulations showing 10^4–10^6 measurements for certain parameter choices and Monte Carlo tests with 10^5 samples per point.
- [speculative] Claims quantum-assisted Monte Carlo algorithms circumvent the classical quantum Monte Carlo sign problem (sampling probabilities are positive) provided one can prepare states of suitable energy efficiently; this is argued theoretically rather than proven by large-scale experiment.
- [supported] Presents empirical evidence (numerical experiments) that microcanonical/canonical expectation values can be approximated with the proposed sampling methods for the tested models/parameter regimes, and that the methods are reasonably robust to some noise models in simulations.
- [supported] Reports numerical evidence that for recovering microcanonical expectation values of pure filtered states, the filter width δ must generally decrease with system size N; data indicate δ scaling like polynomially small in 1/N (e.g., δ ∝ 1/N^2 gives faster convergence in tested non-integrable examples).
- [speculative] Proposes several experimentally-oriented measurement variants (conditional dynamics with a control qubit; use of extra internal levels; cat-like state methods; sequential/qubit-by-qubit product-state methods) with trade-offs between coherence requirements and measurement counts.
- [speculative] States (authors' claim) they are unaware of any classical algorithm achieving the same polynomial scaling for these finite-energy expectation-value tasks.

**Results summary:** The preprint proposes two complementary approaches to extract finite-energy (microcanonical and canonical) properties of many-body quantum systems using quantum simulators. The first is a hybrid algorithm that uses a cosine filter expansion and interferometric time-series measurements to compute expectation values in a target energy window without needing to prepare the filtered state; the authors provide a theoretical argument that the runtime scales polynomially in system size N, 1/δ (filter width) and 1/ε (error). The second comprises quantum-assisted Monte Carlo sampling methods that use a quantum device to compute sampling probabilities (positive by construction), thereby alleviating the classical sign problem under the condition that suitable-energy states can be prepared. The paper also presents several implementational variants tailored to analog and near-term devices, provides numerical benchmarks on Ising-like models (up to N≈100), and offers resource estimates (evolution times, measurement counts) illustrating practical trade-offs.

**Performance claims:**
- The hybrid algorithm runs in time T = poly(N, 1/δ, 1/ε) to obtain expectation values (theoretical claim).
- Maximum coherent evolution time per run is O(1/δ); authors quote in practice runs with t_max ≤ 6/δ (practical estimate used in examples).
- Rough measurement-count estimate: total number of measurements of order R ε^{-2} ≈ 3 * sqrt(N) / (δ * ε^2); example: for N=100, δ=1, ε=10^{-2} this yields ~3×10^5 measurements (numerical/heuristic estimate provided).
- Numerical experiments used Monte Carlo sampling sizes of ~10^5 samples per point in benchmark plots (empirical).
- Numerical validation performed on Ising-like models up to N≈100 for certain observables; exact diagonalization benchmarks up to N≈28 for non-integrable Ising chain used to study δ scaling (empirical).
- Empirical observation in tested non-integrable examples: convergence of filtered-state microcanonical expectation A'_δ,ψ(E) appears fast when δ ∝ 1/N^2, and reasonably close for δ ∝ 1/N (numerical evidence).
## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** theoretical

The authors present theoretical (provable) claims that the hybrid algorithm computes finite-energy expectation values in time polynomial in N, 1/δ and 1/ε, and argue that they are unaware of classical algorithms with the same polynomial scaling. This suggests a potential quantum advantage for the stated task, but the advantage is not empirically demonstrated on problem sizes beyond classical reach; thus the claim is theoretical/speculative rather than experimentally demonstrated.
## Limitations
- Requires an initial state that can be efficiently prepared; the range of accessible energies is limited by the energies of such states (author-stated).
- Algorithm relies on interferometric/conditional-evolution measurements (access to a control qubit or equivalent). This can be challenging on some analog platforms (author-stated).
- Alternatives to conditional dynamics (e.g. extra internal levels, cat-like states, sequential schemes) impose additional experimental requirements or increase measurement overhead (author-stated).
- The number of measurements can be very large: measurement cost scales roughly as R · ε^{-2} (authors estimate ~O(√N / (δ ε^2)) ), so the method may demand many repetitions in practice (author-stated).
- For microcanonical expectation values of filtered pure states, the filter width δ generally needs to shrink with system size N to converge; numerical evidence indicates δ must decrease polynomially with N in nontrivial cases (author-stated).
- The quantum-assisted Monte Carlo sampling algorithms are heuristic and not proven to be efficient in general (author-stated).
- The denominator ⟨ψ|P_δ(E)|ψ⟩ can be exponentially small in N for some choices of ψ and E, creating practical and precision problems (author-stated).
- Sign-problem avoidance holds only if one can prepare states with suitable energies; the technique does not magically remove the need to prepare such states (author-stated).
- The algorithms assume the quantum device can efficiently implement time evolution under H and perform the required interferometric measurements; these are nontrivial operational assumptions (author-stated).
- Practical applicability is limited to energy regions where the broadened local density of states D_{δ,ψ}(E) is non-negligible; at extreme energies the Monte Carlo and direct methods become unstable (author-stated).
- [inferred] Although asymptotic scaling is polynomial in key parameters, prefactors, constants, and realistic device overheads may make the approach resource-intensive for large problem sizes.
- [inferred] Preparing cat-like states or using extra internal levels may be very hard or impossible on many hardware platforms, limiting which variants are implementable.
- [inferred] Sensitivity to realistic noise and decoherence (especially for schemes that evolve entangled/cat-like states) may degrade performance and increase required measurements.
- [inferred] Numerical demonstrations focus on integrable or small-to-moderate-size models; scalability and behavior for complex, strongly correlated, or higher-dimensional systems remain to be validated.
