## Abstract summary
The paper proposes warm-start strategies for quantum optimization, initializing QAOA (and related hybrid algorithms) from solutions of continuous relaxations (QP/SDP) and from randomized-rounding outputs (e.g., Goemans–Williamson) so that the quantum method can inherit classical approximation guarantees. It introduces continuous and rounded warm-start variants, a regularization parameter to interpolate to standard QAOA, and demonstrates in simulations that warm-starting improves low-depth QAOA performance on portfolio optimization and that GW-based warm starts systematically improve Recursive QAOA for MAXCUT. The authors discuss design choices, scalability trade-offs, and implications under the Unique Games Conjecture.
## Methodology
The authors propose and evaluate warm-start strategies for quantum optimization algorithms, primarily QAOA and Recursive-QAOA (RQAOA). They obtain continuous relaxations of binary problems (QP or SDP) and use the relaxation solution to prepare an informed initial quantum state via single-qubit Y-rotations (θ_i derived from c*_i) and a corresponding warm-start mixer Hamiltonian H_ws whose ground state is that initial state. A regularization parameter ε∈[0,0.5] is introduced to avoid reachability issues and to interpolate between warm-start and standard QAOA. For MAXCUT, they also use randomized rounding (Goemans–Williamson) in classical pre-processing to generate candidate cuts; these are used to initialize multiple WS-QAOA runs, aggregate sampled probabilities to form a correlation matrix M, and drive the recursive variable-elimination of RQAOA (WS-RQAOA). Optimization of variational parameters (β,γ) is performed with a classical optimizer (COBYLA), seeded either randomly or via a targeted grid search for depth-one. Experiments are simulation-based: state-vector simulation (Qiskit) for portfolio instances and an efficient classical simulation for depth-one RQAOA (two-qubit reduced density-matrix computation). Metrics include optimized state energy (normalized to ground-state energy), probability of sampling the optimal solution, and cut size relative to the maximum cut. Comparisons are made against standard QAOA, standard RQAOA, the GW algorithm, and a classical recursive GW procedure.

**Algorithms used:** QAOA, Warm-start QAOA (WS-QAOA), Recursive QAOA (RQAOA), Warm-start RQAOA (WS-RQAOA), Goemans–Williamson randomized hyperplane rounding, Trotterized (digitized) annealing, COBYLA classical optimizer, Classical SDP/QP solvers (CPLEX)
**Frameworks:** Qiskit, IBM ILOG CPLEX

**Experimental setup:** All quantum experiments were simulated (no QPU). Portfolio experiments used the Qiskit state-vector simulator; depth-one RQAOA used an efficient classical simulation based on two-qubit reduced density matrices. Classical relaxations (QP/SDP) were solved with IBM ILOG CPLEX 12.10.0. Variational parameters were optimized with COBYLA (classical).

**Dataset:** Synthetic financial time-series (simulated) to build small portfolio instances (n=6 assets) and synthetic graph instances for MAXCUT (random and fully connected graphs with integer weights).
## Experiment details
### Input
Portfolio: Geometric Brownian motion simulated for n=6 assets over N=250 trading days (Si,k = Si,0 exp((µi - σi^2/2)k/N + σi W_k)); returns ri,k computed to obtain expected returns µ and covariance Σ. Budget constraint B=3 enforced by quadratic penalty λ(1^T x - B)^2 with λ=3. 250 random portfolio instances were generated. MAXCUT: synthetic graphs of sizes n=20 and n=30. Two graph families: Erdös–Rényi-like with edge probability pE=1/2 and random ±1 unit weights; and fully connected graphs with integer weights sampled uniformly from {-10,...,10}. For GW pre-solver, N=10 randomized-hyperplane cuts generated per graph and the best M=5 unique cuts retained as WS-QAOA initializations.

### Process
1) Classical relaxation: solve QP (when PSD) or SDP (for general QUBO/MAXCUT) to obtain continuous solution c* or SDP vectors. 2) Prepare initial quantum state: single-qubit RY(θ_i) rotations with θ_i = 2 arcsin(√(c*_i)) (with regularization ε handling near-0/1 values). 3) Define warm-start mixer H_ws that has the prepared state as ground state (or a modified mixer to allow retaining GW rounding). 4) Run QAOA (depth p varied, experiments with p=1..6) applying layers of exp(-i γ_k H_C) and the time evolution of H_ws (implemented via single-qubit rotations  RY(±θ_i) RZ(-2β_k) RY(∓θ_i)). 5) Optimize the variational parameters (β,γ) with COBYLA; for depth-one, a grid search over γ (25 values) and fitting sin terms for β provided initial seeds. 6) Measure outcomes (state-vector probabilities in simulator) to compute energies and sampling probabilities. 7) For WS-RQAOA: run M WS-QAOA instances initialized from GW cuts, aggregate outcome probabilities to form averaged probabilities ¯p_i, compute correlation matrix M via averaged probabilities, identify largest |M_ij| and eliminate one variable via replacement zi := sign(M_ij) z_j, repeat until graph reduced to n_stop (n/2), then solve reduced problem classically (diagonalization). 8) Additional experiments: Trotterized annealing schedules β_k,γ_k with total time T and N steps; analysis of ε effect and use of modified mixers to recover GW rounding at p=1.

### Output
Outputs comprised: optimized variational-state energies normalized to the exact minimum E0 (from exact diagonalization), probabilities of sampling the optimal binary solution, histograms of cut sizes relative to maximum cut (percent), counts of instances where maximum cut found, and energy landscapes E(β,γ) for visualization. Baselines included standard QAOA (equal-superposition initial state and X mixer), standard RQAOA, the Goemans–Williamson randomized-hyperplane cuts, and a classical recursive GW-based heuristic. Results were reported as medians and interquartile ranges over multiple runs/initializations.

## Findings
- [supported] Warm-starting QAOA (WS-QAOA) by initializing the variational state from the solution of a continuous relaxation (QP or SDP) improves low-depth performance in numerical simulations (portfolio and small MAXCUT instances).
- [supported] In a 6-asset portfolio example (n=6, budget B=3) WS-QAOA increased the probability of sampling the optimal discrete portfolio by more than 5× compared to standard QAOA across simulated depths 1–5.
- [supported] WS-QAOA with a matching 'warm-start' mixer (constructed so the relaxation solution is the mixer ground state) yields optimized trial states with energies closer to the true ground state energy than standard QAOA in simulations.
- [supported] When simulated as a Trotterized anneal, the warm-start mixer required less total annealing time T and fewer Trotter steps N to reach low energy than the equal-superposition (standard QAOA) mixer in the examples studied.
- [supported] Using Goemans–Williamson (GW) randomized hyperplane rounding as a classical pre-solver to provide binary warm-starts (WS-RQAOA) leads to systematically larger cuts (better objective values) than standard RQAOA and than the base GW cuts in the simulated random / fully connected graphs tested (n=20,30).
- [supported] In the MAXCUT experiments reported, depth-one WS-QAOA initialized from GW cuts with regularization parameter ε=0.25 produced median normalized cut energies exceeding the GW expected approximation (reported median value ≈0.929 in the studied set vs GW constant ≈0.878).
- [supported] WS-RQAOA (using multiple GW warm-starts and aggregating variational states) outperformed standard RQAOA and a purely classical recursive-GW elimination heuristic in the simulated ensembles of graphs considered.
- [speculative] Theoretical argument: a warm-started quantum algorithm can inherit the approximation guarantees of the underlying continuous relaxation (e.g., SDP + GW rounding guarantees for MAXCUT) — i.e., quantum routines can be designed to preserve classical randomized-rounding performance ratios.
- [speculative] Under standard adiabatic reasoning, WS-QAOA converges to the global optimum as depth p → ∞ provided the initial relaxed coordinates are in (0,1) or a regularization ε>0 is used (the initial state then overlaps all computational basis states).
- [speculative] If the Unique Games Conjecture were false, there may exist (classical or) quantum algorithms that improve on GW guarantees; warm-start frameworks could in principle exploit such improvements.
- [supported] An implementation of WS-QAOA is available within Qiskit (the authors provide code/integration).
- [speculative] Warm-start approaches are especially promising on noisy near-term quantum hardware because they improve low-depth performance (therefore reducing gate-depth requirements) — supported by small-scale simulations but not proven on hardware.

**Results summary:** This preprint introduces 'warm-start' strategies for quantum combinatorial optimization (primarily QAOA and RQAOA) by initializing quantum variational states from solutions of continuous relaxations (QP, SDP) and from randomized-rounding outputs such as Goemans–Williamson (GW). Theoretical arguments show that quantum algorithms can be designed to preserve the approximation guarantees of classical relaxations; numerical simulations on portfolio optimization (n=6) and MAXCUT instances (n up to 30) demonstrate that warm-starting substantially improves low-depth performance vs standard QAOA/RQAOA — increasing probabilities of sampling optimum solutions, decreasing variational energy, reducing required anneal time in Trotterized simulations, and producing larger cuts when GW warm-starts are used. The authors provide a Qiskit implementation and discuss design choices (what/when/how to round), regularization to avoid reachability issues, and limits related to the Unique Games Conjecture.

**Performance claims:**
- [supported] In the 6-asset portfolio simulation, the probability of sampling the optimal binary solution from the optimized WS-QAOA trial state was reported as more than 5× higher than for standard QAOA for depths p=1..5.
- [supported] Goemans–Williamson (GW) rounding theoretical approximation constant: ≈0.878 (α ≈ 0.878).
- [supported] In the reported MAXCUT experiments, depth-one WS-QAOA with ε=0.25 achieved a median normalized cut energy ≈0.929 on the studied ensemble (exceeding the GW expected ratio ≈0.878).
## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** speculative

The paper does not claim a general, provable quantum advantage over the best classical polynomial-time algorithms. Instead it shows (theoretically) that quantum algorithms can be warm-started to inherit classical relaxation guarantees (e.g., GW for MAXCUT) and provides numerical evidence that warm-starting improves low-depth QAOA/RQAOA performance in small-to-moderate simulated instances. Thus, the work suggests a promising approach for near-term quantum heuristics but does not demonstrate an unconditional or hardware-level quantum advantage over classical methods.
## Limitations
- WS-QAOA and WS-RQAOA were evaluated primarily via numerical simulations on small problem sizes (examples: n=6 for portfolio, n=20 and n=30 for MAXCUT). Scalability to larger, practically relevant instances is not demonstrated (author-stated).
- Implementation on noisy, near-term quantum hardware is challenging because QAOA circuits (especially at larger p or for dense problems) can be deep and require many gates / SWAPs; current gate fidelities limit realizable depth (author-stated).
- Preparing and representing stronger relaxations (e.g., SDP solutions) directly on quantum hardware can require O(n^2) qubits or elaborate oracles, which is impractical in the near term (author-stated).
- Higher-order relaxations (e.g., higher levels of SOS/Moment hierarchies) provide stronger guarantees but their classical solution time grows super-polynomially with problem size and hierarchy order (author-stated).
- When coordinates of the continuous relaxation are exactly 0 or 1, the corresponding qubits in the warm-start may remain frozen under typical cost Hamiltonians (reachability problem), reducing the ability to reach the true discrete optimum (author-stated).
- Using randomized rounding in a classical pre-processing (e.g., GW) constrains the variational form: to preserve the rounded solution the mixer must be modified, but that modified mixer may break the theoretical adiabatic/convergence guarantees of standard QAOA (author-stated).
- The modified mixer that allows the variational form to recover a GW rounding (and also deviate from it) removes the property that the initial state is an eigenstate of the mixer, so convergence proofs analogous to Farhi et al. no longer apply (author-stated).
- The energy/optimization landscape for QAOA and WS-QAOA is non-convex with many local minima; classical optimizers (e.g., COBYLA) starting from random seeds may not find global optima, necessitating grid searches or many restarts (author-stated).
- Depth-one RQAOA admits efficient classical simulation; for p > 1 the same efficient simulation is not valid, limiting numerical exploration for deeper circuits (author-stated).
- The performance gains of WS-QAOA depend on the overlap between the continuous relaxation solution and the true discrete solution (d^T c / B); when overlap is low warm-starting may be less helpful (observed / author-stated).
- The paper's numerical studies often rely on classical pre-solvers (e.g., GW, CPLEX) and limited numbers of randomized-rounding samples (N, M). Performance observed might be sensitive to these choices; how to set them for larger instances is not resolved (author-stated).
- [inferred] Noise, sampling (shot) statistics, and other experimental hardware imperfections were not modelled in the simulations; the robustness of WS-QAOA to realistic device noise remains an open practical limitation.
- [inferred] The need for grid search or careful seeding for parameter optimization at low depth suggests the approach may have high classical overhead in tuning parameters for larger problems.
- [inferred] Quantum SDP solvers and oracles that would prepare/encode SDP solutions efficiently are not yet practical; reliance on classical SDP solvers or quantum-inspired classical algorithms may reduce any quantum advantage.
- [inferred] The trade-off parameter (regularization epsilon) needs proper tuning: too small or too large values can reduce performance, and the optimal choice may be instance-dependent and nontrivial to pick automatically.

- #limitation:simulation-only — All reported experiments are classical simulations (Qiskit statevector and efficient classical simulation for p=1 RQAOA); no results from QPUs are provided.
- #limitation:no-empirical-validation — Findings are not validated on real quantum hardware; noise effects and hardware constraints are not empirically assessed.

- #idea:hybrid-approach — The warm-start mixer H_ws construction (and RY rotations derived from relaxation solution) is an efficient state-preparation technique that interpolates between warm-start and standard X-mixer via ε-regularization.

## Contradictions
- contradiction:classical-vs-quantum — The paper itself notes that at constant depth QAOA cannot, in general, outperform classical Goemans–Williamson randomized rounding for certain MAXCUT instances, tempering claims of quantum superiority and framing any advantage as speculative or instance-specific.
- contradiction:scalability — The authors point out that naively representing full SDP solutions would require Θ(n^2) qubits (and implementing the necessary oracles is unclear), contradicting any expectation that the warm-start approach as presented readily scales to practical, large financial instances on near-term hardware.
