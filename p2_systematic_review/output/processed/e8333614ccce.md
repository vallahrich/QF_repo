## Abstract summary
Presents CCA3-SGAD, a quantum algorithm for Weighted Sparse Anomalous Subgraph Detection (wsasd) on sparse graphs, proving wsasd is W[1]-hard and deriving a practical crossover condition p > (Coracle/M')^{2/3} that determines when heuristic-biased quantum search outperforms heuristic-assisted classical search. The approach combines ε-complete constraint compression, a PQC-based oracle for learned scoring, biased amplitude amplification, and an adaptive quantum–Bayesian loop, and is supported by resource estimates, statevector simulations, IEEE 118-bus validation, and small-scale hardware experiments.
## Methodology
The authors design and evaluate CCA3-SGAD, a four-stage hybrid classical-quantum pipeline for Weighted Sparse Anomalous Subgraph Detection (wsasd). The pipeline consists of: (A) ε-complete constraint compression that deterministically prunes candidate subgraphs using necessary structural invariants; (B) offline training of a classical ML scoring function on sampled subgraphs and compilation of the learned scoring function into a parameterized quantum circuit (PQC) oracle UPQC(θ*) that maps candidate indices to approximate heuristic weights in superposition; (C) biased amplitude amplification (a Grover-like routine reflecting about the PQC-prepared non-uniform initial state) to amplify target amplitudes with O(1/√p) oracle complexity; and (D) an adaptive quantum–Bayesian refinement loop that updates the classical prior from measurement outcomes and repeats amplification until convergence. Methodology validation comprises: classical timing benchmarks on topology-calibrated synthetic graphs (Barabási–Albert and Watts–Strogatz) to measure per-candidate verification cost and empirical prior p; NumPy statevector simulations (small N, planted anomalies) to confirm O(1/√p) scaling and adaptive-loop convergence; a dedicated crossover-condition validation varying candidate-space size M'; real-data validation on the IEEE 118-bus test case to measure empirical p; and hardware experiments on IBM Heron r2 (ibm_fez) executing 3-qubit biased versus uniform Grover circuits under noisy conditions to validate the biased amplification primitive. Resource estimation and adversarial stress testing (oracle cost, routing overhead, precision, extra rounds) are included to project fault-tolerant requirements.

**Algorithms used:** Biased Amplitude Amplification (heuristic-biased Grover), Grover's Search (uniform baseline), Parameterized Quantum Circuit (PQC) oracle for scoring-function evaluation, ε-complete Constraint Compression (deterministic pruning), Adaptive Quantum–Bayesian Refinement Loop, Statevector simulation (NumPy)
**Frameworks:** NumPy (statevector simulation), SABRE (qubit routing/mapping algorithm; used for layout selection)

**Experimental setup:** Statevector simulation: full NumPy statevector simulation on small graphs (e.g., N=30, k=3, planted 3-clique). Classical benchmarking: topology-accurate synthetic graphs (BA, WS) calibrated to infrastructure stats; per-candidate verification timing measured (~47 μs). Real-data: IEEE 118-bus (MATPOWER case118) used for empirical heuristic prior via 3,000 sampled k=3 subgraphs. Hardware: IBM Heron r2 (ibm_fez) 3-qubit biased and uniform Grover circuits; transpiled (opt level=3), SABRE-selected layouts, circuit depths 26–28, two-qubit gate counts 17–19 (CZ), shots=8192; noisy simulation with depolarizing error model (1Q=0.05%, 2Q=1.0%, measurement=1.5%). Resource estimates for full pipeline: 364 logical qubits, PQC depth ~300, logical gate depth per circuit ≈3,000, total T-gates 63,700, circuit repetitions 50.

**Results summary:** This preprint presents CCA3-SGAD, a quantum algorithmic pipeline for detecting small anomalous subgraphs in sparse infrastructure-like graphs. The authors formalise the problem (wsasd) and provide a W[1]-hardness argument, derive an explicit crossover condition p > (C_oracle / M')^{2/3} for when heuristic-biased quantum amplitude amplification outperforms the best heuristic-assisted classical scan, and propose a PQC-based oracle construction with depth O(k log d). Evidence includes classical microbenchmarks (≈47 µs/candidate), statevector simulations confirming O(1/√p) scaling (Pearson r = 0.9861), a crossover-validation simulation, real-data validation on the IEEE 118-bus test case (p_emp ≈0.013), and small-scale superconducting hardware experiments validating the biased-amplification primitive (Δ ≈ +0.18). The authors provide resource estimates for a fault-tolerant implementation (364 logical qubits, ~63.7k T-gates) and argue the crossover condition and advantage are robust under conservative stress tests and heuristic variation. The claimed operational implication is that quantum search could convert currently infeasible real‑time anomaly scans into feasible ones as infrastructure size grows.

## Limitations
- Does not demonstrate infrastructure-scale quantum advantage; hardware validation is limited to small (3-qubit) biased Grover primitives on NISQ hardware rather than the full 364-logical-qubit fault-tolerant pipeline (author-stated).
- Statevector and hardware simulations are performed at small problem sizes (e.g., N up to ~100 in sim, M' values much smaller than infrastructure scale), so large-scale end-to-end behaviour is projected rather than fully demonstrated (author-stated).
- Resource estimates and timelines assume early fault-tolerant hardware (5–8 years), specific surface-code overheads (50:1), target logical error rate (≤10−6/gate), and Coracle ≈300; results depend on those assumptions (author-stated).
- Theorem 6 and performance claims rely on several assumptions that may be instance-sensitive: (i) the heuristic ranks a target in the top-p fraction with high probability (1−δ); (ii) Coracle is instance-independent; (iii) p can be estimated within a factor of 2 by the adaptive loop (author-stated).
- PQC approximation bound depends on per-gate error assumptions (e.g., εg = 10−6) and chosen PQC depth (D ≈300); practical gate noise and compilation overheads could degrade approximation (author-stated).
- W[1]-hardness is inherited by reduction from subgraph isomorphism; while this motivates quantum approach, it also implies that classical improvements could still change practical trade-offs and that the problem remains intrinsically hard (author-stated).
- Hardware validation uses a noise model and specific qubit layouts; while experiments show robustness across a few layouts, noise heterogeneity at larger scale and routing/connectivity effects are not fully evaluated (author-stated).
