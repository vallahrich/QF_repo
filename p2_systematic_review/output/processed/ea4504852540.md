## Abstract summary
The paper introduces the Quantum Binomial Tree, a quantum algorithmic implementation of the classical binomial tree to efficiently load probability distributions for Quantum Monte Carlo option pricing. It details constructions for local volatility, Heston, SABR and general multidimensional SDE models, provides gate-level implementation and source code for time-dependent volatility examples, and presents numerical experiments and complexity/resource analysis showing exponential path generation with overall quadratic speed-up when combined with amplitude estimation.
## Methodology
The paper proposes a new quantum subroutine, the Quantum Binomial Tree (QBT), to load probability distributions for derivative pricing on gate-based quantum computers. The approach discretizes underlying SDEs (Euler scheme) and maps each time-step to controlled update operations on a quantum register that stores the log-price (fixed-point representation). Coin toss registers (Hadamard-created superposition) represent the branching of the binomial tree, and per-branch local parameters (e.g., volatility, drift) are computed in superposition (quantum arithmetic). Up/down log-moves U/D are computed per node and added conditionally to the log-price register using controlled additions implemented via a QFT-based adder (Draper-style). Payoff encoding uses controlled Ry rotations (after sign/testing) to map the payoff into the amplitude of an ancilla qubit; amplitudes would be extracted with amplitude-estimation techniques for quadratic speed-up (discussed), though the experiments reported use statevector simulation to read out probabilities. The paper details adaptations for local volatility, Heston, SABR, and multi-dimensional models, and presents a concrete Julia implementation (finQbit/QuantumCircuits.jl) and experiments on a one-dimensional time-dependent volatility model comparing first- and second-order Taylor approximations of exp(·) for mapping log-price to price, against classical Monte Carlo baselines.

**Algorithms used:** Quantum Binomial Tree (proposed), Quantum Monte Carlo (conceptual, amplitude estimation discussed), QFT-based quantum addition (Draper), Controlled Ry rotations for payoff encoding, Euler discretization (classical SDE discretization)
**Frameworks:** finQbit/QuantumCircuits.jl (Julia), Qiskit (statevector simulator)

**Experimental setup:** Experiments run on simulators (Qiskit statevector and the QuantumCircuits.jl framework). Simulator limited to 32 qubits so the evaluation uses a one-dimensional SDE model. Two variants tested: first-order and second-order Taylor approximation of exp(x). Classical Monte Carlo baselines: (i) 100,000,000 replications with N=30 time steps (reference) and (ii) classical MC with N=5 to separate discretization error.

**Dataset:** No external market dataset. All inputs are synthetically generated from the specified stochastic model (one-dimensional SDE with time-dependent volatility).

## Findings
- [speculative] Proposes a novel algorithm called Quantum Binomial Tree (QBT) to load probability distributions for derivative pricing on gate-based quantum computers.
- [speculative] QBT can, in principle, generate an exponentially large number of Monte Carlo paths (n = 2^N) on a quantum computer while requiring only a polynomial number of gates in the problem parameters.
- [speculative] QBT can be adapted to load a variety of market models: local volatility, Heston, SABR, and (in principle) arbitrary multidimensional systems of stochastic differential equations.
- [supported] A proof-of-concept implementation and source code were provided and executed on a statevector quantum simulator for a one-dimensional, time-dependent-volatility model.
- [supported] Numerical simulation results (statevector simulator) for N=5 time steps and fractional precision P=6 were reported: with a first-order Taylor approximation of exp errors vs classical MC (N=30 reference) were roughly 7–15%; using second-order Taylor approximation errors reduced to roughly 3–10% (depending on strike).
- [speculative] The per-oracle (per-amplitude-preparation A) gate complexity is analyzed and reported as O(P^3 + P·N) (where P = fractional precision, N = number of time steps); controlled-additions via QFT and multiplication dominate costs.
- [speculative] By adding one 'coin' qubit and a constant number of CX gates the number of sampled paths doubles, enabling exponential growth in paths by linear/quadratic growth in gates/qubits.
- [speculative] The paper asserts that, when combined with quantum amplitude estimation, the overall option-pricing workflow retains the usual quadratic speed-up over classical Monte Carlo.
- [disputed] The manuscript at times suggests near-term execution on '14–28 qubit' machines / availability in the next few years; however its own resource estimates (hundreds to thousands of qubits and large CX counts) and the admitted need for fault-tolerance contradict feasibility on small NISQ devices.
- [supported] The author explicitly acknowledges current NISQ devices are inadequate for full-scale runs and that fault-tolerant quantum hardware will be required for the larger instances.

**Results summary:** The paper introduces the Quantum Binomial Tree (QBT), a gate-level method to prepare ensembles of asset-price paths on a quantum computer by encoding coin-toss sequences and performing controlled arithmetic (additions and limited multiplications) to build log-price paths. The method is presented for one-dimensional and extended in principle to Heston/SABR and multidimensional models. A proof-of-concept implementation ran on a statevector simulator (limited to 32 qubits) for a one-dimensional, time-dependent volatility example (N=5, P=6). Numerical comparisons versus classical Monte Carlo (reference: 100 million samples, N=30) show modest absolute errors (first-order exp: ~7–15% error vs high-precision MC; second-order exp reduces errors to ~3–10%). A theoretical complexity analysis yields per-oracle cost O(P^3 + P·N), and the author argues QBT permits exponential path count growth with polynomial resource scaling and therefore can be used as the state-preparation subroutine within quantum amplitude estimation to achieve the standard quadratic speed-up—though full-scale runs would require fault-tolerant hardware.

**Performance claims:**
- Proof-of-concept statevector simulation: N = 5 time steps, fractional precision P = 6, executed on simulator (max 32 qubits available).
- Classical reference Monte Carlo: 100,000,000 replications with N = 30 used as high-precision baseline.
- Reported pricing errors (first-order exp approximation) vs classical MC (N=30): example strike-dependent relative errors in Tables: roughly 7%–15%.
- Reported pricing errors (second-order exp approximation) vs classical MC (N=30): roughly 3%–10% depending on strike.
- Reported resource numbers (as given in paper Table 1): first-order method requires 330 qubits; second-order method requires 5,850 qubits (table also lists small CX counts that appear inconsistent).
- Analytic per-oracle gate complexity: O(P^3 + P·N) (P = fractional precision, N = number of time steps).
- Example resource table (paper Table 4, CP=24): for N=10 (n=1024 paths) reported CX count ~191,720 and qubit count ~87; CX counts scale rapidly with N in that table.
## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** theoretical

The paper claims the usual quadratic Monte Carlo speed-up (via quantum amplitude estimation) is preserved when using QBT for state preparation. This is a theoretical claim: amplitude estimation is known to provide quadratic speed-up, but the end-to-end advantage depends on the cost of state preparation. The author provides a theoretical gate-count analysis arguing polynomial-cost state preparation for exponentially many paths; however no hardware demonstration of a complete end-to-end quantum advantage is shown, and resource estimates indicate the full workflow will require fault-tolerant devices, so the advantage remains theoretical/speculative at this stage.

## Contradictions
- contradiction:classical-vs-quantum — The paper claims a quadratic speed-up when combined with amplitude estimation, but does not implement amplitude estimation or run on hardware; empirical results are limited to statevector simulation of N=5 (32 paths) and show non-negligible pricing errors (≈3.7%–14.7%) versus classical MC, so claimed advantage is not empirically demonstrated.
- contradiction:scalability — While the QBT construction asserts exponential path generation and presents asymptotic resource analysis, experiments are constrained by a 32-qubit simulator and small N; the paper's experimental scale does not validate scalability to realistic problem sizes (many time-steps, higher precision) where qubit/gate costs and noise would dominate.
- contradiction:classical-vs-quantum — There is an internal inconsistency in reported parameters (S0 reported as 1.0 in some places and 17 in others), indicating weaknesses in experimental reporting and making direct comparison to classical baselines less clear.
