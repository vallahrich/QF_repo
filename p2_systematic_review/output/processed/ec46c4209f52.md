## Abstract summary
This empirical study investigates hyperparameter and optimizer tuning of quantum generative adversarial networks (qGANs) to load multivariate classical distributions into quantum registers under NISQ constraints. The authors find Adam (AMSGRAD) yields substantially better approximation accuracy than SPSA, propose a discriminator design that treats univariate and multivariate cases uniformly, highlight sensitivity to random initialization with a clear gap between optimal and sub-optimal runs, and report average reductions of 43–64% in the Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic compared to prior work.
## Methodology
This empirical study evaluates and optimizes quantum generative adversarial networks (qGANs) for approximate loading of multivariate classical distributions into quantum registers. The generator is a layered parametric quantum circuit (ansatz) composed of RY single-qubit rotations and circular cZ entanglement layers; the number of repetitions k controls depth and the parameter count is (k+1)*n for n qubits. The discriminator is a classical feed-forward neural network with two hidden layers (H1, H2), Leaky ReLU activations and a sigmoid output; the input encoding uses one input node per qubit (leading to an input layer of size 2n in the authors' scheme). Training is hybrid quantum-classical and performed epoch-by-epoch on a noiseless quantum simulator (Qiskit). In each epoch: batches of true samples are drawn from the target distribution, the generator circuit (with current parameters) is sampled multiple times (shots) to produce fake samples, the discriminator is trained to distinguish true vs fake, and the generator is trained to fool the discriminator using feedback labels. Both Adam (AMSGRAD variant, implemented via PyTorch) and SPSA (Qiskit) optimizers are evaluated; the non-saturating GAN loss is used. Extensive hyper-parameter sweeps were run (learning rates, betas, shots, discriminator size, number of repetitions k) primarily for n = 3 and n = 4 qubits; each test case was repeated 10 times with different random initializations. Convergence of a run is formally detected by checking parameter stability (linear regression slope over a window m = M/200 epochs bounded by a threshold related to the learning rate). Final quality is measured via a χ2 two-sample goodness-of-fit test (p-value), with additional reporting of Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic and relative entropy (KL) for comparison to prior work. Final evaluations used the statevector simulator to remove sampling noise when computing metrics. The training set is synthetically drawn from a univariate lognormal distribution (µ=1, σ=1), discretized into 2^n bins and trimmed; baseline experiments use batch size 2000 and training-set size 20,000 (≈17,200 after binning). Results are compared to prior benchmarks (Zoufal et al. 2019) using KS and KL metrics.

**Algorithms used:** Quantum Generative Adversarial Network (qGAN), Layered parameterized ansatz with RY rotations and cZ entanglement, Adam (AMSGRAD variant), Simultaneous Perturbation Stochastic Approximation (SPSA)
**Frameworks:** Qiskit, PyTorch, SciPy

**Experimental setup:** Training performed on Qiskit noiseless state simulators (noisy simulator tested but not used for main experiments); final evaluations used the statevector simulator. Experiments run locally/in cloud via IBM Quantum Researchers Program access. Hybrid quantum-classical training: quantum circuit evaluations (shots per evaluation) and classical NN training (discriminator) using PyTorch. Multiple hyper-parameter sweeps and repeated random initializations (10 runs per test case).

**Dataset:** Synthetic univariate lognormal distribution (µ = 1, σ = 1), discretized in 2^n bins on interval [0, 8]. Training set of 20,000 samples (tails trimmed after binning yielding ≈17,200 usable samples). No real financial market data used.

## Findings
- [supported] Extensive hyper-parameter tuning (optimizer, learning rates, discriminator size, shots) yields substantially better qGAN training outcomes (measured in χ2 p-values, KS statistic, and relative entropy) on the studied 3-qubit log-normal benchmark than prior published results.
- [supported] The Adam AMSGRAD optimizer produces much higher-quality trained generators than SPSA in the tested setups: Adam achieves high p-values (>0.9 in best runs) while SPSA typically produces low p-values (often <0.5 or effectively zero when applied to the discriminator).
- [supported] There is a clear gap between ‘optimal’ runs and ‘sub-optimal’ runs under the same hyper-parameters: best runs concentrate around high χ2 p-values (~0.9) and are well separated from the bulk of runs (p-values < ~0.6).
- [supported] The probability of obtaining an optimal run from random initialization is limited (roughly 10–30% for n=3 in their experiments) and decreases as the number of qubits increases; therefore multiple independent restarts are important.
- [supported] Increasing the number of qubits (from 3 to 4 in their experiments) notably degrades the approximation quality (χ2 p-values drop substantially), i.e., training accuracy decreases with n in their tests.
- [supported] The choice of discriminator input encoding (using each qubit as an input node rather than bucket labels) makes training univariate and multivariate distributions equivalent in their framework, at the expense of larger discriminator inputs and training time.
- [supported] Recommended hyper-parameters for the studied 3-qubit, k=1 log-normal case: Adam AMSGRAD, discriminator learning rate ≈ 1e-3, generator learning rate between 5e-4 and 1e-3, betas (β1,β2)=(0.7,0.99), discriminator hidden-layer sizes ≥8, and shots s≈2000.
- [supported] Using their tuning and discriminator design, for a 3-qubit log-normal target with k=1 repetitions they report mean KS and relative-entropy metrics substantially better than the cited prior work (e.g., mean KS reduced from 0.0821 to ~0.0296 under comparable conditions).
- [supported] SPSA can reduce quantum evaluation counts (lower quantum cost per epoch) compared to gradient-based methods but achieved much worse final approximation quality in their experiments.
- [supported] The number of shots (s) affects variance but moderate values (s≈2000) provide good trade-offs between quantum cost and training quality for their setting.
- [speculative] An observed heuristic: the presence of an 'elbow' in parameter trajectories before convergence may indicate an optimal run, while slow/oscillatory behavior may signal sub-optimal solutions (authors present this as preliminary/qualitative).
- [speculative] Adam’s superior accuracy relative to SPSA implies a scaling limitation: Adam becomes more costly as input (ansatz parameter count) grows, so SPSA or other optimizers may still be necessary for larger n — this conclusion combines empirical small-n results with theoretical scaling considerations.
- [speculative] A trained qGAN ansatz can be interpreted as a lossy compression (parameter manifold approximating the target distribution); the expressivity and density of the ansatz manifold around the target control approximation quality.
- [speculative] qGANs are a promising approach for approximate data loading for downstream quantum algorithms (e.g., quantum amplitude estimation) in domains like finance, but concrete end-to-end quantum advantage is not demonstrated here.
- [speculative] The discriminator design and ansatz choices they used are expected to generalize beyond the log-normal target; the authors note no known special favorable link between the ansatz and the log-normal distribution but acknowledge further tests for generality are needed.
- [speculative] The authors note the need for many runs and the hyper-parameter choices to scale-validate qGANs; they explicitly say an asymptotic scaling study requires larger-n experiments not feasible at present.

**Results summary:** This empirical study systematically tuned and evaluated quantum generative adversarial networks (qGANs) for multivariate (and univariate-equivalent) probability loading on small instances (primarily 3 qubits, with some 4-qubit tests) using noiseless simulators. Key findings: Adam AMSGRAD substantially outperforms SPSA in final approximation quality at the tested scales; careful optimizer and discriminator hyper-parameter tuning (discriminator lr ≈ 1e-3, generator lr 5e-4–1e-3, betas (0.7,0.99), shots ≈2000, discriminator hidden layers ≥8) yielded markedly better KS and relative-entropy results than prior benchmarks (e.g., mean KS reduced from ~0.0821 to ~0.0296 in a 3-qubit log-normal k=1 case). The experiments reveal that initialization matters greatly (optimal runs are isolated and relatively rare), that more restarts are more effective than marginal hyper-parameter tweaks, and that increasing qubit count degrades approximation quality in the tested regime. SPSA reduces quantum evaluation cost per epoch but delivers poor final accuracy here. The authors caution that scaling behavior beyond the tested small n is not settled and requires further study.

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** not-applicable

The paper studies qGANs as a method for approximate data loading and focuses on optimizer/hyper-parameter tuning and resulting approximation quality on small simulated instances. It reports improved approximation metrics versus prior qGAN implementations but does not demonstrate or claim an end-to-end quantum computational advantage (no superpolynomial or practical runtime/accuracy advantage over classical methods is shown). Experiments are simulator-based and limited to small n, and the authors explicitly note scaling and advantage questions remain open.
## Limitations
- Tested problem sizes are small (mainly n = 3 qubits, some results for n = 4); findings may not generalize to larger numbers of qubits (author-stated).
- Training and evaluation were performed predominantly on noiseless simulators; behavior on noisy simulators and real quantum hardware was not extensively validated (author-stated).
- Study focused on memorization (ability to reproduce a given discrete distribution) rather than generalization; implications for generative performance on unseen data are not addressed (author-stated).
- The SPSA optimizer, while potentially cheaper in quantum evaluations, yielded substantially worse approximation accuracy in the tested settings — indicating a trade-off and an open scaling limitation (author-stated).
- Training outcomes strongly depend on random initialization: only a small fraction of runs reach the high-quality (near-optimal) solutions and this success rate decreases with number of qubits (author-stated).
- An asymptotic (scaling) analysis could not be completed because it would require assumptions on how k (repetitions), H1, H2 (discriminator sizes), and number of epochs j scale with n; those relations were not determined (author-stated).
- The discriminator input design used (one input node per qubit) increases discriminator input dimensionality and training cost as n grows; its scalability must be reconsidered (author-stated).
- Most experiments used a single target distribution family (log-normal) and a single ansatz design; sensitivity to different distribution shapes or different ansatz architectures is limited in the presented evaluation (author-stated).
- [inferred] Limited on-device experimentation: practical constraints (e.g., queue times, access delays) restricted running extensive experiments on real hardware, so real-device effects are underexplored.
- [inferred] Optimization landscape pathologies (e.g., barren plateaus or other high-dimensional training issues) for larger systems were not empirically characterized and remain a potential obstacle to scaling.
- [inferred] The data-compression interpretation assumes parameters can be stored at comparable numerical precision to probabilities; this assumption was stated but not validated.
- [inferred] The methodology intentionally does not enforce or verify discriminator parameter convergence; the potential impact of an under-converged discriminator on generator quality for larger problems is not fully explored.
- [inferred] Noise sensitivity and robustness (to realistic gate and measurement noise models) were not systematically assessed; resilience claims rely mainly on simulators and shallow ansatz observations.
## Open questions
- How do qGAN training cost and approximation accuracy scale with increasing number of qubits (n) for practical problem sizes?
- Can the Adam optimizer remain practical at larger input/parameter sizes given its cost scaling, or are new optimizers needed to retain quality at scale?
- Is it possible to design optimizers that match Adam's achieved accuracy but approach SPSA-like quantum evaluation scaling?
- What is the root cause of the observed strong separation (isolation) between runs that reach very high-quality solutions and the bulk of sub-optimal runs?
- Is the observed 'elbow' in parameter trajectories a reliable early predictor of an ultimately optimal run, and can it be used for early stopping or restart strategies?
- How can the probability of converging to the (near-)optimal manifold be increased — e.g., better initialization strategies or architectural choices?
- How should discriminator architecture and input encoding be adapted to preserve scalability and training efficiency for larger multivariate/ high-qubit problems?
- How sensitive are the results to different target distribution shapes and to different ansatz choices (entangling gates, rotation types, layering strategy)?
- How do qGANs perform on real noisy quantum devices compared to noiseless simulators — specifically, what are the effects of realistic noise on convergence and final approximation quality?
- What are reasonable and empirically validated assumptions for asymptotic studies (how do k, H1, H2, number of epochs j scale as functions of n)?
- Can gradient-free optimizers or hybrid strategies effectively mitigate barren-plateau-like problems while retaining acceptable approximation accuracy?
- What is the practical trade-off between number of shots s and convergence/accuracy for larger systems and in presence of noise?
