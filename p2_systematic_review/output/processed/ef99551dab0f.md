## Abstract summary
This public deliverable (D5.1) of the NEASQC project reviews the state-of-the-art for applying quantum computing to derivative pricing and computation of Value-at-Risk (VaR). It surveys classical methods (Monte Carlo, PDEs, PDE+ML), quantum alternatives—especially Quantum Amplitude Estimation and variants (including a quantum-coin approach), quantum PDE solvers and QML approaches—and discusses practical challenges such as state/data loading, circuit depth, and scalability to real-world financial problems on NISQ hardware.
## Methodology
This document is a state-of-the-art technical review and conceptual study of quantum computing approaches for derivative pricing and Value-at-Risk (VaR) computation. The authors survey classical methods (Monte Carlo, PDE/finite-difference, Fourier approaches, trinomial/binomial trees, Delta–Gamma approximations, factor models and PCA for credit risk) and then map these problems to quantum alternatives. The methodology consists of: (1) a literature review of relevant quantum algorithms and variants (amplitude amplification/estimation, Grover-based methods, QFT/QPE, variational/imaginary-time approaches, quantum machine-learning techniques, and quantum walks); (2) analytic descriptions and derivations of quantum circuits and operators needed to implement the target tasks (state preparation/loading, oracles for payoff and density, comparator circuits, Grover operator construction, amplitude estimation pipelines); (3) presentation and theoretical analysis of algorithmic variants proposed or synthesised by the authors (including a Quantum Coin (QCoin) style approach as a simpler alternative to standard QAE and discussion of ML-based and tensor-network-based state-loading alternatives); and (4) a qualitative resource and scaling analysis (discussing qubit counts, circuit depth, loading problem, NISQ restrictions, and requirements for error correction). The work is non-empirical: it focuses on theoretical exposition, algorithm pipelines, and feasibility/resource discussion rather than implementation or benchmarking on quantum hardware or simulators.

**Algorithms used:** Quantum Amplitude Estimation (QAE), Quantum Amplitude Amplification (QAA) / Grover's algorithm, Quantum Fourier Transform (QFT), Quantum Phase Estimation (QPE), Iterative Amplitude Estimation, Maximum Likelihood Amplitude Estimation, Power-law / QoPrime Amplitude Estimation, Quantum Coin (QCoin) / direct amplitude embedding, Grover-Rudolph state preparation, Linear Combination of Unitaries (LCU), Harrow-Hassidim-Lloyd (HHL) linear systems algorithm, Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE) / variational imaginary-time evolution, Quantum Principal Component Analysis (QPCA), Quantum Kernel Methods, Quantum Generative Adversarial Networks (QGAN), Quantum walks, Trotterisation / Hamiltonian simulation techniques, Multi-level Monte Carlo (quantum generalisation)

## Findings
- [supported] Current quantum hardware (NISQ devices) have limited qubit counts and substantial noise that constrain circuit depth and practical implementations.
- [speculative] Quantum Amplitude Estimation (QAE) offers a theoretical quadratic speed-up over classical Monte Carlo for expectation/Monte Carlo-type problems (classical O(1/sqrt(M)) vs quantum O(1/M) in precision scaling).
- [speculative] Variants of amplitude estimation that avoid the Quantum Fourier Transform (e.g. iterative QAE, maximum-likelihood QAE, power-law / QoPrime schedules) can reduce resource demands and are more NISQ-friendly.
- [speculative] The 'quantum coin' (direct embedding) approach (loading f(x) rather than sqrt(f(x))) is a viable alternative to QAE with similar asymptotic speed-up potential, and may simplify hardware-level implementation in some cases.
- [supported] The state-preparation/loading problem (efﬁciently loading classical probability distributions and payoff functions into quantum registers) is a major practical bottleneck for end-to-end quantum Monte Carlo workflows.
- [supported] QRAM and similar large-scale coherent data-access primitives are not expected to be practically available in the near term and represent an important obstacle to many QML/QCMC proposals.
- [speculative] Quantum approaches to solving PDEs (map Black–Scholes / option-PDEs to Hamiltonian evolution, imaginary-time propagation, variational imaginary-time methods) are promising research lines but have not yet shown clear end-to-end practical advantage for realistic finance workloads.
- [speculative] Quantum Machine Learning methods (variational circuits, QGANs, QPCA, quantum kernels) could assist tasks like state-preparation, dimensionality reduction and PDE/BSDE solution, but their advantage for real-world, high-dimensional financial problems is not established.
- [supported] Even when quantum subroutines have asymptotic speedups, overall advantage can be negated by state-loading costs, circuit compilation/overheads, and error-correction/resource overheads; several cited works quantify or argue this effect.
- [supported] Resource estimates for realistic, path-dependent derivative pricing indicate very large requirements (e.g., prior literature estimates on the order of thousands of logical qubits and tens of millions of T-gates), making near-term practical advantage unlikely without major advances.
- [speculative] Tensor-network inspired encodings and parameterized quantum circuits (PQC) are promising practical work-arounds for compact approximate state preparation, but scalability and accuracy trade-offs need more study.
- [supported] Classical variance-reduction techniques (importance sampling, multi-level Monte Carlo) remain relevant and quantum algorithms have been proposed to combine with multi-level strategies, but practical gains remain to be demonstrated.
- [supported] The community consensus in the report: quantum computing for derivative pricing and VaR is an active, promising research area, but real-world, demonstrated advantage has not yet been achieved.

**Results summary:** This technical review surveys classical and quantum methods for derivative pricing and risk (VaR/CVaR). It highlights Quantum Amplitude Estimation (QAE) and related Grover-based approaches as the main quantum alternative to classical Monte Carlo, promising a theoretical quadratic improvement in precision scaling. Several QAE variants (iterative, maximum-likelihood, power-law/QoPrime) and the quantum-coin (direct embedding) approach aim to reduce resource demands and make implementations more NISQ-compatible. The report also reviews quantum approaches to PDE-based pricing (Hamiltonian mappings and imaginary-time/variational propagation) and quantum machine-learning techniques relevant to finance (QPCA, QNNs, QGANs). However, it emphasizes major practical obstacles: the loading problem (efﬁciently preparing probability and payoff states), lack of QRAM, limited NISQ hardware (qubit counts, noise, depth), and large resource overheads (error correction, compilation). Estimates from the literature for realistic, path-dependent problems indicate requirements (thousands of logical qubits, extremely deep circuits) far beyond current devices. Overall, the advantage remains theoretical and speculative; end-to-end empirical demonstrations on realistic financial workloads are not yet available.

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** theoretical

The report concludes that quantum algorithms (notably QAE and its variants) provide a theoretical quadratic speed-up for Monte Carlo-like expectation estimation, but that this advantage remains unproven in end-to-end, realistic financial applications due to practical obstacles (state-preparation/loading, QRAM, limited NISQ hardware, and large resource/error-correction overheads). Thus the advantage is currently theoretical/speculative rather than empirically demonstrated.
## Limitations
- Currently available quantum hardware (NISQ) has a small number of qubits and high noise, limiting circuit depth and practical implementations
- Existing amplitude-estimation methods often require resource-heavy subroutines (e.g. inverse Quantum Fourier Transform) that are impractical on NISQ devices
- The 'loading problem': lack of efﬁcient, generally applicable methods to load classical data (probability distributions, empirical PDFs, payoff functions) into quantum registers
- Normalization and embedding subtleties (square-root vs direct embedding) complicate algorithm design and correctness (e.g. QAE vs QCoin)
- State preparation often dominates runtime of quantum Monte Carlo approaches; naive state preparation scales O(N) and is infeasible for large N
- Many algorithmic speedups are concentrated in subroutines; overall end-to-end quantum advantage can vanish once loading/preprocessing is accounted for
- Quantum algorithms for PDEs rely on restrictive assumptions (e.g. diagonalization in momentum space) that do not generalize to more realistic models (interest-rate or volatility-dependent dynamics)
- Imaginary-time / variational PDE approaches depend on ansatz choice and require solving parameter evolution (linear systems) with unclear scaling
- QML/QPCA methods often assume QRAM or multiple copies of density matrices, but QRAM is not currently realizable and classical preprocessing (e.g. building covariance matrix) may cost O(N^2) or O(N^3)
- Piecewise or complex payoff functions (path-dependent payoffs, many exercise boundaries) increase circuit complexity (many comparators, ancillas)
- Tail risk estimation (VaR/CVaR) requires many samples; even with quantum speedups, practical resource overheads (error correction, logical qubits) may negate gains
- Large constant overheads (error correction, gate counts, connectivity) mean quadratic asymptotic speedups may not produce practical advantage in realistic time/energy/cost budgets
- Resource estimates for realistic, path-dependent benchmarks remain extremely large (e.g. thousands of logical qubits, millions of T-gates, requiring clock-rates beyond current devices)
- Some state-preparation proposals (e.g., Grover–Rudolph) have disputed claims about practical speed-up and may not scale beyond tiny problem sizes
- Training-based / generative approaches (PQC, QGAN) require costly training and model-specific tuning; scalability and stability of training are unresolved
- [inferred] Error accumulation and decoherence during multi-step simulations (e.g. keeping superpositions across many time steps for path-dependent options) present a practical fragility not yet solved
- [inferred] Lack of standardized benchmarks and end-to-end resource accounting (including classical preprocessing, energy and wall-clock time) makes cross-comparisons and claims of advantage unreliable
## Open questions
- How can we design generally efficient (poly-time in n) state-preparation circuits to load arbitrary or empirical probability distributions into n-qubit registers?
- Can payoff functions with complex structure and high dimensionality be represented compactly as small-depth unitary circuits, and if so how?
- To what extent can QAE/QCoin and the various QAE variants (iterative, ML-based, Power-law, QoPrime) deliver quadratic speedups in practical NISQ or early fault-tolerant regimes?
- Is QRAM physically realizable in realistic hardware, or are QRAM-free algorithms needed for practical quantum-accelerated finance?
- Can we perform the entire Monte Carlo sampling (scenario generation) coherently on a quantum device (i.e., keep path superpositions and apply payoff) with resource cost lower than the classical alternative?
- Do quantum PDE solvers provide end-to-end advantage beyond stylized models (e.g., beyond Black–Scholes) when loading and measurement overheads are included?
- What are suitable ansatzes and training strategies for variational/imaginary-time methods that scale to finance-sized PDEs and maintain robustness to noise?
- How can quantum algorithms be adapted to accurately estimate tail measures (VaR, CVaR) where rare-event sampling is critical? Can importance-sampling analogues be formulated quantumly effectively?
- Can tensor network approaches or other compressed classical/quantum representations be systematically used to reduce state-preparation cost for empirical distributions?
- What is the practical trade-off between circuit depth and achievable speedup (ND trade-off), and where does the sweet spot lie for real financial workloads?
- How large are the non-asymptotic constant factors (error-correction overheads, gate synthesis costs) that affect claims of practical advantage, and can they be reduced?
- Are hybrid classical-quantum machine learning workflows for pricing and risk robust and advantageous under realistic noise and dataset shifts?
- How does decoherence/controlled dissipation (or intentional decoherence) affect quantum-walk-based models of asset dynamics, and could that be exploited or mitigated?
- [inferred] What are rigorous, hardware-aware benchmarks (including energy, latency, classical preprocessing) that should be used to evaluate quantum advantage in finance?

**Future work:**
- Develop theoretical and practical methods to efﬁciently load probability distributions and payoff functions into quantum registers (the 'loading problem')
- Design and benchmark QAE alternatives suitable for NISQ devices (e.g. QCoin, iterative/ML-based amplitude estimation, Power-law / QoPrime schedules) avoiding heavy QFTs
- Investigate tensor-network and PQC-based state preparation approaches and assess their scalability for empirical finance data
- Extend quantum PDE-solving techniques to more realistic models (stochastic volatility, interest-rate dependent dynamics) and study whether advantage persists
- Explore variational imaginary-time propagation and related hybrid algorithms for pricing PDEs, including better ansatzes and quantum solvers for the parameter evolution linear systems
- Develop quantum-native Monte Carlo / path-generation schemes (including trinomial/binnomial trees and quantum walks) that keep whole-path superpositions and integrate payoffs coherently
- Research QRAM alternatives or QRAM-free architectures for quantum machine learning and QPCA applicable to financial datasets
- Quantify end-to-end resource requirements (qubits, gate-depth, T-count, error correction overhead) for realistic, path-dependent derivative pricing and risk workloads
- Study quantum methods for tail-risk estimation (VaR, CVaR), including quantum versions of importance sampling and multi-level Monte Carlo
- Investigate robustness and potential quantum advantage of quantum machine learning (projected quantum kernels, QNN, QGAN) on noisy hardware for finance tasks
- Produce realistic, hardware-aware benchmarks and cost/energy analyses to determine when/if quadratic (or other) quantum speedups translate into practical advantage
- Address open concerns and validate state-preparation schemes (e.g. Grover–Rudolph) and disputed claims with rigorous resource accounting and empirical tests
