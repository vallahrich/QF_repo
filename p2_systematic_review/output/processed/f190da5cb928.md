## Abstract summary
The paper introduces Hamming Weight Operators, a class of constraint-aware mixers that enforce linear constraints by confining quantum evolution to the feasible subspace, avoiding penalty terms. Building on this, the authors propose Adaptive Hamming Weight Operator QAOA (AHWO-QAOA), which adaptively selects effective operators to construct shallow, problem-tailored circuits; numerical tests on portfolio optimization and two-jet clustering show guaranteed feasibility, faster convergence, higher approximation ratios, and substantially reduced gate counts compared with penalty-based QAOA.
## Methodology
The paper introduces a constraint-aware variational quantum optimization method (AHWO-QAOA) that embeds linear constraints directly into the QAOA mixer using novel Hamming Weight Operators (HWOs). Binary decision variables xi are mapped to qubits via xi -> (1 - σz)/2; the cost function is encoded as a cost Hamiltonian Hc and the linear constraint as Hs. Instead of penalty terms, the authors enumerate Hamming Weight Equations induced by the linear constraint and construct swap-like Hamming Weight Operators that exchange amplitudes only between feasible basis states, annihilating infeasible ones. An operator pool P of candidate HWOs is built (a sparse, connectivity-preserving O(n) subset obtained via a prefix-based search with worst-case O(n^2) complexity). AHWO-QAOA adaptively grows the mixer: starting from an initial state |+>^⊗n and an initial mixer, at each iteration the algorithm evaluates candidate operators by their effect on the expected cost, selects the operator that most reduces the cost, adds it to the mixer, and re-optimizes variational angles using a full-parameter (multi-angle) QAOA ansatz. The variational parameters γ and β are initialized uniformly in (−0.1, 0.1). The approach guarantees feasibility by construction (states remain in the feasible subspace), and is compared in simulation to penalty-based QAOA (λ = 10 and 100) and DC-QAOA baselines. Performance metrics include Constraint Ratio, Approximation Ratio (1 - |⟨Hc⟩ - E0|/|E0|, set to zero if constraints violated), gate counts, and convergence iteration counts. Experiments are numerical simulations on randomly generated problem instances (portfolio optimization and two-jet clustering) for system sizes n ∈ {6, 8, 10, 12, 16, 20} with 100 instances per size, and comparisons consider different layer counts (AHWO-QAOA often with 1 layer versus penalty QAOA with up to 5 layers).

**Algorithms used:** AHWO-QAOA (Adaptive Hamming Weight Operator QAOA), QAOA (Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm), Multi-Angle / Full-Parameter QAOA, ADAPT-VQE (inspirational adaptive ansatz construction), Penalty-based QAOA (baseline), DC-QAOA (baseline)

**Experimental setup:** Numerical (classical) simulations of variational quantum circuits run for synthetic constrained combinatorial problems. System sizes used: n ∈ {6, 8, 10, 12, 16, 20} qubits. For each size, 100 randomly generated problem instances were tested. Variational parameters were initialized uniformly at random in (-0.1, 0.1). Comparisons included AHWO-QAOA (typically 1 ansatz layer) versus penalty-based QAOA and DC-QAOA (up to 5 layers). No specific quantum hardware or simulator name is reported.

**Dataset:** Synthetic randomly generated instances for portfolio optimization (random µij, ηk, ωi, and linear constraints) and for two-jet clustering (random µij encoding angular separations and particle energies ϵi). No real-world financial datasets were used.

**Results summary:** The paper proposes Hamming Weight Operators as constraint-preserving mixer elements and develops an adaptive QAOA variant (AHWO-QAOA) that incrementally selects effective operators from a pool to build a shallow, constraint-aware ansatz. Through numerical simulations on two benchmark problem families (portfolio optimization and two-jet clustering with energy balance) for up to 20 qubits and 100 random instances per size, AHWO-QAOA consistently satisfied all linear equality constraints, converged faster (e.g., ~30 iterations vs ~90 or ~410 for penalty-based baselines on a representative 12-qubit instance), achieved higher Approximation Ratios, and used substantially fewer gates (reported ~470 vs ~1270 gates at 20 qubits) than penalty-based QAOA with multiple layers. The authors argue the method reduces reliance on penalty tuning and provides a modular, hardware-efficient approach to constrained combinatorial problems, while noting further extensions (inequalities, hardware tests) as future work.

**Performance claims:**
- AHWO-QAOA satisfied the linear constraints in 100% of tested instances (Constraint Ratio = 1.0) across problem sizes up to 20 qubits (100 random instances per size).
- Convergence on a representative 12-qubit instance: AHWO-QAOA ~30 iterations on average; penalty-based QAOA with 5 layers and λ=10 ~90 iterations; penalty-based QAOA with λ=100 ~410 iterations (means over 100 random initializations).
- Quantum gate counts reported at 20 qubits: AHWO-QAOA ≈ 470 total gates (1 ansatz layer) vs penalty-based QAOA ≈ 1270 total gates (5 ansatz layers) — AHWO-QAOA uses roughly half as many gates in this example.

- Simulations were averaged over 100 randomly generated problem instances for each system size in portfolio optimization experiments.
## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** speculative

The paper demonstrates empirical improvements of AHWO-QAOA over penalty-based QAOA variants on small-to-moderate simulated instances (up to 20 qubits) in terms of constraint satisfaction, convergence, approximation ratio, and gate count. However, it does not claim or provide evidence of a provable or demonstrated quantum advantage over the best classical algorithms or heuristics; statements about scalability to practical quantum advantage on NISQ hardware are presented as prospective and theoretical.
## Limitations
- Numerical validation is limited to classical simulations of problem instances up to 20 qubits (no experimental hardware demonstrations).
- Full enumeration of all induced Hamming Weight Equations quickly becomes intractable for large n, requiring the paper to resort to a sparse, connectivity-preserving subset; the authors note worst-case complexity O(n^2) for their prefix-based search construction (Appendix A).
