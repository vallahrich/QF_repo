## Abstract summary
This paper explores hybrid classical-quantum machine learning models for finance, implementing a quantum GAN on real cryptocurrency data, a hybrid quantum-classical QLSTM for NVIDIA stock time-series prediction compared to a classical LSTM, and a Quantum Circuit Born Machine (QCBM) on a synthetic toy dataset. The experiments show qGAN and QCBM are promising for generative and optimization tasks in finance, and that QLSTM produced slightly better test predictions than classical LSTM despite higher training cost and GPU dependence.
## Methodology
The authors implemented and compared three hybrid classical-quantum generative/time-series approaches applied to financial data: a quantum-classical qGAN for modeling asset return distributions and downstream QUBO optimization for asset selection; a hybrid QLSTM (replacing LSTM cell outputs with variational quantum circuits (VQCs)) versus a classical LSTM for NVIDIA stock-price forecasting; and a Quantum Circuit Born Machine (QCBM) trained on a synthetic Bars-and-Stripes (BAS) toy dataset. For qGAN they collected real cryptocurrency time-series from Binance, preprocessed by trimming values outside the 5th–95th percentiles and discretizing into 32 bins, then trained a variational quantum generator (1-qubit true state / variational layers) against a classical neural-network discriminator using an Adam optimizer (generator/discriminator losses tracked). After qGAN training they formulated a QUBO from the learned distribution and solved it (pyqubo + CLARABEL/cvxpy) to pick the optimal crypto. For QLSTM they encoded classical input vectors into quantum states via tensor-product encoding (single-qubit rotations), used VQCs (Hadamard + rotational encoding layer, variational layer, measurement) inside LSTM-like cells (6 VQC blocks with 4 qubits each) and trained the hybrid network (VQCs trained for 50 epochs with Adam) and compared training/test loss and RMSE to a PyTorch LSTM baseline (hidden size 7). For QCBM they built a layered ansatz (5 layers) on 4 qubits for the 2x2 BAS dataset, compared optimizers (COBYLA, SPSA, Nelder-Mead), and selected SPSA as the best-performing optimizer based on KL-like cost. Simulations and experiments used Qiskit Aer and PennyLane simulators with GPU acceleration (cuQuantum / CUDA-Q via NVIDIA GPUs) and Lightning.gpu to accelerate training.

**Algorithms used:** qGAN, QCBM, QLSTM, LSTM, Variational Quantum Circuit (VQC), QUBO, Born machine, VQA
**Frameworks:** Qiskit, PennyLane, PyTorch, pyqubo, cvxpy (CLARABEL solver), Lightning.gpu, cuQuantum / CUDA-Q

**Experimental setup:** Simulations run primarily on local GPUs: NVIDIA RTX 3070 (primary), RTX 3060 and Apple 30-core M3 GPU. Quantum circuit simulation used Qiskit Aer and PennyLane; GPU-accelerated simulation used NVIDIA's cuQuantum / CUDA-Q and PennyLane Lightning.gpu. LSTM training was run on an M3 MacBook and GPUs (RTX series) under Linux Ubuntu 22.04 for other experiments.

**Dataset:** (1) Binance cryptocurrency pairs: BNBBTC, ETHBTC, LTCBTC, NEOBTC, QTUMETH (>5,000 samples total) collected via Binance API; (2) NVIDIA historical stock price time series for 210 days (up to 2024-08-28); (3) Synthetic Bars-and-Stripes (BAS) 2×2 toy dataset for QCBM.

**Results summary:** The paper presents proof-of-concept implementations of three hybrid quantum-classical generative/time-series methods for finance: a qGAN trained on multivariate cryptocurrency data (with subsequent QUBO-based asset selection), a QLSTM applied to NVIDIA stock-price time series compared against a classical LSTM, and a QCBM trained on a synthetic BAS toy dataset. Empirical results are obtained via simulator experiments accelerated on GPUs (including CUDA-Q/cuQuantum). Key empirical outcomes include successful qGAN training and histogram matching, QUBO selection of BNBBTC in the studied timeframe, QLSTM producing test predictions reportedly closer to real stock movements at volatile peaks despite much higher simulation training cost, and QCBM with SPSA approximating the target BAS distribution. The authors emphasise GPU dependence of QLSTM simulation performance and frame broader claims of quantum advantage as prospective rather than demonstrated.

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** speculative

The paper repeatedly frames quantum advantage as a potential/future outcome (e.g., qGANs and QCBMs 'promise' advantage on NISQ devices). The work presents simulator-based evidence of promising behavior (better test prediction fidelity in a specific QLSTM run, QCBM approximation on a toy dataset) but does not demonstrate a clear, reproducible quantum advantage on quantum hardware or via controlled classical baselines; comparative claims (e.g., fewer iterations than classical GAN) lack direct empirical classical baselines in this paper, so any advantage remains speculative.
## Limitations
- Practical, large-scale deployment on real quantum hardware is not demonstrated; most experiments were run on simulators (Qiskit Aer, PennyLane) and GPU-accelerated simulators (CUDA-Q).
- QLSTM training is significantly slower and computationally more expensive than classical LSTM (training-time and GPU-dependence explicitly reported).
- qGAN and QCBM experiments are small-scale: qGAN used heavy discretization (32 discrete values) and a 1-qubit generator state in parts of the implementation; QCBM evaluated only on a toy BAS (2×2) dataset.
- The study uses relatively small/short real-world time-series for QLSTM/LSTM (210 days of NVIDIA stock data), limiting conclusions about generalizability to longer horizons or other assets.
- Optimization and solver choices rely on classical optimizers/solvers (Adam, SPSA, pyqubo + CLARABEL) rather than quantum optimization hardware; QUBO was solved classically.
- Reported performance gains are modest/mixed: while QLSTM test predictions are described as slightly superior, classical LSTM trains more efficiently and 'LSTM performs marginally better for the 50 epochs' in some metrics—implying limited, non-robust improvements.
- Statistical analysis is limited: only a paired t-test on RMSE is mentioned without extensive cross-validation, multiple seeds, or broader statistical robustness checks.
- Noise, decoherence and other NISQ hardware effects are not evaluated because experiments were performed on simulators; thus claims about NISQ-device advantage remain speculative.
- The work uses specific encoding schemes (tensor-product / basis encoding) and a particular VQC ansatz; the sensitivity of results to encoding/ansatz choices is not thoroughly explored.
- Hyperparameter tuning and exploration (number of layers, qubits, circuit depth, batch sizes, epoch counts) appear limited, restricting understanding of scalability and optimal configurations.
- [inferred] Limited dimensionality: multivariate financial distributions (beyond the 5-crypto example and small discretization) and high-dimensional time-series were not fully explored, limiting applicability to realistic, high-dimensional finance problems.
- [inferred] Dependence on powerful classical hardware (high-end GPUs and cuQuantum) to simulate quantum circuits may obscure whether observed benefits would persist on actual quantum devices.
- [inferred] Potential overfitting risk given small datasets and low sample counts (e.g., 210-day stock series) and limited reporting on regularization or validation strategies.
- [inferred] The qGAN discriminator is classical; full quantum discriminator/generator combinations and their comparative advantages are not explored.
- [inferred] Integration with production-grade financial workflows (risk constraints, regulatory requirements, transaction costs, latency) is not addressed.
## Open questions

- How do different data encoding schemes, variational ansatz choices, circuit depths, and numbers of qubits affect performance for financial time-series and generative modelling?
- What is the scalability of qGAN and QCBM to high-dimensional, multivariate financial distributions (e.g., many assets, fine-grained price levels) without aggressive discretization?
- Which optimizers and training strategies (SPSA, COBYLA, Adam, gradient-based vs gradient-free) are most robust and efficient for finance-focused quantum models, especially under realistic noise?
- Can quantum-native solvers (quantum annealers, QAOA, or other quantum optimizers) improve the QUBO-based selection/optimization beyond classical solvers used in this work?
- How to best combine qGAN, QLSTM and QCBM into integrated pipelines for forecasting, risk modeling and synthetic data generation in finance, and what synergies or conflicts arise?
- What are the concrete metrics and benchmarks (beyond RMSE and loss curves) that should be used to demonstrate practical quantum advantage in finance (e.g., risk-adjusted returns, downstream trading performance, latency)?
- How sensitive are results to hyperparameter choices and stochasticity (different random seeds, initializations), and are the reported improvements statistically and economically significant?
- What is the impact of financial-specific constraints (transaction costs, market impact, regulatory limits) on the real-world usefulness of models trained using qGAN/QCBM/QLSTM?
- How will advances in quantum simulation hardware (e.g., NVIDIA DGX Quantum with CUDA-Q) versus actual quantum processors influence the trade-off between simulation-only studies and experiments on hardware?
