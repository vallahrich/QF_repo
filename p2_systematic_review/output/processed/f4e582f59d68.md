## Abstract summary
This preprint benchmarks five quantum machine learning models (gate-based and annealing-based) against three classical approaches (Last Value, ARIMA, LSTM) for one-step univariate time-series forecasting on two real datasets (Apple stock and pasta sales). Using k-fold cross-validation, early stopping, and extensive hyperparameter grid search with noiseless quantum simulations and simulated annealing, the authors find that the best classical models outperform the best quantum models overall, though several quantum approaches achieve comparable performance and outperform ARIMA on one dataset; results are highly dataset-dependent.
## Methodology
The study implemented and benchmarked five quantum machine learning models (two QNN variants, a Quantum Deep Boltzmann Machine (QDBM), Quantum Reservoir Computing (QRC) and a quantum-enhanced LSTM (QLSTM)) against three classical baselines (Last Value, ARIMA via pmdarima AutoARIMA, and a classical LSTM). Two real-world univariate datasets were used (Apple daily adjusted close prices from Yahoo Finance and an aggregated daily pasta sales dataset from Mancuso et al.). All data were min-max normalized to [0,1]. Forecasting was performed as one-step-ahead prediction using the last n observations as input. The experimental pipeline preserved temporal order and used a rolling-window k-fold scheme: each fold used a 500-point window with the first 450 points for training and the last 50 for validation/test, shifting the window by 50 points between folds. Three folds were used for hyperparameter optimization (validation) and three additional folds for final testing. Hyperparameter optimization was performed by grid search (different numbers of configurations per model; e.g., 48 configurations for the QNN variants and 108 for QLSTM and QDBM), with each configuration repeated 10 times per fold (30 runs per configuration). Training used early stopping; the primary training loss was mean squared error (MSE) for QNNs, LSTM, QLSTM and QDBM, while QRC used a linear regression readout optimized with MSE. Final evaluation used mean absolute error (MAE). All gate-based quantum models were run on noiseless simulators and the QDBM used simulated annealing (QUBO) for its hidden-layer computation. The best hyperparameter configuration per model and dataset was selected by lowest average MAE over validation folds and repeats, and final test performance was reported as mean ± standard deviation over 10 repeated runs and 3 test folds.

**Algorithms used:** Quantum Neural Network (QNN) - Rx/Ry/Rz encoding + Ising layers, Quantum Neural Network (QNN) - Rx encoding + Rx + pairwise CNOT variational layers, Quantum Deep Boltzmann Machine (QDBM) - QUBO solved with simulated annealing, Quantum Reservoir Computing (QRC) with linear readout, Quantum Long Short-Term Memory (QLSTM) - VQC replacing LSTM gates, Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), ARIMA (Auto ARIMA via pmdarima), Last Value baseline, Linear regression (readout for QRC), Simulated annealing (for QDBM)
**Frameworks:** pmdarima

**Experimental setup:** Gate-based quantum models were executed using noiseless (ideal) simulators; the QDBM was executed using simulated annealing on a generated QUBO. Time-series forecasting used a rolling-window k-fold scheme: 500-point windows with 450 points for training and 50 for validation/test, shifting start by 50 for subsequent folds. Three folds were used for hyperparameter selection and three for final testing. Hyperparameter grid search was applied for each model (different grid sizes per model); each configuration was repeated 10 times per fold (30 runs per configuration). Early stopping was used during training. Training loss: MSE (except QRC readout optimized via linear regression); evaluation metric: MAE. Best hyperparameters chosen by lowest average MAE over validation folds and repeats.

**Dataset:** Apple historical daily adjusted closing prices (AAPL) from Yahoo Finance (financial dataset); additionally a non-financial Pasta sales dataset (118 daily series aggregated into a univariate daily sales series) from Mancuso et al. Both datasets were min-max normalized to [0,1].

## Findings
- [supported] In their benchmarks on two real-world univariate time series (Apple stock adjusted close and aggregated daily pasta sales), the best classical models achieved higher average accuracy than the best quantum models.
- [supported] For the Apple stock data set the naive 'Last Value' baseline produced the lowest average error, followed by ARIMA; all tested machine-learning models (classical and quantum) performed worse than this naive baseline.
- [supported] For the pasta sales data set the classical LSTM was the best performing model and the 'Last Value' baseline was the worst.
- [supported] The relative ranking of quantum vs classical models is highly data-set dependent: a quantum model that outperforms classical methods on one data set does not imply a general advantage across data sets.
- [supported] Most quantum models achieved results that were comparable to classical methods (i.e., not dramatically worse), and for one data set two quantum models outperformed the classical ARIMA model.
- [supported] Quantum Reservoir Computing (QRC) was the best-performing quantum model on the Apple stock data and had similar average test error to the classical LSTM on that data set.
- [supported] The Quantum Deep Boltzmann Machine (QDBM, annealing-based with simulated annealing) showed the largest variation in performance across hyperparameter configurations and produced a small fraction (< 1%) of divergent predictions in their experiments.
- [supported] QNN variants with different entangling gates (QNN vs QNN Ising) displayed similar performance, indicating that having more gates (Ising variant) did not necessarily improve forecasting performance in these experiments.
- [supported] Variation across hyperparameter configurations was larger for the Apple stock data than for the pasta sales data.
- [supported] Hyperparameter optimization had a smaller effect on performance than the choice of model family for the problems studied.

**Results summary:** The authors implemented and extensively hyperparameter-optimized five quantum forecasting models (two QNN variants, a quantum deep Boltzmann machine using simulated annealing, a quantum reservoir computer, and a quantum-enhanced LSTM) and compared them to three classical baselines (Last Value, ARIMA, LSTM) on two real univariate time series. Across both data sets the best classical models outperformed the best quantum models; however, many quantum models produced comparable results and, in one data set, two quantum models beat ARIMA. Performance rankings were highly data-dependent: the naive Last Value baseline was best for Apple stock while LSTM was best for pasta sales. Hyperparameter choice affected results, especially on the stock data, but model family (class choice) had a larger impact than hyperparameter tuning. The annealing-based QDBM showed large variability and a small rate of divergent outputs. Overall, no general quantum advantage was demonstrated in these experiments.

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** not-applicable

The study did not demonstrate a robust or general quantum advantage: the best classical methods outperformed the best quantum models on the two tested real-world univariate forecasting tasks. Some quantum models were competitive and occasionally outperformed a classical method (ARIMA) on one data set, but no general or reproducible advantage was shown.
## Limitations
- Only single-step, univariate forecasting problems were considered (simplest prediction task).
- All gate-based quantum models were noiselessly simulated and the annealing model used simulated annealing — no experiments on real quantum hardware were performed.
- The study focused only on forecast accuracy and did not evaluate trainability, parameter counts, or other resource/optimization properties of models.
- Hyperparameter optimization budgets and the number of hyperparameter configurations differed across models (due to differing numbers of relevant hyperparameters and simulation run times), which may affect fairness of comparisons.
- Only two real-world data sets were used (Apple stock and aggregated pasta sales), limiting the scope of empirical evaluation.
- Some models (notably the QDBM) exhibited instability/divergent predictions in a small fraction of runs.
- The set of classical baselines was limited (Last Value, ARIMA, LSTM), so comparisons against a broader set of state-of-the-art classical forecasting methods were not made. [inferred]
- [inferred] Noiseless simulations (and simulated annealing) may not reflect performance under realistic noise and hardware constraints, potentially overestimating quantum model performance.
- [inferred] Unequal hyperparameter search effort across models and limited computational budget could bias results and hide better configurations for some models.
- [inferred] Using only two datasets (and aggregating sales series into a single univariate series for pasta) may limit generalizability of conclusions across financial or other time-series domains.
## Open questions

- How do the quantum models perform on multidimensional (multivariate) inputs and multi-step-ahead forecasting tasks?
- How do quantum models behave on real quantum hardware, particularly in the presence of noise and device imperfections?
- What are the trainability characteristics, parameter-efficiency, and generalization properties of the evaluated quantum models compared to classical counterparts?
- How should effort be balanced between exploring different model architectures versus extensive hyperparameter optimization for each model?
- Can the stability issues observed in models like the QDBM be mitigated, and what causes the divergent predictions?
- [inferred] How do quantum forecasting models scale with larger datasets, longer horizons, and different application domains (e.g., other financial instruments)?
- [inferred] How do quantum resource constraints (qubit counts, gate depth, entanglement, annealer size) and noise impact practical forecasting performance and scalability?
- [inferred] Can quantum models outperform stronger or more recent classical baselines (e.g., Transformers, gradient-boosted trees, ensemble methods) on forecasting tasks?

**Future work:**

## Key ideas
- #idea:near-term-feasibility — Multiple quantum ML models (QNN variants, QDBM, QRC, QLSTM) were benchmarked on real univariate time series (Apple stock and aggregated pasta sales) and some quantum approaches achieve comparable performance to classical baselines in noiseless simulation, indicating dataset-dependent potential for near-term methods.
- #idea:near-term-feasibility — Overall the best classical models (notably LSTM and simple baselines) outperform the best quantum models across datasets, suggesting quantum approaches do not yet deliver repeatable advantage for one-step univariate forecasting on these tasks.
- #idea:hybrid-approach — Experiments used hybrid workflows (simulated annealing for QDBM, linear regression readout for QRC, classical hyperparameter search and classical optimizers), showing practical quantum-classical integration in current pipelines.
- #limitation:simulation-only — All gate-based quantum models were executed on noiseless simulators and the QDBM used classical simulated annealing (QUBO solved classically), so no experiments on real quantum hardware were reported.
- #limitation:no-empirical-validation — No real-hardware runs or noisy-hardware validation were performed; conclusions are thus limited to idealized simulations.
- #limitation:noise — Absence of noisy/hardware experiments means impact of device noise and error mitigation on model performance is unassessed.
- #limitation:data-encoding — The study used specific encodings (Rx/Ry/Rz, Rx) but does not address costs or scaling of encoding high-dimensional/time-series data into quantum states.
## Contradictions
- contradiction:classical-vs-quantum — The paper's empirical results show classical models (e.g., LSTM and simple baselines) outperform the tested quantum models overall, contradicting claims of broad quantum superiority for time-series forecasting in finance.
- contradiction:scalability — Results are obtained on noiseless simulators and small rolling windows (500-point windows); the study indicates quantum approaches that perform comparably at this scale may not scale or generalize to larger, noisy, real-world financial time-series, challenging optimistic scalability claims.
