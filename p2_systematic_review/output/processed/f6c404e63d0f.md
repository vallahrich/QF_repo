## Abstract summary
The paper formulates the subset sum problem arising in automated auditing and financial table consistency checks as a QUBO and applies gradient-descent on Hopfield networks to find solutions. The authors evaluate the approach on artificial and real financial data, demonstrate reliable performance, and report experiments on quantum annealing hardware, discussing current practical limits and potential for auditing applications.
## Methodology
The authors reformulate the subset sum problem as a QUBO by expressing the squared error (sum(subset) - T)^2 and mapping that QUBO to an Ising formulation. They solve the resulting binary optimization using Hopfield networks: derive Hopfield weights W and thresholds θ from the QUBO (W = -1/2 xx^T, θ = 1/2(xx^T1 - 2T x)), initialize batches of bipolar states s ∈ {−1,+1} and perform discrete gradient-descent-like updates (single-neuron updates using sign of local field) until the Hopfield energy converges. They run many random restarts (batched on GPU) to increase probability of finding a global optimum and check for zero energy indicating an exact subset sum. In addition to Hopfield/GPU experiments, they convert QUBOs and run them on a D-Wave Advantage quantum annealer to evaluate the annealer's capability to find solutions for small/medium problems. Experiments are conducted on both synthetic (controlled n, Xmax, k) and real financial tables parsed from corporate reports; performance is measured in time-to-solution, number of restarts, and success/found-solution counts.

**Algorithms used:** QUBO formulation (subset-sum -> quadratic objective), Hopfield network energy minimization (discrete gradient descent via single-neuron updates, multiple random restarts), Quantum annealing (D-Wave Advantage)

**Experimental setup:** GPU experiments: NVIDIA A100 used to run batched Hopfield-network runs; batched initialization with batch size chosen according to GPU memory (reported batch size used: 10,000). Up to ~1e8 initializations in total were attempted across batches, with a maximum wall clock run for some experiments of ~8 minutes. Quantum experiments: D-Wave Advantage quantum annealer (5436 physical qubits) used to solve each QUBO instance after mapping. No other simulator frameworks are reported.

**Dataset:** Two types of datasets: (1) Synthetic/artificial subset sum problems: n ∈ {16,32,64,128,256}, select k (e.g. k=4 for n=16, k=8 for others), values sampled uniformly from integer ranges [−Xmax, Xmax] with Xmax ∈ {1e4, 1e5, 1e6}. For each configuration M=5 problem instances were sampled. (2) Real financial data: parsed columns from corporate financial reports (examples include Deutsche Bank Q4 2020 and adidas annual/half-year reports). The parsed real dataset comprises 190 individual subset-sum problems across multiple tables; table-specific examples: assets (n=17, M=15, values 1.7e7..1.5e14), consincome (n=31, M=49, values −2.5e12..2.5e12), liabilities (n=20, M=29), net revenues (n=25, M=97). Values are integer monetary amounts (cents precision).

## Findings
- [supported] The subset sum problem can be expressed as a QUBO (quadratic unconstrained binary optimization) and converted to an Ising form for optimization with Hopfield networks and quantum annealers.
- [supported] Gradient descent on Hopfield networks (with many random initializations / batch GPU execution) reliably finds subset-sum solutions on the evaluated artificial and real financial datasets.
- [supported] On the parsed real financial tables used in the study, the Hopfield algorithm found a correct solution for each tested sum (all presented M problems per table were solved).
- [supported] On the artificial benchmark, the Hopfield solver succeeded for most parameter combinations; only the hardest configuration (n=256, Xmax=1e6) failed for some samples (2 of 5 solved).
- [supported] Empirically observed that the magnitude of the numbers (Xmax) has a larger impact on runtime/effort to find solutions than the number of items n in the tested ranges.
- [supported] The authors successfully mapped and ran small subset-sum QUBOs on a D-Wave Advantage quantum annealer (5436 physical qubits) for small instances (example: 17-variable table yielding 6 subset-sum problems); the annealer returned correct solutions for those cases.
- [supported] Current quantum annealers face embedding/graph constraints so that many physical qubits are required per logical variable; with current D-Wave hardware, practical logical-variable problem sizes are on the order of O(sqrt(M)) and quoted practical tops are ~50–70 logical variables.
- [speculative] Digital annealers / FPGAs and more advanced quantum hardware could make QUBO-based subset-sum solving practical at larger scales for auditing applications.
- [speculative] As quantum annealing hardware improves and becomes more affordable, it will become practically useful for real-world financial auditing tasks.
- [speculative] The presented Hopfield/QUBO approach can be integrated into smart auditing software to assist auditors in daily work.

**Results summary:** The paper formulates subset-sum instances arising in auditing as QUBOs and solves them using gradient-descent-style optimization on Hopfield networks with GPU-accelerated batches of random restarts. Experiments on both synthetic data and parsed real financial tables demonstrate that the Hopfield solver reliably finds correct subset-sum solutions in the tested cases (with higher numeric magnitude increasing difficulty). The authors also mapped small problems to a D-Wave Advantage quantum annealer and obtained correct solutions for the small instances they tested. They argue that FPGA/digital annealers and future quantum hardware could extend applicability, but currently quantum annealers are limited by embedding costs, scale and operational practicality.

**Classification:** speculative

The authors argue future quantum/hardware improvements could make quantum annealing practical for larger auditing problems; this is a forward-looking, speculative claim rather than a demonstrated quantum advantage.
## Limitations
- Hopfield networks can converge to local minima and do not guarantee finding the global optimum without multiple random restarts (author-stated).
- The Hopfield algorithm's runtime and success probability degrade as the magnitude (precision) of input numbers (Xmax) increases; larger numeric magnitudes increase compute time (author-stated / empirically observed).
- Dynamic programming / exact pseudo-polynomial algorithms have impractical space complexity O(nT) for realistic financial data magnitudes, making them unsuitable for the target application (author-stated).
- Current quantum annealers are constrained by qubit connectivity and embedding overhead: only O(sqrt(M)) logical variables can be represented with M physical qubits, limiting solvable QUBO sizes (author-stated).
- Available large quantum annealers (e.g., D-Wave Advantage) currently only support QUBOs of the order of tens of variables (authors give ~50–70 variable estimate), which is far smaller than many real financial tables (author-stated).
- Quantum annealers are costly to operate and not yet practical for routine deployment in this auditing application (author-stated).
- Datasets with many small-magnitude numbers and larger n can produce many mathematically valid subsets for a given target (combinatorial ambiguity); many returned solutions do not correspond to semantically meaningful/real-world relationships (author-stated / observed).
- [inferred] Treating tables as unordered sets (ignoring table structure and domain semantics) can increase false positives / return subsets that are mathematically valid but not meaningful in accounting context.
- [inferred] Generalization beyond the evaluated datasets is uncertain: experiments are limited to selected artificial instances and parsed financial tables, so behaviour on diverse real-world reports is not fully validated.
- [inferred] GPU memory and batching impose practical constraints on the number of parallel Hopfield initializations and thus on wall-clock performance (the algorithm refers to batch size depending on GPU memory).
- [inferred] The method requires potentially many random restarts to achieve reliability; worst-case number of restarts may be large for hard instances, impacting runtime predictability.
