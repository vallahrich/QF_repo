## Abstract summary
This systematic review compares classical machine learning (ML) and quantum machine learning (QML) in the financial sector, surveying applications such as fraud detection, anti-money laundering, algorithmic trading, portfolio optimization, credit scoring, and derivative pricing. It synthesizes recent algorithmic advances (e.g., QSVMs, QNNs, QAOA), highlights practical constraints (data encoding, NISQ hardware limits, interpretability, cybersecurity and regulatory issues), and proposes hybrid quantum–classical frameworks and an assessment roadmap to bridge theory and real-world deployment.
## Methodology
This work is a systematic, comparative, and integrative literature review of classical machine learning (CML) and quantum machine learning (QML) applied to the financial sector. The authors synthesize peer-reviewed articles, preprints, industry white papers and tooling documentation to (1) map application domains (fraud detection, AML, algorithmic trading, portfolio optimization, credit scoring, option pricing, risk assessment, etc.), (2) summarize relevant quantum and classical algorithms and frameworks, (3) compare strengths, limitations and implementation challenges, and (4) derive a strategic roadmap and an assessment framework for QML suitability in practical finance. The review compiles and organizes findings into comparative tables and a literature-review matrix (e.g., Tables 1–4) and discusses representative case studies and experimental results reported in the primary literature. No novel empirical experiments or primary data collection were performed by the authors; instead, the paper aggregates results from simulation studies, hardware experiments and hybrid implementations reported by other groups and vendors, highlighting gaps in benchmarking, scalability, hardware noise, data-encoding bottlenecks, and regulatory considerations. The manuscript also proposes future research directions (hybrid quantum–classical architectures, federated/privacy-preserving QML, domain-specific encoding techniques and explainable quantum AI) based on the synthesized evidence.

**Algorithms used:** QSVM, QNN, QAOA, QA (Quantum Annealing), VQA (Variational Quantum Algorithms), VQE (Variational Quantum Eigensolver), QAE (Quantum Amplitude Estimation), QPE (Quantum Phase Estimation), QFT (Quantum Fourier Transform), HHL (Harrow–Hassidim–Lloyd algorithm), QGAN, QCBM, QRL (Quantum Reinforcement Learning), QADQN (Quantum Attention Deep Q-Network), QGNN (Quantum Graph Neural Network), QBMs (Quantum Boltzmann Machines), Quantum clustering (e.g., qLUE / dynamic quantum clustering), Quantum encoders, Grover's algorithm, Shor's algorithm
**Frameworks:** Qiskit, PennyLane, TensorFlow Quantum, Qiskit Finance, D-Wave hybrid solvers (quantum annealer platforms)

## Limitations
- Quantum hardware fragility: decoherence, high gate error rates, and limited qubit connectivity on current NISQ devices.
- Scalability constraints of quantum devices: insufficient qubit counts and circuit depth limitations for large-scale financial problems.
- Lack of quantum error correction and fault-tolerant architectures for practical, long-depth quantum computations.
- Data-encoding bottlenecks: inefficient or impractical methods for embedding classical high-dimensional financial data into quantum states (e.g., amplitude encoding costs).
- Algorithmic maturity: many QML algorithms (QSVM, QNN, QAOA, QAE, etc.) are still experimental and lack extensive benchmarking against classical baselines on real financial datasets.
- Limited real-hardware experimentation and few production-grade case studies demonstrating consistent quantum advantage in finance.
- Noise sensitivity: quantum circuits and models remain vulnerable to noise, and noise mitigation is not yet universally effective.
- Interpretability/explainability: QML models are largely black boxes and lack mature explainable frameworks required by regulators and stakeholders.
- Regulatory readiness: absence of governance frameworks, standards and regulatory guidelines specific to QML in financial contexts.
- Integration challenges: difficulty in integrating quantum/hybrid systems with existing legacy financial infrastructures and low-latency trading environments.
- Latency constraints for HFT: current quantum approaches are not yet suitable for sub-millisecond decision loops required by high-frequency trading.
- Data quality and availability: financial ML (classical and quantum) requires high-quality, labeled, timely data which is often unavailable or noisy.
- Class imbalance and training-data volume limitations impact model performance, particularly for rare events like fraud or AML anomalies.
- Security and cryptographic risk: quantum computing threatens current cryptographic systems and requires quantum-safe protocols for financial security.
- Benchmarking and evaluation gaps: lack of standardized evaluation metrics, benchmarking procedures and reproducible baselines for QML in finance.
- Simulation costs: classical simulation of quantum algorithms for benchmarking scales exponentially and becomes infeasible for larger problem sizes.
- Operational costs and complexity: development and deployment of QML/hybrid solutions entail significant resource investment and operational complexity.
- Ethical and legal concerns: potential for model bias, auditability issues, and unclear liability in automated quantum-driven financial decisions.
- [inferred] Limited availability of skilled personnel and domain expertise capable of developing, validating and operating QML solutions in financial institutions.
- [inferred] Potential organizational resistance and procurement hurdles for adopting quantum infrastructure due to high initial costs and uncertain ROI.
