## Abstract summary
This preprint compares a quantum computing approach (CVaR-augmented Variational Quantum Eigensolver) to classical molecular dynamics (MD) simulations for folding 50 seven-residue peptides drawn from disorder-promoting amino acids. The authors report that CVaR-VQE on a local quantum simulator yields lower (more favorable) ground-state energies for the majority of peptides compared to 50 ns MD trajectories, and argue that quantum algorithms hold promise for protein-folding problems as hardware and methods mature.
## Methodology
The study compares molecular dynamics (MD) simulations and a hybrid quantum-classical Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE) workflow (with a Conditional Value at Risk, CVaR, objective) to find low-energy folds for 50 coarse-grained 7-residue peptides. Peptide sequences were sampled at random from permutations of seven disorder-promoting amino acids (D, H, Q, K, S, E, P). For the quantum workflow each peptide fold is encoded into a sparse turn-to-qubit mapping (12 classical bits described, with 7 variable qubits for turns and additional qubits for interactions; nine qubits were used for the full fold model including interaction/helper qubits). An explicit Hamiltonian including nearest-neighbour contact interactions and penalty terms for invalid geometries and chirality was constructed. A parameterized quantum circuit (ansatz) with two layers of RX/RY/RZ rotations and added conditional rotation gates plus CNOTs and Hadamards was used to prepare trial states. The CVaR-VQE objective computes an expectation over the lowest-energy tail (alpha=0.05; 0.025 used for noise-free cases) of measured bitstrings; optimization of variational parameters used surrogateopt (Global Optimization Toolbox) in this work (the original approach used a genetic optimizer). Quantum runs used a high shot count (500,000 shots) per evaluation and 100 optimizer iterations. For MD, each peptide was built as an extended structure in Maestro, prepared with Epik and OPLS3e force field (peptides were not minimized prior to production), solvated in an orthorhombic box with TIP3P water and no extra ions, and subjected to 50 ns Desmond MD runs. Trajectories were sampled every 10 ns (six frames per peptide), frames were minimized, and Lennard-Jones interaction energies and folding RMSDs were analyzed. Results were compared via energy metrics (kcal/mol), counts of peptides reaching negative ΔG, and statistical testing (two-tailed t-test, R^2 reported).

**Algorithms used:** Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE), CVaR-VQE (VQE with Conditional Value at Risk objective), surrogateopt (Global Optimization Toolbox), Genetic optimization (referenced/used in prior work), Molecular dynamics (Desmond)
**Frameworks:** Amazon Braket (local simulator), SV1 state vector simulator (Amazon Braket), Schrödinger Maestro (3D builder), Epik (Schrödinger), Desmond MD engine, OPLS3e force field

**Experimental setup:** Quantum: Amazon Braket local simulator and SV1 state-vector simulator; nine-qubit circuits (7 configuration qubits + 2 interaction/helper qubits); 500,000 shots per energy evaluation; 100 optimization iterations; CVaR alpha=0.05 (0.025 for noise-free). Classical MD: Desmond 50 ns production runs per peptide, TIP3P solvent, OPLS3e force field, orthorhombic box, frames extracted every 10 ns.

**Results summary:** The preprint reports a head-to-head comparison between MD-based folding (50 ns Desmond simulations) and a CVaR-enhanced VQE pipeline (simulated locally) on 50 coarse-grained seven-residue peptides derived from disorder-prone amino acids. In their experiments the CVaR-VQE pipeline produced substantially lower mean ground-state energies (mean -5.885 kcal/mol) and achieved energetically favorable (< 0 kcal/mol) folds for 49/50 peptides, compared with 36/50 favorable results from 50 ns MD. The quantum experiments used 100 optimization iterations, 500,000 shots per evaluation, a tetrahedral lattice encoding, and reported convergence behavior on example sequences. The authors argue these simulation results indicate promise for quantum approaches to protein folding, while acknowledging current hardware and scaling limitations.

## Quantum advantage claim
**Classification:** speculative

The authors present simulation-based evidence that their CVaR-VQE pipeline yields lower ground-state energies than 50 ns MD for the specific set of small, coarse-grained peptides tested. However, the experiments were performed on simulators/local resources (not on quantum hardware), on small lattice-encoded peptides, and without demonstrated scaling to larger/protein-relevant sizes or performance comparisons against advanced classical optimization or enhanced-sampling MD approaches. Therefore any claim of practical quantum advantage is speculative based on the reported results.
## Limitations
- The current VQE model has biases with respect to defining the qubit configuration and interaction qubits.
- Limited QPU qubit counts currently prevent running larger peptide/protein sequences on actual quantum hardware.
- Quantum algorithms, circuits and hardware are still in early stages; noise and hardware constraints were not fully addressed.
- This study only considered 1-nearest-neighbour (1-NN) interactions in the Hamiltonian for this work.
- Use of a coarse-grained tetrahedral lattice model (lattice/bead representation) simplifies the physics compared to atomistic models.
- VQE experiments were performed on simulators (Amazon Braket local simulator and SV1 state-vector simulator) rather than demonstrated on real noisy quantum hardware.
- MD simulations were limited to 50 ns per peptide; many peptides required longer/variable simulation times to reach minimum energy.
- Study limited to small peptides (7 amino acids); conclusions may not generalize to larger proteins.
- [inferred] The coarse-grained lattice representation likely omits important atomistic details (side-chain chemistry, detailed solvent effects, long-range electrostatics).
- [inferred] The dataset is small (50 randomly selected sequences) and restricted to disorder-promoting residues, which may limit generalizability and introduce selection bias.
- [inferred] MD setup choices (peptides not minimized prior to production runs, no added ions, specific force field/water model) may influence folding outcomes and comparability.
- [inferred] The CVaR cutoff (alpha) choice and other optimizer hyperparameters may materially affect VQE outcomes but sensitivity analysis is not reported.
- [inferred] The high number of shots (500,000) and 100 iterations increase computational cost and may be impractical on real QPUs or for larger problems.
- [inferred] The comparison does not report wall-clock runtime or resource/cost comparison between MD (classical) and VQE (quantum/simulator) workflows.
- [inferred] While an enhanced conditional-rotation ansatz was used, the study does not fully assess risks like barren plateaus or optimizer trapping for the chosen ansatz.
- [inferred] Mapping lattice bitstrings to realistic 3D structural validation beyond energy (e.g., detailed structural metrics, experimental validation) is limited.
## Open questions
- How will the CVaR-VQE approach scale to larger peptides and full proteins as system size increases?
- How robust are the reported VQE results to realistic quantum hardware noise and connectivity constraints?
- How sensitive are the VQE outcomes to choices of encoding, qubit mapping, CVaR alpha, and variational ansatz design?
- Can VQE-based lattice folding predictions be validated and reconciled with atomistic structural metrics and experimental data?
- How to incorporate medium- and long-range interactions (beyond 1-NN) and more realistic potentials into the quantum Hamiltonian efficiently?
- What is the comparative wall-clock time, energy consumption and computational cost trade-off between VQE (on real QPUs) and classical MD for equivalent accuracy?
- Which classical optimizers (genetic algorithms, surrogateopt, COBYLA, etc.) are most effective/stable in CVaR-VQE for this problem class?
- How well do adaptive or problem-tailored ansätze perform versus the enhanced conditional-rotation ansatz used here, especially regarding barren plateaus?
- Can quantum algorithms (VQE, QAOA, digitized counterdiabatic approaches) be combined with enhanced-sampling MD methods to improve folding predictions for disordered regions?
- How generalizable are the findings beyond the selected disorder-promoting residue set and the specific lattice model used?
- What is the minimal quantum hardware capability (qubit count, fidelity) required to meaningfully outperform classical methods for peptide folding?
- How to systematically choose the CVaR alpha parameter and other hyperparameters to balance exploration/exploitation in the probability tail used for objective calculation?

**Future work:**
- Integrate QAOA with VQE to improve folding ground state predictions (explicitly mentioned).
- Investigate adaptive and problem-tailored ansätze (k-UpCCGSD, adaptive ansätze) and study their computational cost and accuracy trade-offs.
- Explore alternative classical optimizers for CVaR-VQE (e.g., COBYLA) and compare optimizer performance.
- Scale the approach to larger peptides/proteins as quantum hardware (qubit counts and fidelities) and algorithms improve.
- Further studies to improve noise resilience and test CVaR-VQE on actual quantum hardware rather than simulators.
- Extend the Hamiltonian/encoding to include more interaction types and longer-range contacts (beyond 1-NN) and side-chain representations.
- Combine quantum approaches with classical MD/enhanced sampling methods to address disordered regions and sampling challenges.
